# The Operator Economy

## Evidence base and 18-month signal system

**What the evidence supports now, what remains a forecast, and what would falsify the thesis**

> **Status:** This report codifies a public thesis and its evidence standard. It is not a claim that the phrase was invented here, not legal clearance for a trademark, and not proof that a new economy already exists. Evidence cutoff: 24 August 2026.

**The enabling stack exists. The economic class has not yet been proven.**

<!-- pagebreak -->

# Executive verdict

The Operator Economy is credible as a **falsifiable forecast about a changing firm boundary**. It is not yet credible as an established economy.

Cloud, software, payment rails, logistics, marketplaces, fractional specialists, contractors, and generative AI make more business capabilities purchasable in smaller increments. Controlled studies show that AI can compress time and raise quality on bounded tasks. Field evidence shows that organizational integration can sometimes translate those gains into customer and revenue outcomes. Those are real enabling conditions.

They do not prove the full thesis.

The missing evidence is not cosmetic. No public dataset currently identifies firms that fit the proposed behavior-based definition and then measures their total labor, normalized surplus, customer ownership, provider dependence, owner workload, survival, and transferability over time. Federal data can count payroll firms, tax entities, applications, receipts, employment, and some purchased services. It cannot observe who controls the customer promise, who bears recourse, whether external providers are substitutable, or whether the operator retains the residual economics.

The disciplined conclusion is therefore:

> **Generative AI compresses codified task capability faster than it compresses organizations. In compatible work, it may reduce the permanent core required to operate an accountable firm. The thesis becomes economically meaningful only when that core produces superior quality-adjusted output and durable operator surplus per unit of total labor while retaining the customer relationship, decision rights, portability, and recourse.**

That statement is assertive. It also carries its own test.

## The hard answer to “isn’t the thesis what will be enabled?”

Yes—but only as a forecast. The distinction matters:

- **Enabled** means the cost, granularity, and accessibility of capability have changed enough to make a new firm architecture plausible.
- **Observed** means identifiable firms actually use that architecture.
- **Economically validated** means the architecture produces durable advantages after all labor, vendor, failure, financing, and owner costs are counted.
- **An economy** means the configuration is measurable, increasingly prevalent, institutionally consequential, and independently replicable.

The evidence is strongest at the first level, partial at the second, weak at the third, and absent at the fourth.

Calling that gap “early” does not close it. It defines the research program.

<!-- diagram:two-bet -->

## The two independent bets

The thesis contains two bets that must not be blended:

1. **Boundary compression:** a firm can produce a comparable customer outcome with a smaller permanent core after contractors, vendor-mediated labor, owner hours, rework, and supervision are counted.
2. **Operator capture:** the owner-controlled core retains demand, decision rights, cash access, portability, margin, and customer recourse rather than becoming a thin tenant of platforms and infrastructure providers.

Bet 1 can succeed while Bet 2 fails. In that case, the result is not a distinctive Operator Economy. It is a more automated platform economy in which operators carry accountability while suppliers capture control and surplus.

That is the central strategic risk the original framing underestimates.

# 1. The codified thesis

## Core thesis

> **The Operator Economy is an emerging firm architecture in which an owner-controlled, deliberately small permanent core remains accountable for a customer outcome while scaling execution through AI, software, platforms, contractors, and specialist partners. Its economic significance is a testable forecast: these firms must produce more quality-adjusted output and durable operator surplus per unit of total labor while retaining the customer relationship, decision rights, portability, and recourse.**

This is the long-form research statement. It separates architecture from outcome and present evidence from future validation.

## Public hook

> **AI is not eliminating the firm; it may be reducing the permanent core required to operate one. The test is whether the operator keeps the customer, control, margin, and accountability.**

## Six doctrine statements

1. **Capability is becoming more divisible.** More expertise, infrastructure, and execution can be bought by task, usage, transaction, project, or fractional allocation.
2. **Payroll is not productive capacity.** A small payroll can sit on a large hidden system of contractors, platforms, models, infrastructure, and unpaid owner labor.
3. **AI compresses tasks faster than organizations.** Production gains do not automatically remove routing, verification, coordination, governance, or liability.
4. **The bottleneck moves.** As production becomes cheaper, scarce advantages shift toward demand, judgment, trust, integration, customer context, and recourse.
5. **Risk and labor relocate; they do not disappear.** External capacity can move fixed commitments, but it also moves bargaining power, failure modes, labor protections, and switching costs.
6. **Control and surplus are the discriminating tests.** A firm is not economically independent because it has few employees. It is independent to the extent it owns demand, can substitute critical providers, retains decision rights, and earns durable surplus after full-cost accounting.

## What this thesis does not claim

- It does not claim that one person can reliably replace a full company.
- It does not claim that AI has already reduced permanent team size across the economy.
- It does not treat every freelancer, creator, nonemployer, agency, or software-using small business as an operator firm.
- It does not equate a delayed first hire with productivity; weak demand and scarce capital can produce the same observation.
- It does not call gross revenue, gross margin, revenue per employee, follower count, or marketplace GMV operator surplus.
- It does not treat a model benchmark as a firm-level economic result.
- It does not claim the name “Operator Economy” was coined here. Public uses predate this report.

## The doctrine’s uncomfortable constraint

If the definition is broad enough to include every small business using software and contractors, it is tautological and unoriginal. If it is narrow enough to be distinctive, it may be too rare to deserve the word “economy.”

The way out is not rhetoric. It is a classifier, a denominator, longitudinal evidence, and precommitted promotion rules.

# 2. Fixed vocabulary

## Operator firm

An **operator firm** is a firm in which a small, owner-controlled permanent core:

- owns the customer promise and commercial recourse;
- controls the offer, price or payer terms, cash allocation, and acceptance standard;
- assembles a material share of execution from a capability perimeter;
- remains accountable for the delivered outcome;
- can show where labor, liability, dependency, and value sit across the full system.

Technology is not required for classification. AI intensifies the mechanism; it does not define the category.

## Permanent core

Working owners plus people whose relationship is functionally permanent, regardless of payroll label. For the first research cohort, count a person inside the core when they perform at least 0.5 full-time-equivalent firm-specific work for six continuous months and cannot be substituted through the firm’s normal supplier process.

This rule prevents contractor relabeling from creating a fake small core.

## Capability perimeter

The software, AI systems, contractors, fractional specialists, platforms, logistics, payments, cloud, capital, licensed partners, and other external systems that carry economically material or operationally critical capacity.

The perimeter is part of the productive system even when it is outside payroll or the legal entity.

## Boundary compression

A reduction in the permanent core required to deliver a comparable customer outcome **after total labor and quality are held visible**. It is not demonstrated by fewer employees alone.

## Operator capture

The share of customer access, decision rights, cash authority, data portability, bargaining power, and residual economics retained by the operator core.

## Normalized operator surplus

Revenue less every economically necessary cost, including:

- employee compensation and benefits;
- an owner replacement wage for actual operating hours;
- contractors and vendor-mediated human labor;
- AI, software, API, cloud, platform, payment, and marketplace fees;
- implementation, training, orchestration, review, correction, and support;
- security, compliance, insurance, financing, refunds, claims, and reserves;
- expected failure, outage, migration, and concentration costs;
- maintenance capital and a reasonable resilience reserve.

This is an analytical measure, not an accounting standard. Its purpose is to stop outsourced work and owner sacrifice from masquerading as productivity.

## Dependency-adjusted independence

Observed operator control discounted for critical dependencies that cannot be replaced within an agreed recovery window. A firm with direct customer contracts but a single irreplaceable payment processor, marketplace, model, cloud environment, or human specialist is not fully independent.

## Accountable outcome

A result for which a named legal entity and human decision-maker provide customer recourse. AI can execute. Vendors can carry defined duties. Neither should become an excuse for accountability theater.

## Working classifier for the first dataset

| Dimension | Preregistered starting rule | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | One firm × one materially distinct offer or business line × one calendar quarter | Prevents a multi-line entity from being classified once despite different boundaries |
| Small permanent core | No more than ten full-time-equivalent people under the functional permanence rule | A testable research cutoff, not a natural law |
| Owner control | Working owners hold more than 50% of residual voting or equivalent control and retain final authority over offer, payer terms, cash allocation, acceptance, and customer recourse | Excludes dependent work dressed as entrepreneurship |
| Material perimeter | At least one external capability accounts for 20% or more of recurring economically necessary delivery cost, or its loss interrupts more than 20% of delivery or revenue beyond 30 days without a tested substitute | Excludes ordinary incidental software and procurement |
| Direct accountability | The operator’s entity is responsible to the buyer for the core promise and cannot disclaim that promise to a platform, model, or contractor | Keeps recourse at the center |
| Evidence sufficiency | Labor, owner hours, provider costs, customer origin, concentration, incidents, and outcome quality are available for the observation period | Prevents classification without economic visibility |

The 10-person, 20%, six-month, and 30-day thresholds are governance choices to preregister and test. They are not discovered scientific constants.

## Adjacent forms and exclusions

| Form | Include when | Exclude when |
|---|---|---|
| Freelancer | Owns a repeatable offer, P&L, customer relationship, acceptance system, and material capability perimeter | Sells individual labor inside a client’s system |
| Gig or marketplace worker | Retains meaningful offer, pricing, customer portability, and recourse | Platform controls access, price, relationship, and suspension risk |
| Creator | Audience and customer data are usable assets inside an operator-controlled firm | Revenue and reach depend almost entirely on one revocable platform |
| Agency | Core owns promise and orchestrates a substitutable perimeter | “Small team” is merely a large bench of functionally permanent contractors |
| Traditional small business | Boundary, control, and evidence rules are met | Smallness alone is the classification |
| Venture startup | Working owners retain the control rule and the boundary fits | Board or capital structure removes residual operating control |
| Agentic system | Sits inside an accountable human-led firm boundary | Autonomous software is treated as the bearer of legal and customer responsibility |

# 3. The claim ladder

<!-- diagram:claim-ladder -->

Every public statement must be labeled internally as **fact, inference, forecast, or unknown**. The label does not need to be spoken every time. The epistemic status must never be changed for rhetorical convenience.

## Facts supported now

1. **Relevant AI capability became dramatically cheaper.** Stanford’s 2025 AI Index reports that the price of querying a model at roughly GPT-3.5-level MMLU performance fell from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 by October 2024, a more than 280-fold decline. This is a benchmark-normalized inference-price result, not an all-input workflow cost. [E1]
2. **Bounded task gains are real and heterogeneous.** A preregistered writing experiment with 453 professionals reported roughly 40% less time and 18% higher quality. A deployed support tool across 5,172 agents raised issues resolved per hour about 15% overall, with larger gains among less-skilled workers. [E2][E3]
3. **The capability frontier is jagged.** In an experiment with 758 BCG consultants, AI improved speed, completion, and quality on tasks inside the measured frontier, but made users 19% less likely to solve an outside-frontier problem correctly. [E4]
4. **Judgment remains a scarce complement.** A five-month field experiment with 640 Kenyan entrepreneurs found no detectable average revenue or profit gain; stronger baseline performers appeared to gain while weaker performers performed worse, driven by which advice they implemented. [E5]
5. **AI adoption is rising but shallow and size-skewed.** Census evidence from late 2025 and early 2026 found roughly 18% of employer firms using AI; 57% of adopters used it in no more than three functions, 65% in no more than three worker tasks, 66% reported augmentation only, and about 2% reported AI-related employment decreases. [E6]
6. **Micro employer adoption is material, not dominant.** In the 27 July–9 August 2026 BTOS collection, reported use ranged from 21.9% among 1–4 employee firms to 41.5% among firms with at least 250 employees. BTOS excludes nonemployers and changed its AI wording in November 2025. [E7]
7. **The United States has a very large nonemployer population.** The 2023 Nonemployer Statistics counted 30,427,808 establishments and $1.753 trillion in gross receipts. About 86.75% were below $100,000 in receipts. These are tax establishments with no paid employees, not a count of full-time operators, and receipts are not profit or productivity. [E8]
8. **Very small employer firms are common and fragile.** About 63% of 2022 employer firms had fewer than five employees. Employer-establishment survival was 51.4% at five years for the 2020 birth cohort and 34.7% at ten years for the 2013 cohort. [E9][E10]
9. **Current labor evidence shows task reorganization more clearly than broad displacement.** Linked Danish data found no detectable average earnings or hours effect over two years and ruled out effects larger than roughly 2%, while documenting new oversight and integration work. U.S. payroll data show no broad displacement but a descriptive early-career hiring weakness in highly exposed occupations. [E11][E12]
10. **Long, messy autonomy remains unreliable.** Production-agent studies report frequent intervention, narrow step counts, and high reliance on human evaluation. Economically grounded benchmarks remain far below dependable autonomous completion on multi-hour work. [E13][E14]

## Defensible inferences

- More business capability can be purchased in smaller increments than before generative AI, continuing a longer cloud, software, outsourcing, and platform trend.
- In compatible sectors, this lowers experimentation cost and may reduce the minimum viable permanent core.
- Tool access alone is not enough. Workflow design, task selection, context, integration, verification, and operator judgment govern whether task gains become firm gains.
- The first-order effect is more likely to be output expansion and task reorganization than immediate economy-wide headcount removal.
- As production becomes cheaper, demand, trust, customer context, judgment, compliance, and accountability become relatively more important.
- Apparent independence can decline even as capability rises when provider concentration, platform rules, switching costs, and customer acquisition dependence increase.

## Forecasts to test

- New firms in AI-compatible sectors will reach comparable accepted output and revenue bands with smaller permanent cores than matched pre-2023 or low-integration cohorts.
- The firms with the strongest outcomes will combine broad workflow integration with direct customer access and provider substitutability, not merely high AI spend.
- Total labor will fall less than payroll headcount because some work moves to contractors, providers, supervision, exception handling, and owner time.
- Small-core firms that do not own demand or portability will show weaker normalized surplus and higher failure sensitivity than matched firms with direct customer relationships.
- Customer trust, insurance, security, procurement, and legal recourse will become a binding ceiling in regulated or high-consequence work.
- The first durable labor-market effects will appear in hiring, freelance demand, and junior task bundles before broad layoffs.

## Material unknowns

- How common behaviorally defined operator firms are in any sector.
- Whether their prevalence is rising after controlling for pre-AI small-business trends.
- Whether they use less **total** labor at comparable quality and output.
- Whether they retain more surplus or merely transfer margin to providers.
- Whether direct customer relationships are truly portable.
- Whether they survive, recover, and transfer better than matched small firms.
- Whether owner hours, health, interruption risk, and key-person exposure improve or worsen.
- Who bears income volatility, benefits loss, and error risk across the perimeter.

## The only honest status line

> **What exists today is an enabling stack, a large adjacent small-business population, real task-level productivity gains, and early evidence of organizational reconfiguration. What does not yet exist is a representative, longitudinal proof of a distinct operator economic class.**

# 4. Evidence by mechanism

## Mechanism A — capability becomes cheaper and more granular

The enabling case is strong. Cloud infrastructure, SaaS, payment and logistics rails, specialist marketplaces, and fractional labor already reduced the need to internalize every capability. Generative AI adds inexpensive cognitive output to that perimeter.

The 2025 Stanford AI Index’s 280-fold price decline at a fixed benchmark threshold is the cleanest compressed-capability signal. [E1] But token price is only the beginning of the cost stack. It excludes requirement discovery, data preparation, system access, retries, routing, review, correction, integration, governance, maintenance, migration, and failure.

The correct business metric is therefore:

> **Cost per accepted workflow = all model, tool, build, labor, review, correction, governance, failure, and maintenance cost divided by outputs accepted at the required quality and service level.**

Anything narrower is a demo metric.

## Mechanism B — task capability compresses

The evidence is substantial but conditional:

| Setting | Result | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short professional writing, 453 participants | About 40% faster and 18% higher quality | Bounded writing compression | Job or firm productivity |
| Customer support, 5,172 agents | About 15% more issues per hour; larger gains for lower-skill workers | Pattern transfer in structured workflows | Autonomous service or headcount reduction |
| BCG consultants, 758 participants | Faster, more complete, higher quality inside frontier; 19% less likely correct outside it | Jagged, task-specific augmentation | Safe general autonomy |
| P&G ideation, 791 professionals | Individual with AI matched two-person no-AI team on a one-day ideation task | Broader bounded functional coverage | Durable replacement of a team |
| Experienced open-source coding, 16 maintainers and 246 tasks | AI access made completion 19% slower despite perceived speedup | Context can reverse gains; perception is unreliable | That coding AI is generally harmful |
| Kenyan entrepreneurs, 640 firms | No average revenue/profit effect; gains and harm split by baseline capability | Judgment determines economic effect | Uniform democratization of expertise |

Sources: [E2][E3][E4][E5][E15][E16]

The synthesis is simple: AI can compress codified task capability. It does not reliably compress the full system of choosing the task, supplying context, judging output, coordinating dependencies, accepting consequences, and learning over time.

## Mechanism C — task gains become firm outcomes

This is where the evidence thins.

The strongest current firm-level causal result is the 2026 working paper “Mapping AI into Production.” A preregistered three-month accelerator experiment gave 515 self-selected, early-stage, AI-focused startups tools, credits, and training; the treatment group also received case studies on AI-native organizational redesign. The treatment increased AI use cases by 44%, tasks by 12%, and the probability of having a paying customer by 11 percentage points. The paper reports 1.9 times revenue and lower external-capital demand, with unchanged labor demand. [E17]

That is meaningful evidence that organizational mapping can help task gains aggregate into firm outcomes. It is not proof of the Operator Economy:

- the cohort was selected from an AI-focused accelerator;
- the window was only three months;
- revenue gains were concentrated in the upper tail;
- several headline estimates were marginal rather than conventionally precise;
- labor demand did not fall;
- survival, normalized surplus, customer control, and durability were not observed.

The correct public statement is: **early causal evidence suggests organizational integration can create firm-level value without increasing measured labor demand in a selected startup cohort.** Anything stronger outruns the study.

## Mechanism D — the permanent core contracts

Direct causal evidence is absent.

Some provider datasets show small startup teams. Carta reports a four-person median seed team in a selected venture-backed population and shrinking later-stage teams relative to earlier peaks. Gusto reports smaller headcounts in recent information and professional-service cohorts. These observations are confounded by the capital cycle, weak demand, cohort composition, remote work, and the pre-existing cloud/SaaS trend. They do not count contractors, providers, or owner hours. [E18][E19]

The closest bounded experiment is P&G ideation, where an individual using AI matched the measured quality of a two-person team without AI. Human teams retained value in evaluative selection. One-day task substitution is not organizational redesign. [E15]

The thesis must therefore say **may reduce**, not **has reduced**, the permanent core.

## Mechanism E — coordination and verification move rather than vanish

A six-month randomized rollout across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers found active Microsoft 365 Copilot users spent roughly two fewer hours per week on email in the later period. Researchers did not detect a corresponding reduction in meetings or broad work composition. [E20]

Production-agent evidence points in the same direction. IBM’s 2026 production study found 68% of reported agents executed no more than ten steps before human intervention and 74% relied primarily on human evaluation. Reliability was the leading challenge. [E13]

An Alibaba service-agent experiment found only about 35% of eligible treated chats completed without escalation; technical escalation protected quality but added time, while emotional escalation performed poorly. [E21]

The implication is not that agents are useless. It is that human intervention is a production input. Review and escalation cannot be placed below the line as temporary cleanup.

## Mechanism F — formation becomes cheaper

The evidence supports faster experimentation more strongly than durable entrepreneurship.

Business Formation Statistics recorded 578,926 seasonally adjusted applications in July 2026 and projected 29,959 employer formations within four quarters. Applications are EIN submissions; projected formations infer later payroll-tax activity. Neither measure observes a viable operator business. [E22]

A 2026 Gusto survey found that 60% of surveyed 2025 founders used AI during launch, but only 3% said they likely would not have started without it. Half said AI made launch faster or cheaper. This suggests acceleration more than creation of entrepreneurial supply. The survey is retrospective and noncausal. [E19]

A 2025–2026 working paper using ChatGPT’s release and industry AI compatibility estimates more than 10% higher firm formation in more compatible industries and presents evidence consistent with lower experimentation cost. It is a substantial observational result, not a proof of operator capture or durability. [E23]

The historical cloud-computing analogy is a warning. Research on AWS-era falling startup costs found lower initial funding and more experimentation, but also more abandonment and failure. Cheaper entry can increase the number of tests without increasing the number of durable winners. [E24]

## Mechanism G — the operator retains value and control

This is the weakest and most important part of the thesis.

Provider terms demonstrate why apparent independence can be misleading:

- Upwork-originated relationships generally must remain on-platform for two years unless a conversion fee is paid; circumvention can trigger suspension. [E25]
- Shopify describes the merchant as seller of record, but Shopify Payments can impose reserves, suspend, or terminate under specified risk and compliance conditions. [E26]
- UK competition authorities found AWS and Microsoft each held 30–40% of 2024 UK/EEA infrastructure-as-a-service spend, fewer than 1% of customers switched annually, and small and medium customers rarely used multiple clouds. [E27]
- OECD competition analysis warns that integrated control over compute, data, cloud, and model interfaces can create lock-in, raise switching costs, and let upstream providers leverage power into downstream markets. [E28]

A firm can legally own the customer contract and still rent discovery, cash access, storefront continuity, model capability, cloud state, or fulfillment. Control must be measured through actual substitution and recovery, not branding.

## Mechanism H — customer acceptance and accountability remain binding

AI capability does not waive procurement, security, insurance, regulatory, or liability gates.

NIST’s 2026 analysis of public responses on agent security found broad agreement that agents create novel security threats and that these concerns are a barrier to adoption. [E29] Verizon’s 2026 breach reporting found third parties involved in 48% of analyzed breaches; an insured small-business loss sample showed a meaningful severe-loss tail. [E30]

Specific markets add explicit gates. Defense contracting can require current cybersecurity certification and flow-down to covered subcontractors. UK public procurement can require Cyber Essentials or equivalent for higher-risk work. EU AI transparency rules began applying to specified uses in August 2026. [E31][E32][E33]

The implication is sector-specific, not universal: fixed acceptance costs may preserve scale advantages in high-consequence work even when production becomes cheaper.

## Mechanism I — durability and transferability

Small firms can survive as income streams while remaining poor transferable assets. Owner dependence is the unmeasured liability.

BizBuySell reported 9,586 marketplace transactions in 2025, with a median sale price of $350,000, median revenue of $703,000, median cash flow of $158,950, and a 170-day median closing period. The data are broker-reported and selected. Separate administrative research found large profit declines at top pass-through firms after owner retirement or premature death, and founding-member death reduced revenue in small startups. [E34][E35][E36]

An operator firm should therefore be tested with owner-absence drills, process and access transfer, customer relationship transfer, and actual buyer diligence—not merely survival or profitability while the owner is constantly present.

# 5. The base population is real; the category is not yet visible

## Nonemployers are not evidence of the thesis

The United States counted 30.43 million nonemployer establishments in 2023, compared with roughly 6.4 million employer firms in the latest detailed size data. That scale makes nonemployers an essential baseline. It does not make them operator firms. [E8][E9]

The nonemployer universe mixes:

- full-time owner-operated businesses;
- gig and platform-dependent workers;
- independent professionals;
- side-income and moonlighting activity;
- partnerships and corporations with no payroll;
- multiple entities owned by one person;
- inactive, intermittent, and tax-structuring entities;
- growth-oriented firms that may later hire.

The experimental Census Self-Employment Job Frame makes this ambiguity explicit. In 2019, about 52.8% of owner-business jobs were held by someone who also earned wages, and 33.7% by someone who concurrently owned another active business. [E37]

The headline count therefore cannot answer whether the owner works full time, owns demand, uses AI, orchestrates a material perimeter, earns a durable surplus, or is economically independent.

## The receipt distribution is a warning

2023 nonemployer gross receipts totaled $1.753 trillion, but the arithmetic mean was only about $57,600 per establishment and roughly 86.75% of establishments were below $100,000 in receipts. These figures are not measures of profit or owner income. They do show why aggregate scale cannot be turned into a prosperity claim. [E8]

The Federal Reserve’s 2026 nonemployer chartbook adds a fragility signal: in its nonrandom 2025 survey, 64% of nonemployers used owner personal funds to handle financial challenges, 31% did not regularly use external financing, and 38% used contractors. [E38]

That combination is entirely compatible with a thick capability perimeter and weak operator economics.

## Small firms already dominated firm counts before generative AI

In 2022 SUSB data, about 63% of employer firms had fewer than five employees, 79% had fewer than ten, and 89% had fewer than twenty. The shares vary by sector. [E9]

Research covering 1982–2014 finds long-run growth in nonemployer activity predating generative AI. [E39] Cloud, outsourcing, industry structure, tax treatment, demographic change, platform work, and weak employer dynamism all matter.

The thesis cannot claim a new AI-driven trend from a stock that was already large and growing.

## Formation and survival must be kept separate

Census Business Dynamics Statistics recorded 508,777 age-zero employer firms and 523,958 firm deaths in 2023. Smaller firms show high entry and exit rates. BLS establishment cohorts show only about half surviving five years and roughly one-third surviving ten. [E10][E40]

Cheaper launch, faster incorporation, more applications, and more experiments can coexist with:

- weak demand;
- low receipts;
- rapid dormancy;
- owner-financed losses;
- platform dependence;
- no transfer value;
- more failed tests.

The correct sequence is **formation → activation → repeat customers → normalized surplus → survival → owner independence → transferability**. Skipping the middle is how startup activity becomes a false economy claim.

## Current AI adoption does not prove boundary change

BTOS provides the strongest high-frequency official measure of employer-firm AI use. It shows adoption spreading, including among micro employers, while larger firms maintain a substantial lead. [E7]

The 2026 AI supplement also shows why adoption is an insufficient metric: most use was narrow and augmentative, while reported AI-related employment decreases were rare. [E6]

“Uses AI” should be banned from the evidence standard unless accompanied by:

- functions and workflows covered;
- task criticality;
- repeated-run accepted completion;
- human intervention and review;
- all-input cost;
- error severity and recovery;
- effect on total labor;
- effect on customer, margin, and durability outcomes.

# 6. The strongest counter-case

The thesis is not mature until it can survive the following alternatives.

## Alternative 1 — payroll compression is denominator fraud

Fewer employees may conceal equal or greater labor in contractors, BPOs, model providers, data work, platform operations, and unpaid owner hours. Revenue per employee rewards this concealment.

**Required test:** compare quality-adjusted output per **total labor hour**, counting employee, owner, contractor, and attributable vendor-mediated human work. If the advantage disappears, the core boundary mechanism fails.

## Alternative 2 — lean firms are a capital-cycle artifact

Tight venture funding, higher interest rates, weaker demand, remote work, and pre-AI cloud/SaaS adoption already encouraged smaller teams. Selected AI-native startups and accelerator cohorts are not representative; a few breakouts can drive means.

**Required test:** matched cohorts by sector, age, demand, funding, business model, geography, and founder experience, with medians, distributions, and pre-trends—not hero cases or aggregate averages.

## Alternative 3 — falling cost creates more failure, not a superior class

Lower experimentation cost increases the number of viable tests. It can also increase entry, competition, abandonment, and owner losses. The cloud analogy supports both effects. [E24]

**Required test:** follow formation cohorts through activation, 12- and 24-month survival, normalized surplus, owner hours, and transferability.

## Alternative 4 — this becomes Platform Economy 2.0

If discovery, billing, customer data, model access, cloud state, and fulfillment sit with a few providers, the operator is a fragile tenant. A small permanent core may be efficient while upstream providers capture the residual economics.

**Required test:** measure direct revenue share, customer-data portability, payment and marketplace dependency, provider spend as a share of contribution margin, substitution time, suspension exposure, and recovery drills.

## Alternative 5 — scale, trust, and regulation preserve incumbent advantage

Large firms already integrate AI more broadly. Security, procurement, insurance, governance, data, and liability create fixed costs that can favor incumbents. Task augmentation may improve large organizations without changing their boundaries.

**Required test:** compare operator firms with larger matched firms on customer acceptance, quality, cycle time, unit economics, and compliance cost. Absolute improvement by small firms is not enough if the relative gap widens.

## Alternative 6 — demand, not capability, remains the constraint

Faster production can flood markets with similar output and raise the relative cost of attention, trust, distribution, and customer acquisition. More capability can reduce prices and margins rather than enrich operators.

**Required test:** direct demand share, customer acquisition cost, payback period, retention, referral, repeat purchase, price realization, and contribution margin—not output volume alone.

## Alternative 7 — the operator is the missing institution

AI can widen performance gaps when judgment determines which advice to use. [E5] A firm may gain capability but become more dependent on one overloaded decision-maker.

**Required test:** decision-error rate, exception load, owner hours, interruption readiness, delegated authority, and ten-working-day owner-absence performance.

## What would make the thesis false rather than merely early

The thesis should narrow or be rejected if, after adequate time and measurement:

- smaller permanent cores do not emerge at comparable accepted output;
- total labor does not fall even when payroll does;
- normalized surplus is no better than matched firms;
- customer retention, quality, survival, or owner workload is worse;
- provider and channel concentration erase operator control;
- large firms widen their adoption and productivity advantage;
- the proposed classifier cannot be applied reliably or identifies a trivial population;
- another category explains the observations more parsimoniously.

These are not hostile conditions. They are what make the thesis more than content.

# 7. Why coined economies hold—or collapse

The phrase is not the moat. Other “economy” categories show what endures.

| Category | What gave it force | What limited it | Lesson for the Operator Economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Experience Economy | Pine and Gilmore offered a memorable progression from commodities to goods to services to experiences, with unit and design logic, then books and consulting [C1] | Broad adoption made the language descriptive; trademark status did not create the category | A causal ladder and usable design principles matter more than phrase ownership |
| Passion Economy | Li Jin contrasted differentiated creators with standardized gig work and described a participant class and platform criteria [C2] | The category was later absorbed into broader ownership and creator narratives | A sharp contrast can launch a category; it still needs durable measurement and institution |

| Ownership Economy | A clear mechanism—users build, operate, fund, and own networks through tokens—connected actors to value distribution [C5] | The author’s 2026 correction narrowed where ownership actually materialized [C6] | Publish falsifiers and corrections; category credibility rises when the author can narrow it |
| Subscription Economy | Zuora paired category language with billing infrastructure, SEC disclosure, a book, institute, events, and a recurring index based on platform data [C7][C8][C9] | The mark survives only for specific services and depends on continuing source use | The strongest model is product + data + institution + repeated measurement, not a manifesto |

## Seven durability conditions

1. **Crisp boundary:** readers can say what is in and out.
2. **Distinct unit:** a countable actor, firm, transaction, or resource flow.
3. **Causal mechanism:** an explanation that differs from adjacent categories.
4. **Measurable proxies:** repeated denominators and uncertainty, not selected examples.
5. **External validation:** independent data or replication.
6. **Institution:** a useful product, dataset, case corpus, benchmark, or recurring report.
7. **Correction cadence:** public revision when forecasts fail.

Operator Economy currently has a plausible mechanism and a proposed boundary. It does not yet have representative prevalence, a longitudinal panel, independent replication, or a mature institution.

## The correct protection strategy

Do not confuse legal protection with epistemic ownership. A category phrase can describe subject matter without functioning as a strong source mark. The defensible assets are:

- original prose, diagrams, and human-authored presentation;
- a behavior-based classifier;
- a longitudinal operator panel;
- a transparent total-labor and normalized-surplus method;
- named, separately cleared products or instruments;
- recurring data releases and correction notes;
- an institution people rely on.

The phrase earns authority through that system. Filing paperwork before the system exists reverses cause and effect.

## The five signals that matter most

If resources are limited, track these first:

1. **Core FTE and total labor at comparable accepted output.** This tests boundary compression without denominator fraud.
2. **Normalized operator surplus.** This tests whether capability becomes operator economics.
3. **Direct-demand and provider concentration.** This tests whether the operator owns the business or rents it.
4. **Twelve-month durability and owner absence.** This tests whether the firm is more than self-employment under load.
5. **Cost per accepted workflow.** This tests whether technical capability survives full economic accounting.

Everything else supports interpretation.

# 9. The Operator Panel

Public data will not resolve the thesis because it does not observe the defining behaviors. Build the missing dataset.

## Research objective

Estimate whether behaviorally defined operator firms are becoming more common and whether, relative to matched conventional small firms, they achieve:

- a smaller permanent core at comparable accepted output;
- higher quality-adjusted output per total labor hour;
- higher normalized operator surplus;
- stronger customer, cash, data, and provider control;
- no worse quality, retention, survival, or labor incidence;
- lower owner dependence and greater transferability.

## Minimum viable design

Recruit **150 firms** for an 18-month panel:

- 60 firms that appear to pass the operator classifier;
- 60 matched conventional small firms;
- 15 platform-dependent or marketplace-mediated businesses as a contrast group;
- 15 capability suppliers—contractors, fractional firms, or infrastructure providers—to observe the perimeter from the other side.

Use at least four sector families with different task and acceptance conditions:

1. knowledge and professional services;
2. software, information, and digital products;
3. commerce and operational services;
4. local or regulated customer services.

Match on age, sector, revenue band, demand conditions, founder experience, funding, geography, and business model. Do not rely on self-identification as an “operator.” Apply the behavior-based classifier with independent coding.

This is the minimum useful panel, not a representative national sample. It can validate measurement and mechanisms before a probability-based prevalence study.

## Data collection burden

- **Onboarding:** ownership, offer, team and role map, provider map, customer origin, historical financials, permissions, and baseline owner time.
- **Monthly pulse:** revenue, customer count, direct/repeat share, employee and owner hours, contractor and provider spend, accepted output, quality failures, incidents, and material changes.
- **Quarterly evidence:** redacted P&L or ledger export, payroll and contractor totals, provider invoices, channel attribution, workflow benchmark, access/dependency review, and customer acceptance events.
- **Semiannual drill:** provider substitution, data export and restore, owner absence, critical workflow recovery, and customer recourse test.
- **Exit interview:** closure, dormancy, hiring, acquisition, owner withdrawal, or category reclassification.

Use a data dictionary, versioned definitions, a consent and privacy protocol, and publication thresholds that prevent re-identification. Publish aggregate evidence and methods; do not publish company-sensitive raw records.

## Core measurement model

### Quality-adjusted output per total labor hour

> **Accepted customer outcomes × quality weight ÷ employee, owner, contractor, and attributable vendor-human hours**

The outcome unit must be sector-specific and fixed before analysis: resolved case, delivered engagement, shipped order, deployed change, completed service, or another customer-accepted unit.

### Cost per accepted workflow

Include:

- model, tool, API, cloud, and platform fees;
- retries and parallel runs;
- integration and amortized build cost;
- data preparation, routing, monitoring, orchestration, security, and governance;
- human review, correction, approval, and escalation;
- training, coordination, customer support, rework, refunds, and liability;
- maintenance and migration.

Divide by outputs accepted at the stated quality and service level. Pair the metric with intervention rate, defect or reopen rate at 30 and 90 days, and contribution margin per accepted workflow.

### Normalized operator surplus

Calculate operating surplus after a market replacement wage for owner work, all perimeter costs, financing, expected losses, and a resilience reserve. Report both dollars and margin.

Do not hide negative owner economics behind retained earnings or a zero salary.

### Operator control ledger

Score and retain raw measures for:

- direct and portable customer relationships;
- pricing and offer authority;
- cash access and processor concentration;
- customer-data export and contractual rights;
- model, cloud, platform, and specialist substitutability;
- owner-only approvals and relationships;
- customer recourse, warranty, claims, and insurance;
- actual migration and recovery time.

Do not publish a proprietary-looking composite score until the components predict real outcomes. A weighted index without calibration would be branding disguised as measurement.

## Coding reliability

Before outcome analysis:

1. write a casebook with positive, negative, and boundary examples;
2. train at least two independent coders;
3. double-code the first 100 firm-offer-quarter observations;
4. require agreement of at least 0.80 on the main classification;
5. resolve ambiguous rules publicly before examining performance differences;
6. freeze the classifier version for each analysis wave;
7. publish reclassification rates and reasons.

The category is not measurable if independent coders cannot apply it.

## Bias controls

- Recruit beyond the existing audience; followers of the thesis are a selected population.
- Record firms that decline and why; financial data demands will produce nonresponse bias.
- Keep failed, dormant, sold, and reclassified firms in the denominator.
- Report medians, distributions, and uncertainty—not only means.
- Pre-register the matching and outcome model.
- Separate exploratory analysis from confirmatory analysis.
- Use independent analysts or a data partner for the first replication.
- Publish nulls and negative cases with the same prominence as favorable results.

# 10. Promotion rules and falsifiers

The thresholds below are **preregistered research-governance rules**. They are deliberately demanding and should be revised only before outcome analysis, with reasons recorded. They are not universal scientific laws.

## Stage 0 — named hypothesis

Allowed language:

> “The Operator Economy is a thesis about how the minimum viable firm boundary may be shrinking.”

Current status: **met**.

## Stage 1 — enabling conditions

Requirements:

- multiple independent studies show material capability gains in compatible tasks;
- relevant capability prices or access barriers fall;
- evidence also maps reliability, integration, and judgment limits.

Current status: **met with strong heterogeneity**.

## Stage 2 — observed architecture

Requirements:

- a behavior-based classifier reaches at least 0.80 independent coding agreement;
- at least 100 cases across three sectors are documented with full boundary maps;
- payroll relabeling and incidental software use are excluded.

Current status: **not met**.

## Stage 3 — measurable emerging category

Requirements:

- two independent, defensibly weighted or probability-based samples estimate prevalence;
- the lower 95% confidence bound exceeds 5% of eligible firm-offer-period units in at least three sectors;
- prevalence rises across at least two waves;
- another group reproduces the classification result.

Current status: **not met**.

## Stage 4 — economic validation

Matched operator firms must show, over at least 12 months:

- at least 15% smaller permanent core at comparable accepted output;
- the advantage persists under total-labor accounting;
- at least 15% higher quality-adjusted output per total labor hour;
- at least five percentage points higher normalized operating margin;
- retention, quality, survival, and owner workload are no worse;
- most revenue comes from directly held customer relationships;
- critical data are exportable and provider-replacement drills pass.

Current status: **not met**.

## Stage 5 — established economy claim

Requirements:

- Stage 4 results replicate independently;
- the population is economically material, not merely statistically visible;
- labor and risk incidence are measured, not externalized from the story;
- at least three years of corrections and cohort outcomes exist;
- the category predicts something adjacent small-business labels do not.

Current status: **not met**.

## Kill conditions

Narrow or kill the category if any of the following persists after adequate measurement:

- reliable classification fails;
- prevalence remains trivial or does not rise;
- smaller cores vanish when total labor is counted;
- economics are no better after owner and perimeter costs;
- dependence prevents meaningful customer, cash, or data control;
- customer outcomes or labor conditions deteriorate;
- large firms compound the same tools faster and widen the performance gap;
- “operator firm” adds no predictive value beyond small firm, networked firm, freelancer, agency, or platform seller.

The kill decision is not a personal failure. Refusing to kill an unfalsifiable category would be.

## What not to use as proof

| Misleading metric | Why it fails | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue per employee | Rewards outsourcing and ignores owner labor and capital | Quality-adjusted output and normalized surplus per total labor hour |
| Nonemployer count | Mixes side gigs, entities, partnerships, platform work, and deliberate operators | Behavior-based prevalence plus survival and surplus |
| Business applications or incorporation speed | Measures entry intent and process, not durable success | Activation, repeat customers, 12/24-month survival, normalized surplus |
| Smaller team or delayed hire | Can reflect weak demand or scarce capital | Matched boundary at comparable output, with total labor counted |
| “Uses AI” | Reveals no integration, reliability, or economics | Workflow breadth, accepted completion, intervention, cost, and outcome |
| Benchmark score | Selected task performance is not a firm | Cost and value per accepted end-to-end workflow |
| Gross margin | Can exclude owner work, failures, vendors, and concentration cost | Normalized operator surplus |
| Followers, GMV, or marketplace customers | May be controlled by the platform | Direct revenue, data portability, repeat purchase, and switching evidence |
| Survival | Can coexist with poverty wages, extreme owner hours, and no transfer value | Survival plus surplus, owner load, resilience, and transferability |
| One-person company | Hides human execution outside payroll | Small permanent core with disclosed capability perimeter |

# 11. Multi-medium doctrine

The thesis should sound consistent across a post, talk, essay, interview, and video. Length can change. The epistemic status cannot.

## Twelve-second version

> **AI is not eliminating the firm. It may be shrinking the permanent core required to operate one. The test is who keeps the customer, control, margin, and accountability.**

## Thirty-second version

> **The Operator Economy is my thesis that more owner-controlled firms will stay deliberately small at the core while scaling through software, AI, contractors, platforms, and specialist partners. We already have strong evidence that capability is getting cheaper and many bounded tasks are compressing. We do not yet have proof that these firms are more prevalent, more profitable, or more durable. That is the research program: count total labor, follow the money, test the dependencies, and publish what would disprove it.**

## Two-minute version

> **For most of business history, a firm hired people and owned infrastructure because outside capability was expensive to find, coordinate, verify, and trust. Cloud, SaaS, payments, logistics, fractional specialists, and now AI are changing that. More capability can be bought in smaller units.**
>
> **That does not mean one person replaces a company. The task evidence is real but jagged. AI can make a bounded workflow dramatically faster, then fail on a nearby task that requires context or judgment. It can save individual time without removing meetings, review, or accountability.**
>
> **So the unit I care about is not the solo founder. It is the accountable firm boundary: a deliberately small, owner-controlled permanent core that keeps the customer promise and orchestrates a wider capability perimeter.**
>
> **There are two bets. First, that the core can become smaller after every contractor, owner hour, and hidden labor input is counted. Second, that the operator keeps the customer, cash, decisions, portability, margin, and recourse. If only the first happens, this is Platform Economy 2.0—not an Operator Economy.**
>
> **The enablers are here. The economic class is not proven. Over the next 18 months, I am tracking total labor, normalized surplus, direct demand, provider concentration, accepted-workflow cost, durability, and owner independence. The thesis earns the word economy only if those measures move together and independent researchers can reproduce it.**

## Ten-minute structure

1. **Start with the boundary, not AI:** explain why payroll is not capacity.
2. **Show the enabling stack:** cloud, SaaS, payments, logistics, specialists, and divisible cognitive output.
3. **Present the task evidence:** gains, jagged frontier, skill heterogeneity, and judgment failures.
4. **Separate the two bets:** boundary compression and operator capture.
5. **Show the strongest counter-case:** hidden labor, capital-cycle confounding, platform capture, and incumbent advantage.
6. **State what is known and unknown:** do not bury the evidence ladder.
7. **Present one falsifier:** total labor or normalized surplus can kill the thesis.
8. **End with the signal system:** invite operators and data partners into the research, not into a belief system.

## Long essay architecture

Use this sequence for a 2,500–4,000 word essay or 20–30 minute video:

- scene-setting example with a visible boundary map;
- definition and exclusions;
- historical lineage;
- capability evidence;
- organization-evidence gap;
- two-bet causal model;
- counter-evidence;
- operator economics and control ledgers;
- 18-month predictions;
- falsifiers and research invitation.

Do not lead with a billion-dollar one-person-company fantasy. It attracts attention by making the least supported claim the category’s identity.

## Claim discipline for every medium

- Put a date on forecasts.
- Attach the denominator to every prevalence claim.
- Say whether a study measured a task, worker, workflow, firm, or economy.
- Say “employer firms” when nonemployers are excluded.
- Carry the strongest methodological caveat beside the headline number.
- Identify vendor and selected-sample data as such.
- Distinguish gross receipts, revenue, contribution margin, profit, and normalized surplus.
- Never call headcount productivity without total-labor accounting.
- Show one contradiction or null in every substantive evidence piece.
- Update or retire claims when a source changes definitions or is superseded.

## Five recurring content pillars

1. **Boundary maps:** one real business, every capability, duty, dependency, and recourse path.
2. **Accepted-workflow economics:** full cost, human intervention, quality, and value.
3. **Operator control:** demand, cash, data, provider, and key-person dependence.
4. **Cohort evidence:** formation, survival, revenue, total labor, and owner hours over time.
5. **Thesis corrections:** what changed, which claim moved, and why.

These pillars compound into a body of evidence. Generic “AI lets one person do more” content does not.

# 14. Method and limitations

## Research question

What current evidence supports the forecast that more owner-controlled firms can operate with deliberately small permanent cores, and which observable signals over the next 12–18 months would strengthen, narrow, or falsify that forecast?

## Evidence cutoff

24 August 2026. Dynamic dashboards and vendor terms should be treated as dated snapshots.

## Source hierarchy

1. official statistical releases and administrative data;
2. peer-reviewed causal or field research;
3. preregistered and transparent working papers;
4. official regulatory, competition, standards, contract, and incident documents;
5. provider datasets and vendor benchmarks with visible methods;
6. selected company cases used only as illustrations.

Provider data were not used to estimate population prevalence. Working papers were not presented as settled peer-reviewed findings. Benchmark performance was not generalized into firm productivity.

## Research strands

The review searched and cross-checked:

- official firm demography, formation, size, survival, nonemployer, purchased-input, and alternative-work data;
- official employer AI adoption by size, sector, business function, and worker task;
- randomized and quasi-experimental evidence on writing, customer support, consulting, coding, entrepreneurship, ideation, workplace time use, and firm outcomes;
- production-agent reliability, intervention, long-horizon, and economically grounded benchmark evidence;
- labor-market evidence using payroll, earnings, hours, job postings, and freelance work;
- platform, payment, cloud, model, and marketplace control and switching conditions;
- security, procurement, insurance, regulatory, and customer-acceptance gates;
- small-business revenue, finance, survival, owner dependence, and transferability;
- the institutional histories of Experience, Passion, Creator, Ownership, and Subscription Economy narratives;
- prior public and commercial uses of “Operator Economy” and the limits of trademark protection.

## Inclusion and synthesis rule

A source was included when it directly measured an enabling mechanism, firm boundary, economic outcome, dependency, accountability constraint, or competing explanation. Every load-bearing quantitative claim carries the observed unit and its main methodological limitation.

Evidence was synthesized by level:

- **task:** performance on a bounded activity;
- **worker:** accepted output or time in a real role;
- **workflow:** end-to-end completion with review and consequences;
- **firm:** customer, revenue, margin, labor, or survival outcome;
- **population:** prevalence or economy-wide effects.

No result was promoted upward without direct evidence at the higher level.

## Important series and denominator breaks

- BTOS broadened its AI question in November 2025 from use in producing goods and services to use in any business function.
- BTOS excludes nonemployers.
- Eurostat’s standard mandatory enterprise coverage begins at ten workers.
- NES changed methodology in 2022, limiting naïve long-term comparisons.
- Business Formation Statistics measure EIN applications and projected payroll-tax formation, not operating success.
- Nonemployer establishments are tax units, not one-to-one people or full-time businesses.
- Revenue, receipts, profit, owner income, and normalized operator surplus are different measures.

## What this is not

This is not a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. It does not estimate one pooled “AI productivity” effect because the tasks, populations, interventions, outcomes, and horizons are not commensurate. It does not claim exhaustive coverage of every private operator or provider dataset.

The stopping rationale is structural: repeated discovery across official data, causal research, firm surveys, provider data, and regulatory sources produced the same boundary. Enabling capability and task evidence are observable. Representative operator classification, total-labor economics, control, and durability are not. More search cannot manufacture variables that existing datasets do not collect. Original longitudinal measurement is the next rational step.

## Confidence summary

| Proposition | Current confidence | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capability is more divisible and cheaper in many compatible tasks | High | Multiple independent price, task, and field results |
| Gains are heterogeneous and require judgment, integration, and review | High | Convergent positive and negative experiments |

## Naming and legal scope

The exact phrase “operator economy” appeared publicly by April 2024 in Ivo Entchev’s [CoinDesk essay describing a Web3 version of the gig or sharing economy](https://www.coindesk.com/opinion/2024/04/26/depin-for-the-win-spreading-the-benefits-of-the-gig-economy). Other current uses apply the phrase to fractional operators, leadership, operationally heavy businesses, and AI-enabled firms. This defeats a factual claim of coinage and demonstrates semantic fragmentation.

The U.S. Patent and Trademark Office explains that a trademark identifies the source of specified goods or services; it does not grant ownership of an idea or category. See [What is a trademark?](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/what-trademark), [Strong trademarks](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/strong-trademarks), and [Federal trademark searching](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/federal-trademark-searching). Exact federal-database absence is not clearance; common-law use, similar marks, goods and services, and failure-to-function or descriptiveness issues still matter. Obtain qualified counsel before filing or adopting any source mark.

# Final position

The Operator Economy does not need to pretend the future has arrived.

Its strongest claim is more interesting than the one-person-company fantasy:

> **The minimum viable accountable firm may be shrinking, but capability alone does not create operator power. The economic category exists only when small cores turn rented capacity into durable surplus while keeping demand, control, portability, and recourse.**

The enabling evidence is already strong enough to present that thesis with conviction. The gaps are serious enough to present the conditions under which it fails.

That combination—clear forecast, hard boundary, visible contradictions, total-labor accounting, and a live signal system—is the foundation for a doctrine worth repeating across mediums.

**Build it. Measure it. Correct it.**
