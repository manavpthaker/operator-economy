# The 5 Billion Dollar Business That Sounds Boring

Zapier is worth around five billion dollars for one job: moving data between apps that don't talk to each other. That's it. No AI breakthrough, no platform play, just plumbing.

The solo version of that same job reportedly pays $500 to $5,000 a month per client, for work most people find too dull to touch. That gap between "boring" and "valuable" is the whole opportunity, and this week's episode of The Operator Economy breaks down exactly how to build it.

## The idea

Every small company runs on a stack of tools that were never designed to work together. The CRM doesn't tell the invoicing system anything. The web form never reaches the right inbox. The spreadsheet doesn't make it to Slack. Someone has to build the wiring between them, and keep it running.

That's the boring-automation agency: build workflows on n8n or Make, connect the apps a small business already pays for, drop an AI node in the middle to handle anything messy (a free-text email, an unstructured form), and charge a build fee plus a monthly retainer to maintain it.

The reason one person can now do a job that used to need an integration team is that the plumbing became drag-and-drop. At Lovingly, I watched a team move the same florist data between three systems by hand, every week. A few automated workflows handled about 80% of that busywork. The hours that came back were the entire point.

## The evidence

On the high end, fully sourced: Zapier reached a reported $310M in annual revenue and a $5B valuation, built on only about $1.4M raised. That capital efficiency tells you the margins on connecting apps are extraordinary.

The newer proof is n8n, the tool a lot of these agencies actually build on. It raised at a $2.5B valuation in 2025, with revenue reportedly growing tenfold in a year and 3,000+ enterprise customers including Vodafone and Microsoft. The tool solo operators run their businesses on is itself a billion-dollar company.

Zoom out further and the whole integration-platform category is estimated at $14-23 billion in 2026, growing over 20% a year. The range is wide because analysts don't agree where the category ends, but the direction isn't in question.

On the low end, the numbers get less rigorous, they come from agency how-to guides, not audited books, so treat them as reported, not confirmed. What they consistently show: a one-time build fee of $1,500-$5,000 for a simple workflow (more like $6,000-$15,000 for a complex one), then a monthly retainer of $500-$5,000 to monitor and maintain it. Two solid retainer clients, reportedly $7,000-$12,000/month combined, can cover a solo founder's income with room to grow.

## The takeaway: price the upkeep, not the build

The retainer isn't payment for building the automation. It's payment for the automation still working the morning an app changes its login, a form adds a field, or a workflow quietly breaks at 2am. That maintenance is the recurring revenue, and it's the reason this work never fully commoditizes.

Price as a build fee plus a flat monthly retainer, never hourly, because hourly punishes you for getting faster. Tell clients it's a dial tone: they never think about it until it stops, and the whole value is that it doesn't stop.

## The honest math

Operator tooling runs well under $100/month (n8n self-hosted is free; cloud runs $20-50; Make starts at $9). The client pays for their own software. Your margin is almost entirely your time.

Realistic year-one income for a solo operator: roughly $2,000-$6,000/month from 3-6 retainer clients, once you're past a slow first month. That's an estimate reasoned from reported pricing bands, not an audited outcome, no promises here, just documented ranges.

Three ways this fails: platform risk (the tool you build on, or an app you connect to, changes underneath you), commoditization (the simplest automations get easy enough that clients build them alone, so the durable work is the messy, judgment-heavy integrations), and distribution (the tools got easy, finding clients did not, your pipeline still runs on trust and word of mouth).

The open question the episode ends on: does this stay a bespoke solo retainer practice, or do the best operators productize the same five workflows and sell them a hundred times?
