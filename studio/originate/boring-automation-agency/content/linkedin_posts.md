**Theme: Career as Product**

The most underrated skill in this economy isn't building things. It's noticing what's broken.

Every small business runs on tools that don't talk to each other. The CRM doesn't tell the invoicing system anything. The form never reaches the right inbox. The spreadsheet doesn't make it to Slack.

Someone spends three hours a day copy-pasting between systems to cover that gap. Nobody built a product for it because it's not exciting enough to pitch to investors.

But it's exactly exciting enough to build a career around.

The operators who do well right now aren't the ones chasing the flashiest tools. They're the ones who can see a manual, repetitive gap in someone else's business and fix it quietly, reliably, for a fee.

That skill doesn't require a CS degree. It requires paying attention to how the people around you actually spend their day, and being willing to do the unglamorous work of fixing it.

Your career is the same shape as this business. Find the gap nobody wants to look at. That's usually where the value is.

**Theme: The Broken Market**

Zapier is worth about five billion dollars for one job: moving data between apps that don't talk to each other.

Think about what that means. Not a breakthrough. Not a moat built on some proprietary technology. Just: your CRM and your invoicing tool were never designed to speak to each other, and someone built a business on closing that gap.

This is the market most people miss. Every small business is full of these gaps. A web form that never reaches the right inbox. A spreadsheet that has to be manually copied into Slack. An order that has to be re-typed from the website into the system that ships it.

None of this is broken because it's hard. It's broken because it's boring, and the market has quietly priced that boredom at real money. Solo operators doing the small version of Zapier's job reportedly charge $500 to $5,000 a month per client.

The biggest inefficiencies left in the economy aren't hiding in complex problems. They're hiding in problems too dull for anyone to bother fixing.

**Theme: Building with AI**

The most useful AI feature in most of my automations isn't a chatbot. It's one node in a workflow.

Here's the pattern: an email comes in, messy, unstructured, written by a human. An AI call reads it, pulls out the order or the request, and outputs clean structured data into the next system. That's it. No conversation. No interface. Just one step that used to require a person and now doesn't.

That single node is what turns a simple automation into something worth a real retainer. Without it, you're just moving data from box A to box B. With it, you're handling the judgment calls that used to need a human, at the speed of the rest of the workflow.

At Lovingly, I watched a team move the same data between three systems by hand every week. A few workflows, each with an AI step doing the interpretation, automated about 80% of that busywork. The hours that came back were the entire point.

This is what building with AI actually looks like for most small businesses right now. Not flashy. Just one well-placed node that removes a human bottleneck.
