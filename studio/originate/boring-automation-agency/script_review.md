## hook
- (0) Welcome in. This is The Operator Economy. A series where we break down one business you can build on your own. This week: the least glamorous software business there is, and why it quietly pays.
- (1) Zapier is worth around five billion dollars for one boring job, moving data between apps that don't talk to each other. The solo version of that same job pays a reported five hundred to five thousand dollars a month per client, for work most people find too dull to bother with.
  - source: Zapier valuation ~$5B (reported, market aggregators); agency retainer bands (reported, agency how-to guides)

## thesis
- (1) This is the boring-automation agency. Small companies run on a stack of tools that were never designed to talk to each other. The CRM doesn't tell the invoicing system, the web form never reaches the right inbox, the spreadsheet doesn't make it to Slack. You build the plumbing between them, and you charge a retainer to keep it running.
- (2)  ⚠️ POV NEEDED The reason one person can do this now is that the plumbing became drag-and-drop. Tools like n8n and Make let a single operator deliver what used to need an integration team. [POV: the most tedious data-shuffling task you've watched a team do by hand, week after week, that one automation could have quietly killed.]
- (3) Built small, this is three or four retainers run in a few hours a week, around a job. Built serious, it is a niche automation studio with a waitlist. The unglamour is the moat, because the people who could do this mostly don't want to. So does the money actually show up?

## evidence
- (1) Start at the low end and stay honest. The public numbers here come from agency how-to content, not audited books, so treat them as reported, not confirmed. What they consistently show is a solo operator charging a setup fee plus a monthly retainer, and needing only a handful of clients to replace a salary.
  - source: Agency how-to guides (LearnForge, Arsum, n8nitro) — reported, vendor-adjacent, not audited
- (2) The pattern is a one-time build of fifteen hundred to five thousand dollars for a simple workflow, more like six to fifteen thousand for a complex one, then a monthly retainer of five hundred to five thousand to monitor and maintain it. Two solid retainer clients, reportedly seven to twelve thousand dollars a month combined, can cover a solo founder's income with room for more.
  - source: Agency pricing guides (Arsum, taskip, Digital Identity Architects) — reported, unaudited
- (3) The reason a small business pays every month for something invisible is that the alternative is a person doing it by hand, or the work just not happening. An owner losing three hours a day to copy-paste between systems will happily pay a few hundred dollars a month to make those hours disappear, and that is the whole pitch.
- (4) Now the high end, fully sourced. Zapier, the company that turned this into a product, reached a reported three hundred ten million dollars in annual revenue and a five billion dollar valuation, and it did it on about one and a half million dollars of venture money. That capital efficiency tells you the margins on connecting apps are extraordinary.
  - source: Zapier revenue/valuation/capital efficiency — reported via market aggregators (getLatka, Sacra)
- (5) The newer proof is n8n, the tool a lot of these agencies actually build on. It raised at a two and a half billion dollar valuation in 2025, with revenue reportedly growing tenfold in a year and three thousand enterprise customers including Vodafone and Microsoft. The tool the solo operator runs on is itself a billion-dollar business.
  - source: n8n $180M Series C at $2.5B (2025) — verified, multiple outlets (Ventureburn/TechCrunch); ~$40M ARR + 10x usage per Sacra
- (6) Zoom out and the whole category, integration platforms, is a market analysts estimate at somewhere between fourteen and twenty-three billion dollars in 2026, growing north of twenty percent a year. The range is that wide because nobody agrees where it ends, but the direction isn't in question. So what does the operator actually need to build this?
  - source: iPaaS market size — analyst estimates, wide range (Precedence, Fortune Business Insights)

## stack
- (1) The engine is n8n or Make. You can self-host n8n for free, or run it in the cloud for twenty to fifty dollars a month, and it's the one most agencies standardize on because you own the workflows outright. Make starts around nine dollars a month and is friendlier if you never want to touch a server.
  - source: public pricing — n8n cloud, Make plans (vendor pricing pages)
- (2) The parts you connect are whatever the client already uses. A CRM like HubSpot, an invoicing tool, a web form, a spreadsheet, a Slack channel. You aren't replacing their software, you're the wiring between the boxes they already bought and already pay for.
- (3) Where it gets interesting is the AI step in the middle. A Claude or GPT call inside the workflow can read a messy email, pull out the order, and drop clean data into the next system, the part that used to need a human to interpret. That one node is what turns simple automation into something worth a real retainer.
- (4) All in, the operator's own tools run well under a hundred dollars a month, and the client pays for their own software on top. Your cost is the platform subscription and your time, which is exactly where the margin comes from, the same margin Zapier scaled to five billion.
  - source: public pricing — operator stack totals under $100/mo
- (5) And the retainer isn't for the build, it's for the upkeep. An app changes its login, a form adds a field, a workflow quietly breaks at 2am. That maintenance is the recurring revenue and the reason the work never fully commoditizes. So how do you land the first client?

## playbook
- (1) Week one, pick one industry and one painful workflow inside it, ideally one you already understand. Real-estate lead routing, clinic intake forms, e-commerce order sync. Narrow beats broad, because a niche lets you reuse the same build and speak the client's language instead of learning a new business every time.
- (2) Then build one workflow for a real business, not a demo. Take an actual hand-off they do manually, automate it end to end, and show them the before and after. The working automation is the sales pitch, the same way a finished renovation sells the next one better than any brochure.
- (3)  ⚠️ POV NEEDED Price it as a build fee plus a monthly retainer, never per hour, because hourly punishes you for getting faster. A setup fee to deliver it, then a flat monthly to monitor and maintain. [POV: how you'd explain to a skeptical owner why they keep paying every month for something that just runs quietly in the background.]
- (4) Month one, land your first two or three clients from people who already know you run this. Their working automations, with the hours saved written down, become the case studies that get the next five. Boring work spreads by word of mouth precisely because the result is so concrete, an owner can point at the hours they got back.
- (5) Month two is delivery discipline. Document every workflow so you aren't the single point of failure, set alerts so you hear about a break before the client does, and send a short monthly note showing what ran and what it saved. That note is what renews the retainer. So what does this actually pay?

## economics
- (1) We turned this whole build, the niche picker, the first workflow, and the retainer pricing, into a blueprint you can download free, linked below, if you'd rather start from a checklist than a transcript.
- (2) Realistic year one for a solo operator is somewhere around two to six thousand dollars a month, from three to six retainer clients, once you're past a slow first month. That's an estimate reasoned from the published pricing bands, not audited results, and it assumes you can actually find those first clients.
  - source: Estimate reasoned from reported agency pricing bands — unaudited
- (3) The first failure mode is platform risk. You're building on someone else's tool, and n8n or Make can change pricing, limits, or terms, or the app you connect can break its integration overnight. Own your workflows where you can, and never let one client's whole system hang on a single fragile link.
- (4)  ⚠️ POV NEEDED The second is commoditization. The simplest automations are getting easy enough that clients will eventually build them alone, so the durable work is the messy, judgment-heavy integrations, not the two-step ones. [POV: your read on which unglamorous tasks people will always pay to never think about again.]
- (5) The third is the one that haunts every service business. The tools got easy, finding clients did not. Your pipeline still comes from trust and word of mouth, and no workflow automates that. The open question is whether this stays a solo retainer practice, or whether the best operators productize the same five workflows and sell them a hundred times. Which one are you building?
