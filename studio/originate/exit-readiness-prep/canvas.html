§01

Operator

An experienced professional in operations, finance-adjacent work, consulting, or systems, who can run a structured engagement, read a set of accounts, and tell a founder that the business depends on them. PARALLEL: ANA-002, ANA-003.

No licence or sector credential is required for preparation work in most of the country. OBSERVED: CLM-004. The barrier is judgement, not certification.

Not a fit: someone who wants to broker sales, give valuation opinions, or introduce buyers. Those are different businesses and some of them are licensed.

Read CLM-004 as directionalLicensing varies by state and changes. This is not legal advice. Verify the line where you work, in writing, before any paid conversation.
§02

Buyer and beneficiary

Economic buyer: the owner of an owner-operated business, typically 55+, intending to exit within one to five years. OBSERVED: CLM-002.

Beneficiary: the same owner. Secondarily the acquirer, who receives a business they can actually diligence.

Purchase trigger: a buyer walks or reprices; an adviser tells them they are not ready; a peer's sale falls through; a health or partnership event makes the timeline real. MODELED — triggers are inferred from the failure pattern, not surveyed.

Qualified-buyer rule: real profit, owner intends to exit in one to five years, records exist but are not diligence-grade, and operations depend materially on the owner.

Unsuitable-buyer rule: already professionally managed under a non-owner operator; no realistic buyer; owner unwilling to give the interview time; or an owner who wants a valuation or a buyer introduction rather than preparation.

§03

Costly problem

Owners reach the point of sale with owner-dependent operations, undocumented process and records a buyer cannot verify — and are repriced or passed over.

Consequence, quantified from two independent directions. OBSERVED: CLM-001.

  • Only about 30% of listed small businesses successfully sell. The modal outcome of going to market is not transacting.
  • About half of exits are involuntary — death, disability, divorce, disagreement, distress. The event is certain; its timing often is not the owner's.

Buyers now run fast, competitive diligence: 87% of deals over $5M drew at least three offers in Q2 2026, and 33% drew ten or more. OBSERVED: CLM-003.

What is not claimed: no specific multiple uplift is attributed to preparation. UNKNOWN.

Read the hedge before the numberCLM-001 and CLM-002 come from the Exit Planning Institute, which trains and certifies exit planners and has an interest in owners feeling unprepared. CLM-002 is a self-reported survey average, not any particular owner. CLM-003 is advisers reporting their own closings, not an audited database. Take all of them as directional rather than precise.
§04

Offer

A fixed-scope, fixed-fee sale-readiness engagement for one business.

Included: readiness diagnostic against a published checklist; records and reporting hygiene; process documentation; owner-dependence reduction; customer-concentration and key-person analysis; assembled diligence package; prioritised remediation plan with what was fixed and what remains open.

Excluded, explicitly: valuation opinions, buyer introductions, representing the owner in a transaction, negotiating or structuring a deal, tax and estate planning, and any legal advice.

Pricing is fixed-fee as a legal consequence, not a preference. Success fees tied to a transaction are what trigger brokerage licensing. OBSERVED: CLM-004.

Evidence status: PARALLEL/MODELED — the format transfers from due-diligence preparation and compliance-readiness consulting; the combination is unproven.

§05

Buyer result

The owner receives a business that survives inspection, and a written record of what was resolved and what was not.

Primary acceptance measure: every item on the pre-agreed readiness checklist is marked resolved, deferred with a stated reason, or out of scope — and the owner signs that record.

Why the acceptance measure is not the sale. The operator does not control whether a sale happens, when, at what price, or to whom. Roughly half of exits are involuntary, and most listings do not transact at all. Tying acceptance to a sale would make the deliverable depend on an event outside the engagement — and would also convert the fee into a success fee, which is the thing that triggers licensing. The acceptance event is therefore the signed checklist, not the closing. OBSERVED: CLM-001, CLM-004.

Baseline: the diagnostic reading taken at engagement start.

Influence boundary: the operator changes what the business can show a buyer. They do not change whether a buyer appears.

§06

Delivery system

Before delivery

  1. Qualify against the buyer rule; decline unsuitable owners.
  2. Agree scope, the readiness checklist, confidentiality, and what the engagement will not do.
  3. Take the baseline diagnostic reading.

Core workflow

  1. Structured owner and staff interviews to surface undocumented process and owner-held relationships.
  2. Draft SOPs and the operating record from those interviews.
  3. Records and reporting hygiene — resolve what a buyer would ask for and cannot currently be produced.
  4. Concentration and key-person analysis.
  5. Owner-dependence reduction: identify what only the owner does, and move or document it.
  6. Assemble the diligence package.

Quality and exception handling

Human judgement points, none delegable to tooling: what a buyer will actually discount; what is fixable inside the engagement versus structural; when an owner is not going to do the work; and when a finding needs a licensed professional instead.

Failure routing: anything touching valuation, deal structure, tax, or law routes out to the owner's own adviser. Recovery: if the owner will not give interview time, the engagement pauses rather than producing an unsupported record.

After delivery

  1. Walk the owner through what was resolved and what remains.
  2. Hand off — including, where a transaction follows, a named referral to a licensed broker or adviser.
  3. Optional re-reading later, scoped and priced separately.

Access ownership: the owner owns every artifact produced. The operator retains no ongoing access after handoff. Revocation is immediate on request.

§07A

Business-of-one design

What AI compresses: the documentation burden — drafting, assembly, organisation. Historically this is what made readiness work a team activity.

What stays human: judgement, accountability, the owner relationship, and the boundary that keeps the practice outside brokerage.

Why one operator can own the initial model: the engagement is sequential, bounded, and gated on one person's availability — the owner's. Adding people does not compress owner-interview time. MODELED.

Declared capacity boundary: one operator, one engagement at a time in its interview phase, with documentation phases overlapping.

§08

Go-to-market path

First segment: owners already inside an adviser's orbit — CPAs, wealth and insurance advisers, and brokers who decline listings as unready.

Credibility artifact: the readiness checklist itself, published.

First conversation: with the adviser, not the owner. "What do you do today with the ones who aren't ready?"

First paid ask: one fixed-fee engagement.

Single point of failure, stated plainly: this rests on brokers referring the work rather than doing a version of it free to win the listing. UNKNOWN — first thing the 30-day plan tests.

Capacity check: 8 × 85 = 680 delivery hours. Against ~900 available hours, that leaves ~220 hours for acquisition, admin and overrun.

Break-even condition: roughly 1 engagement per year covers fixed overhead. The business breaks even almost immediately and that is not the interesting number.

Modeled owner-compensation requirement: $120,000.

Customers or transactions required to meet it: this is where the model strains.

required gross = $120,000 + $10,000 costs = $130,000
at base price $12,000  ->  10.8 engagements
10.8 × 85 hours        =  920 delivery hours  (against ~900 available)

The base case does not clear the modeled livelihood requirement. At $12,000 it needs roughly 11 engagements, which consumes the entire delivery capacity and leaves nothing for acquisition. The alternatives are explicit:

  • Raise the fee. At 8 engagements, $130,000 requires about $16,250 each — the upper half of the modeled range.
  • Or accept less than the modeled compensation while the practice is being proven.

Most sensitive assumption: delivery hours per engagement. Everything turns on it, it is unmeasured, and its largest component — owner interview time — is the part AI does not compress.

Cash-timing risk: fixed-fee engagements should stage payments; a two-to-three month engagement paid on completion creates a working-capital gap.

Share of the reachable buyer set implied: single-digit engagements per year against a transition population in the millions — a negligible share. That is not the argument. A large market does not become reachable demand. The real question is whether one operator can find eight qualified owners a year through a referral network, and that is UNKNOWN.

Why the underlying problem is large and costly enough for that share to matter: the operator needs eight owners a year out of a population where most listings fail and most owners are unprepared. The problem's size makes eight plausible; it does not make eight reachable.

Evidence or test needed to show the share is reachable: one adviser referring one owner who pays.

Why this remains a sustainability model rather than an income promise: every figure is an assumption with its arithmetic shown, and the base case is recorded as failing the compensation target rather than adjusted until it passes.

§12

First 30-day construction and validation plan

  1. Build the checklist and publish it. It is the credibility artifact and the delivery spine.
  2. Run one full diagnostic, unpaid, on a real business. Record actual hours. Note which findings the owner had not already seen.
  3. Ask three brokers what they do today with listings they decline, and whether they would refer.
  4. Verify the perimeter in the operator's own state, in writing.

Success signal: an owner recognises findings they had not seen and asks what remediation costs; at least one adviser will refer.

Failure signal: the owner sees nothing new; brokers do it themselves.

Kill or redesign condition: measured hours make the fee unsellable, or advisers will not refer, or the operator's state defines the perimeter so tightly that fixed-fee preparation needs a licence.
