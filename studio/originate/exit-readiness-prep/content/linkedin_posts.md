## Post 1: the incentive gap

Every professional around a small-business sale is paid when it closes. A broker takes a success fee. Accountants and lawyers are paid for work on the deal, and a wealth adviser becomes interested once there are proceeds to manage.

For the owner, that is a reasonable arrangement. She doesn't want to write cheques for a sale that might not happen, and success fees put that risk on the broker. For a business already in good shape, it works.

That arrangement has a cost, and it shows up years before a sale. Work from the Exit Planning Institute puts the share of listed small businesses that actually sell at roughly three in ten. EPI certifies exit planners, so treat that as directional. It is not for lack of buyers: in the IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey for Q2 2026, almost nine in ten deals above five million dollars drew three or more offers (advisers reporting their own closings, not an audited database).

A failed inspection shows up in three shapes. Customers are loyal to the owner personally, two clients carry most of the revenue, or the records can't be verified by a stranger in an afternoon. A buyer can't price what he can't inspect, so he discounts it or walks.

Fixing that takes years of lead time, and nobody in the room is paid to do it that early. That work has no owner and no budget line.

## Post 2: the fee is fixed by the law, not by preference

The obvious way to price business-sale preparation is a cut of the sale. It aligns you with the outcome, and it is how most of the professionals around a sale are paid.

For a preparer, it is also the one model you may not be able to use. In a meaningful number of US states, taking a fee tied to the sale of a business is a regulated activity, and in some places doing it without the right licence can cost you every fee earned on the deal. Rules vary and change, so verify your own jurisdiction with counsel. This is not legal advice.

That constraint shapes the whole business. The fee has to be fixed and paid for defined work, and the deliverable can't be the sale, because the moment payment depends on the deal, the preparer is doing the regulated thing. What gets delivered instead is a checklist: every item resolved, deferred with a stated reason, or ruled out of scope, and signed by the owner.

A house sale has the same split: an agent is paid when it completes, and a surveyor is paid for looking, whether it completes or not. A sale-readiness practice is the surveyor.

## Post 3: the number that decides it has not been measured

The base case for a one-person sale-readiness practice does not clear its own income target, and the input that decides it has never been measured. What follows is a modeled scenario with every assumption stated, not observed performance or an earnings forecast.

Assume twelve thousand dollars per fixed-fee engagement and eight engagements a year. That is ninety-six thousand gross, about eighty-six after an assumed ten thousand in tools and overhead, before the owner is paid or taxed. Against an assumed target of a hundred and twenty thousand, the base case does not clear.

Closing the gap takes a fee near sixteen thousand, or about eleven engagements. At an assumed eighty-five hours each, eleven engagements is roughly 935 hours of delivery, close to a full working year with nothing left for finding the next client.

Everything turns on the eighty-five. Nobody has measured it. The largest share of that time is spent in a room with the owner, and no tool shortens that part. AI has made the documentation cheap; it has not touched the interviews.

That makes the first useful step unusually cheap: run the diagnostic once, for an owner you already know, and time it.
