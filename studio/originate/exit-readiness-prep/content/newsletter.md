**Subject:** Everyone is paid when a small business sells. Nobody is paid to get it ready.

**Preview text:** A one-person practice built in that gap, and the number that decides whether it works.

---

A buyer asks an owner one question: what happens here if you're not around for a month? If the honest answer lives in her head, he is looking at a job, not a business. He can discount it or walk away.

That moment is common for a reason. Work from the Exit Planning Institute puts the share of listed small businesses that actually sell at roughly three in ten. EPI trains and certifies exit planners, so it has an interest in owners feeling unprepared, and the figure is best read as directional. Buyers are not scarce, though. In the IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey for Q2 2026, almost nine in ten deals above five million dollars drew at least three offers, and a third drew ten or more. That survey is advisers reporting their own closings, not an audited database.

Put those side by side and the problem is rarely that nobody is looking. It is what a buyer finds when he does: customers loyal to the owner personally, revenue sitting with two clients she brought in, or records a stranger can't verify in an afternoon.

Now look at who is in the room. The broker is paid when the deal closes. The accountant and the lawyer work the deal. The wealth adviser becomes interested once there are proceeds. That arrangement is good for owners, who don't have to pay for a sale that might not happen. It also means nobody in the room is paid to sit down with her three years out and tell her what a buyer will see.

This week's episode builds that missing job as a sale-readiness practice: one person, a fixed scope, a fixed fee. It documents how the business runs, cleans up the records, maps what only the owner can do, and hands over a checklist the owner signs, each item resolved, deferred with a reason, or ruled out of scope. It does not value the business, find a buyer, negotiate, or advise on the deal.

The fee structure is not a style choice. In a meaningful number of states, taking a fee tied to the sale of a business is a regulated activity, and in some places doing it without the right licence can cost you every fee earned on the deal. The episode names no states, and neither does this email. Verify your own jurisdiction.

AI matters here in one specific way. Interviewing an owner, writing up how the work happens, and assembling records into something a stranger can follow used to take a team, which is why the fee never worked at this size. Drafting and assembly are now the cheap part. Deciding what a buyer will discount, and telling an owner the business depends on her, still is not.

Then the arithmetic, which is a modeled scenario, not observed performance or an earnings forecast. At an assumed twelve thousand dollars an engagement and eight engagements a year, gross revenue is ninety-six thousand, or about eighty-six after an assumed ten thousand in costs. Against an assumed target of a hundred and twenty thousand, that does not clear. Getting there takes a fee near sixteen thousand, or about eleven engagements, which at an assumed eighty-five hours each fills the working year.

That eighty-five is a guess. Nobody has measured how many hours one of these takes, and the biggest piece of it, time in a room with the owner, is the part no tool shortens.

The verdict is build, carefully enough to find out. Two findings would end it: owners who agree and still defer, and brokers who already do this for free to win listings. The first move costs nothing. Write the checklist, run the diagnostic once for an owner you already know, and time it.
