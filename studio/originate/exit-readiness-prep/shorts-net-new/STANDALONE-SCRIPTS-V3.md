## 1. The Thirty-Day Map

> You can build a service that helps an owner get a business ready to sell before there is even a buyer.
>
> Start with one question: could this business run for thirty days without the owner?
>
> Ask them to explain how. Circle every answer that becomes “I call,” “I approve,” or “I know.”
>
> Beside each circle, write who else could act, what they would need, and what stops if nobody can.
>
> That gives you a first dependency map.
>
> It shows where the service starts: transferring or documenting the knowledge, decisions, and relationships the business still can’t hand off.

Standalone payoff: the viewer can create a first owner-dependency map and see where a bounded
preparation service starts. This is a working diagnostic, not proof of business failure, sale or
valuation effect.

## 2. An Operations Business Wearing a Suit

> Helping owners get a business ready to sell sounds like M&A. But the service before the deal is really operations work.
>
> A buyer needs to know whether customers get served, decisions get made, and records can be found when the owner steps away.
>
> An experienced operator can trace those dependencies and document how the business actually runs.
>
> AI can organize interviews and draft procedures. It can’t decide which missing process a buyer will question.
>
> So the core qualification isn’t deal experience. It’s knowing how to make a company less dependent on one person.
>
> Valuation, buyer search, and negotiation stay outside the engagement.

Standalone payoff: the viewer can distinguish preparation from transaction work, understand the
operator skill at the center, and see the engagement boundary.

## 3. How You Charge Changes the Job

> You can build a service helping owners prepare a business for sale. But how you charge changes what job you’re doing.
>
> A fee tied to the sale can trigger brokerage rules, depending on where you operate. Verify your jurisdiction. This isn’t legal advice.
>
> Keep the offer focused on preparation: map owner dependencies, organize records, document operations, and deliver a readiness plan.
>
> One bounded model is a fixed fee for that defined work, independent of whether a sale closes.
>
> Fixed-fee wording doesn’t settle every legal question, but it gives you a clear scope to take to local counsel.

Standalone payoff: the viewer receives a bounded fixed-fee preparation model and the correct next
legal check without mistaking wording for legal clearance.

## 4. Test the Front Door

> If you want to build a service that gets businesses ready to sell, your first useful conversation may be with a broker, not an owner.
>
> The service is the preparation in between: document how the company runs, clean the records, and map what still depends on the owner.
>
> Ask the broker: what happens to a business you can’t list because it isn’t ready?
>
> If they already do that work for free, the referral channel may be blocked. If they turn the business away or refer it elsewhere, ask what was missing and who handles it now.
>
> That gives you real readiness problems and a possible path to owners.
>
> But a referral proves access, not demand. Only an owner agreeing to pay begins to prove the business.

Standalone payoff: the viewer gets a concrete broker-channel interview, an interpretation rule and
the boundary between access and demand.
