## hook
- (0) Welcome in. This is The Operator Economy. A series where we break down one business you can build on your own. This week: selling video to companies that will never point a camera at anyone.
- (1) Translating one corporate video into ten languages used to cost a reported fifty thousand dollars and take three months. One operator with an AI avatar can now do it in an afternoon, on about two hundred dollars a month of tools. That gap is the whole business.

## thesis
- (1) This is a video service business for companies that need video but will never film it. Training modules, product demos, onboarding, sales explainers, the same message in six languages. An AI avatar reads the script, and you handle the parts that actually matter, the script, the quality control, and the delivery.
- (2)  ⚠️ POV NEEDED What changed is that the avatars crossed the line from obviously fake to good enough for internal and marketing video. - (3) Built small, it's a handful of clients paying you to turn their scripts into finished, localized video. Built serious, it's a productized studio churning out training libraries at volume. The catch is that the same tools are cheap enough for the client to try themselves. So does anyone actually pay a person to do this?

## stack
- (1) The avatar engine is HeyGen or Synthesia. HeyGen is the self-serve one, from about twenty-nine dollars a month, and it does avatars and video translation with matched lip-sync in over two hundred languages. Synthesia is the enterprise-flavored option, similar entry price, but famously capped at ten minutes a month on the low tier.
  - source: public pricing — HeyGen, Synthesia plans
- (2) The voice is ElevenLabs, which also powers a lot of these platforms underneath. It handles the narration and the dubbing into other languages, billed by the minute on top of a plan that starts around five dollars. This is where the six-languages promise actually gets delivered.
  - source: public pricing — ElevenLabs plans + per-minute dubbing
- (3) The finishing tools are boring and cheap. Descript or CapCut to cut and caption, a script written with Claude or GPT, maybe a generative b-roll tool for the parts an avatar can't carry. None of this is where your value is, and that is exactly the point.
- (4) All in, a credible one-person studio runs on about a hundred and fifty to three hundred dollars a month of tools. The client pays nothing for software, they pay for finished video in the languages they need, and the margin is whatever your time is worth.
  - source: public pricing — combined avatar + voice + finishing stack
- (5) And the value was never the generation, because the client can press generate themselves. It's the taste to make it not look like a hostage video, the quality control, and knowing which disclosure rules apply. So how do you actually land clients?

## playbook
- (1) Week one, pick a lane where video is a cost center nobody wants to touch. Employee training, compliance modules, product how-tos, or localization for a company already selling in multiple countries. Pick the one you can speak about credibly, and build one finished example, not a demo reel.
- (2) Take a real script a target company already uses, a training doc or a help article, and turn it into a polished two-minute video in three languages. That finished piece is your entire sales pitch. You aren't describing a service, you're handing them their own content, already done.
- (3)  ⚠️ POV NEEDED Price per finished video or as a monthly library retainer, never per hour. And build disclosure in from day one, because from August 2026 the EU requires AI-generated video to be labeled, with real fines behind it. Make compliance part of the product, not a risk you hid. [POV: how you'd pitch a cautious brand on an AI avatar without it feeling like you're hiding something.]
  - source: EU AI Act Article 50 — transparency obligations from Aug 2, 2026 (verified)
- (4) Land the first two or three clients from companies that already know your work, or through the marketplaces where these jobs already live. There are thousands of open AI-video briefs on freelance platforms right now. Their finished libraries become the portfolio that gets you the next clients.
  - source: Upwork ~4,200+ open AI-video jobs, Feb 2026 — reported
- (5) Month two is systems. A template for intake, a consistent avatar and voice per client so their videos feel like a brand, and a review step so nothing ships that looks off or breaks a disclosure rule. That reliability is what turns a one-off video into a monthly retainer. So what does this actually pay, and where does it break?
