# Too Small to Bother

In 2017, America had 5,060 software companies with fewer than five employees. By 2022 there were 7,857. Nobody covered a single one of them — not because the story is small, but because it doesn't fit the only story this industry knows how to tell.

Every piece of tech commentary right now is about the one-person billion-dollar company: whether it's happened, who's closest, how soon. I've stopped finding that interesting. It's not that the ceiling moved. It's that the floor did. The minimum size a software business has to be in order to exist fell through the basement — and almost nobody is asking what that actually produced.

## What the Census Actually Counted

Between 2017 and 2022, U.S. software firms with fewer than five employees grew 55%. They're now 57.5% of all software publishers in the country — the majority. Together they booked $5.3 billion, which works out to roughly $674,676 each. Stripe's economics team separately found that the number of solo operators clearing $1 million a year roughly doubled between 2023 and 2025.

That's the half everyone wants to hear. Here's the half nobody sells: across roughly 115,000 subscription apps tracked by RevenueCat, the top 5% of new launches now earn about 400 times what the bottom quartile earns after year one — up from 200x just the year before. The gap doubled in twelve months. The median app makes under $50/month at month twelve, and about 70% of micro-SaaS products never clear $1,000/month. iOS added roughly 600,000 new apps in 2025, up 30%, while downloads grew 2-3%. That's what happens when you remove a cost constraint from a market and leave the attention constraint exactly where it was.

These two stories aren't in tension. They're the same fact. When the floor drops, more people can stand on it, and it gets worse for the median person standing there. Anyone selling you only the first half is selling you something.

And the ceiling didn't move either — Medvi, a telehealth company, reportedly did $401 million in its first full year with two employees and about $20,000 in starting capital (Forbes, reported not audited). Meanwhile the actual monetizers of the cost collapse were the shovel-sellers: Cursor at a reported ~$2B annualized, Replit ~$240M, Lovable ~$200M. Most of the people digging didn't get that outcome. Which raises the real question: who's tracking eight thousand small software companies that quietly work?

Nobody, because the narrators are investors, and a fund only works if it catches outliers. Across 21,640 venture financings, 65% returned less than the capital that went in; about 4% returned 10x or more. That's not a scandal — it's the model, working as designed. It's also why venture supplies only 4.4% of U.S. startup funding, and why small business is 43.5% of GDP while getting none of the coverage.

## The Takeaway

What actually defends a business too small for venture capital isn't market fluency — Toast was founded by engineers with zero restaurant background and still beat incumbents who had nothing but fluency. Fluency buys you the right first product. What holds a market for a decade is what you accumulate after: integrations, years of someone's records living inside your product, the fact that ripping you out breaks their Tuesday. Toast now makes $4.053B in fintech revenue against $706M in software subscriptions — the software was the wedge, the payments are the business.

But accumulation only answers half of it. The other half is size: a niche holds when what you've accumulated grows faster than the cost of reproducing it, and nobody with capital earns enough by paying that cost to bother. Constellation Software runs six operating groups across 70+ vertical markets buying exactly these businesses — proof the knowledge is transferable, which means what protects you isn't anyone's incomprehension. It's their indifference.

So before you build anything, ask two questions: would anyone with real money bother taking this from you, and whose distribution are you actually standing on? Google Play's listings dropped from 3.4 million to 1.8 million in about fifteen months. A business whose distribution is a platform is a tenant, not an owner.

The count of viable small software businesses is rising. The typical attempt is worth less than it used to be. Your protection and your ceiling are the same number, and three decades of being profitable in one storefront was never a consolation prize for failing to become Amazon — it might just be what a business is.
