**Theme: The Broken Market**

In 2017, the US had 5,060 software companies with fewer than 5 employees.

By 2022: 7,857. Up 55%. They're now the majority of software publishers in the country.

That's the good half of the story, and it's the only half getting told.

Here's the other half: across roughly 115,000 subscription apps tracked by RevenueCat, the gap between the top 5% of new launches and the bottom quartile went from 200x to 400x in a single year. The median app makes under $50/month after a year. About 70% of micro-SaaS products never clear $1,000/month.

These aren't contradictory data points. They're the same fact.

When the cost of building software collapses, more people can build it — and the median outcome gets worse, because the thing that actually gates outcomes (attention, distribution, someone paying) never got cheaper.

Most commentary picks one half of this and sells it as the whole picture. The doubling of solo operators past $1M is real. So is the 70% stuck under $1K/month. Neither cancels the other out.

If you're building something small, plan for the distribution you're competing for, not the tools you now have access to.

**Theme: Career as Product**

Before you build anything small, run two questions — not "can this scale," but these:

1. Would anyone with real money actually bother taking this from you?

Estimate what it would cost a funded competitor to reproduce what you've built: your integrations, your accumulated records, the workflow position you hold. Then estimate what the market would pay them back for doing it. If the answer is "not worth their time," that's not a small idea you should feel bad about. That's your protection.

2. Whose distribution is it?

Build cost was never the binding constraint — distribution was, and the AI-driven cost collapse did nothing to fix that. If your reach depends on a platform's algorithm or mood, you're a tenant, not an owner. Google Play's listings dropped from 3.4 million to 1.8 million in about fifteen months. That risk is measured, not theoretical.

The first question tells you if you're defensible. The second tells you if you even control the thing you're defending.

**Theme: Founder/Personal**

I keep running into the same sentence in different forms, and I think it's the most honest thing I can say about small software businesses right now:

Your protection and your ceiling are the same number.

The reason a market is defensible — too small, too unglamorous for anyone with real capital to bother reproducing what you've built — is the same reason it will probably never be huge. Those aren't two separate facts you get to negotiate between. They're one fact.

For a long time I treated "small and durable" as the fallback plan. The thing you settle for after the big outcome doesn't happen. I don't think that's right anymore.

Small business is 43.5% of US GDP. Only 6.5% of Inc. 5000 companies ever raised venture capital. The businesses that actually get written about are a rounding error of the businesses that actually exist.

Thirty years of being profitable in one storefront isn't a consolation prize for failing to become Amazon. It might just be what a business is — and the last twenty years, where that stopped being enough, might be the weird part.
