A guest books a small inn through a booking site, has a good stay, and comes back a year later through the same site. The inn pays the commission again, for a guest it already knows by name. Booking.com's own partner help says it doesn't share private guest email addresses: the inn sees an alias, and partners are asked to keep the conversation on the platform.

Cloudbeds, which sells hotel software, reports that 63.4 percent of independent-hotel bookings worldwide came through the booking sites in 2025. In Europe, HOTREC found direct channels still carried about half of overnight stays. Nobody has published what a small inn in the United States runs.

This episode builds the job nobody at a short-staffed front desk has time for, as a one-person direct-booking practice: an audit first, then a monthly retainer for three jobs. Findable, bookable, remembered. And it works out the number that decides whether a given property can pay for it: four inputs, and a ceiling the fee has to sit under.

The inn and every figure built on it are illustrative. It is a modeled scenario, not observed performance or an earnings forecast. The shift in booking mix the model rests on is a hypothesis nobody has measured, and at twenty rooms the arithmetic is thin. The episode says so.

Nothing here is legal advice. Guest email rules differ by country and by booking site; verify yours.
