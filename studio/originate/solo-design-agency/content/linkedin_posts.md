**Theme: Building with AI**

The tool stack for a one-person design agency is four tools, not seven.

Plan in Figma or Relume. Produce in Figma Make, v0, or Lovable. Ship in Framer. Generate imagery in Midjourney.

Total cost: under $200 a month. The most-cited case in this category reports running the whole thing on $95.

What changed isn't that the tools exist. It's adoption. Figma disclosed that AI features hit roughly 70% adoption among active users this year. The layout and variant work that used to take a junior designer a few days now runs inside a single file, operated by one person.

Here's what doesn't show up on the pricing page: the tools don't pick. Three of them will each hand you a clean, good-looking option. Deciding which one actually fits this client, this brand, this week is still a human call, every time.

Your prospective clients have the same buttons you do. What they don't have is the hours or the judgment to use them well.

That gap is the product.

**Theme: The Broken Market**

One business. Five different revenue numbers. Same year.

Designjoy, a solo design agency with no employees, has been reported at $1M, $1.5M, $1.7M, $2M+, and $3.1M in annual revenue, all covering roughly the same window. Every version traces back to the founder's own posts. There's no audited figure anywhere in that chain.

The point isn't picking the biggest number. It's the pattern: a self-reported case study gets repeated across enough blogs and newsletters that the retelling starts to look like independent confirmation. It isn't.

Meanwhile Superside, same category, subscription design, reports $44.9M in ARR with 850 employees and a $15,000 a month minimum. Designjoy's list price is $4,995. Same shape of business, roughly three orders of magnitude apart in headcount.

That gap is either an efficiency story about what one person can now do, or a warning about what one person genuinely can't cover yet. Probably both, depending on which client you ask.

The lesson isn't "don't trust case studies." It's: check the sourcing before you build a plan around someone else's unaudited number.

**Theme: Founder/Personal**

Six cold emails. One callback. No signed client yet.

That's the honest state of this episode's own test case, BusyLobby: build a working demo site for a hotel before pitching them, then track who actually opens the link.

One in six sounds like a great number. Reported cold outreach benchmarks for this year sit around 3-5%, so on paper that's several times above average.

We're not going to call it that. Six emails isn't a sample size, it's an anecdote with a flattering ratio. Calling it a 17% response rate would be exactly the move this episode spends five minutes criticizing.

What can be said concretely: for one hotel in Austin, the site photos and room menu got scraped, a working lobby-tablet demo went up in about 40 minutes, and the outreach email led with the demo link instead of a pitch. The one callback came from the prospect who had clicked that link twice before anyone spoke.

Small data. Real process. Better to show both than round up.
