# Script v0.1: workflow-reliability service

Status: immutable review draft; not approved

Episode: unassigned fixture

## S01: The client signed. Then nothing happened.

### Narration

A client signs a proposal with a small agency.

Sales marks the deal complete. Operations waits for an intake form. Finance has not created the invoice. The project manager does not know the kickoff needs to be scheduled.

The client thinks the work has started.

Inside the agency, five people are waiting for five different signals.

This is a hypothetical workflow. It represents a common operating shape, not a measured agency case.

And it creates a better automation business than “I connect your apps.”

The service is to follow one important handoff, expose every missing state, automate only the stable path, test what happens when it breaks, and return a system the client can actually own.

The product is not the automation.

The product is a reliable handoff.

## S02: Humans are the integration

### Narration

Smaller firms keep adding forms, calendars, CRMs, project tools, spreadsheets, inboxes, and billing systems.

Each tool knows one part of the customer.

People carry the rest.

They copy a name. Re-enter a date. Notice a missing field. Remember that this client needs a different approval. Send the message that makes the next tool matter.

KfW reported that German SMEs spend an average of thirty-two hours a month on bureaucratic processes, at a cost equal to 3.9 percent of annual personnel costs.

That is broader than disconnected software. It is not a savings estimate for this service.

UK government research separately found that small businesses adopting productivity technology face information gaps and value reliable, personalized support.

The opportunity is not that software is absent.

It is that the operating logic between the tools is still undocumented and unowned.

## S03: The service category already exists

### Narration

Businesses already pay specialists to solve this.

Zapier operates a partner program for hands-on automation services. n8n's expert-partner pilot targets agencies and systems integrators that earn their main revenue from automation and AI services and already serve at least three active customers.

A Zapier case study also describes a partner mapping and implementing a cross-tool workflow, then providing documentation, training, and support.

Those examples prove the category exists.

They do not prove a new operator can win the work, deliver it safely, charge a particular price, or achieve the vendor case's results.

That is why the first offer should not be a broad automation retainer.

It should be one bounded reliability sprint.

## S04: Define the actual product

### Narration

For our agency, the workflow begins when a proposal is signed and ends when the client has completed intake, received an invoice, and booked a kickoff.

The sprint includes discovery, a current-state map, a baseline, an access plan, one production workflow, representative tests, an exception route, logs and alerts, a runbook, training, and thirty days of monitoring.

The buyer should receive evidence that one handoff changed state.

Fewer undocumented manual touches. Faster cycle time. Fewer missed handoffs. Lower error exposure. Clearer recovery ownership.

The buyer and operator choose the measure before the build.

No promised savings. No promised revenue. No promise that automation is the right answer.

## S05: Build reliability, not the happy path

### Narration

Start by observing the people doing the work.

Map the inputs, states, decisions, systems, credentials, exceptions, and failure consequences.

Keep ambiguous, sensitive, and material decisions human.

Build the minimum stable path in client-controlled accounts where practical. Use least-privilege access and test data.

Then test ordinary, missing, duplicate, delayed, malformed, unauthorized, rate-limited, and downstream-unavailable cases.

Add logs. Add alerts. Decide when the system retries, when it stops, and who owns the exception.

Train the team. Transfer the runbook and access inventory. Monitor the workflow for thirty days.

At the end, recommend maintain, expand, redesign, or retire.

That last option matters. A workflow that should not exist is still a useful result if the sprint proves it early.

## S06: The economics are a stress test

### Narration

Now the model.

It assumes a three-thousand-dollar fixed sprint, two new sprints per month, and twenty-four delivery hours per sprint.

Two sprints produce six thousand dollars in modeled gross revenue.

After two hundred dollars of software and six hundred dollars of acquisition and listed overhead, modeled cash contribution is five thousand two hundred dollars before owner compensation and tax.

Assign forty-eight hours of operator labor at a modeled sixty dollars per hour, and economic contribution becomes two thousand three hundred twenty dollars.

If each sprint takes thirty-two hours, it falls to one thousand three hundred sixty dollars.

Modeled scenario. Not observed performance. Not an earnings forecast.

The model works only if the buyer pays for discovery, testing, documentation, and handoff—not merely the connection between two tools.

## S07: The support trap

### Narration

The attractive version of this business adds a monthly retainer to every project.

The responsible version asks whether an ongoing job actually exists.

Did incidents occur during monitoring? Who responds? How quickly? Which vendor failures are included? Who pays when the client changes a field or revokes access? Does the operator hold credentials or infrastructure the client cannot safely take back?

If those responsibilities are real and the buyer will fund them, monitoring can become a separate offer.

If they are not, the operator should hand the workflow back cleanly and stop billing.

Recurring revenue is not a reason to invent recurring responsibility.

## S08: Sell discovery before architecture

### Narration

The first thirty days are not for building an agency website.

Interview ten owners or operations leads in one reachable service segment.

Require a real process walk-through. Map three workflows. Reject anything with unstable rules, high-risk judgment, inaccessible systems, no owner, or no measurable baseline.

Offer two paid diagnostics. Seek one paid sprint at the predeclared price and scope.

Track the outreach, discovery, proposal, build, tests, failures, support, and buyer acceptance.

Continue only if buyers expose a recurring consequential handoff, authorize safe discovery, and one of them funds a bounded implementation.

Stop or narrow if no one pays, the workflow cannot be bounded, or the support burden breaks the model.

The first deliverable is not an automation.

It is evidence that one workflow deserves to be automated—and that someone will pay for the full reliability job.
