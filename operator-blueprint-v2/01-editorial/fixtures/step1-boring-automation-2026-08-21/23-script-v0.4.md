## S01: The client signed. Then the group project began.

Claim IDs: C008

### Narration

A client signs a proposal with a small agency.

Sales marks the deal complete. Operations is waiting for an intake form. Finance has not created the invoice. The project manager does not know the kickoff needs to be scheduled.

The client thinks the work has started.

Inside the agency, everyone is waiting for a different signal.

And every tool has technically done its job.

That is the problem.

The software stack is behaving like a group project where everybody finished their own slide and nobody assembled the deck.

So the business is not connecting two apps. That is the demo.

The business is making one important handoff reliable when the form is incomplete, the event arrives twice, the billing tool is down, or the person who normally saves the day is not there.

The normal flow is the demo.

Failure handling is the product.

## S02: The people are doing the integration

Claim IDs: C001, C002, C007

### Narration

Look at what the staff is actually doing.

They copy the client name. Re-enter the date. Catch the missing field. Remember which account needs another approval. Send the message that finally makes the next tool useful.

They are not working around the system.

They are the system.

KfW reported that German SMEs spend an average of thirty-two hours a month on bureaucratic processes, at a cost equal to 3.9 percent of annual personnel costs. That is broad administrative burden in Germany, not a count of disconnected software hours.

UK government research separately found information gaps among small businesses adopting productivity technology, along with value in reliable, personalized support.

Put those pieces together and the opening becomes clear.

Tools are available. Burden is real. Support matters.

What is missing is not another app.

It is the operating logic that tells the apps and the people when the shared assignment has actually moved forward.

## S03: The category exists. You still have to earn the business.

Claim IDs: C003, C004, C005, C008

### Narration

People already sell hands-on automation services.

Zapier runs a partner program for them. n8n's expert-partner pilot describes selected agencies and systems integrators that earn their main revenue from automation and AI services and already serve at least three active customers.

A Zapier-published case also shows the delivery shape: cross-tool implementation, documentation, training, and support.

So yes, the category exists.

No, that does not mean a new operator gets customers by putting “AI automation agency” in a LinkedIn headline and waiting for the economy to notice.

You still have to prove the buyer, the handoff, the price, and the result.

That is why you do not begin with a broad retainer.

You begin with one paid diagnostic around one consequential handoff.

## S04: Find the missing owner before you build

Claim IDs: C007, C008

### Narration

Take the signed proposal.

The handoff begins when the proposal is accepted. It ends when one named person has acknowledged ownership of the kickoff.

Now watch the work happen.

What comes in? Which state does it enter? Which decision changes the path? Which system holds the data? Who has access? What can fail? Who owns the consequence?

The acceptance measure is concrete: time from signed proposal to acknowledged kickoff ownership.

Then ask the question automation marketing loves to skip.

Should you build this at all?

Are the rules stable? Does the handoff happen often enough to matter? Can access be bounded? Are the exceptions understood? Can the buyer measure the starting point? Does one person own recovery?

If the answer is no, the diagnostic ends with the map and a recommendation not to build.

That is not a failed sale.

That is proof you sell judgment before software.

The group project does not need another slide. It needs one assignment, one definition of done, and one owner for what happens next.

## S05: Test the dropped work, not just the happy path

Claim IDs: C007, C008

### Narration

If the handoff passes, build the stable path.

Just the stable path.

Keep ambiguous, sensitive, and material decisions human. Use client-controlled accounts where practical, least-privilege access, and test data.

Then test what the polished demo politely avoided.

What happens when intake information is missing?

What happens when the same acceptance event arrives twice?

What happens when billing or the project system is down?

What happens when access is revoked?

If you only tested the happy path, you did not build an automation. You recorded a screen demo.

Failure needs a visible state and a human owner.

Add logs and alerts. Decide when the system retries, when it stops, and who takes over.

Train the team. Transfer the runbook and access inventory. Monitor the handoff for thirty days.

Then recommend maintain, expand, redesign, or retire.

## S06: Make the economics count the operator

Claim IDs: C009

### Narration

Here is the model. Every number in this section is a test assumption, not a market benchmark or an income forecast.

Assume a three-thousand-dollar fixed sprint, two new sprints a month, and twenty-four delivery hours for each one.

The cash view looks cheerful.

Six thousand dollars of modeled gross revenue. Subtract two hundred dollars of software and six hundred dollars of acquisition and listed overhead.

That leaves five thousand two hundred dollars before owner compensation and tax.

Now invite the operator back into the business.

Assign forty-eight hours of labor at sixty dollars an hour. Modeled contribution falls to two thousand three hundred twenty dollars.

If each sprint takes thirty-two hours, it falls again, to one thousand three hundred sixty dollars.

There it is.

The business survives only if the buyer pays for discovery, exception design, testing, documentation, and handoff.

If the buyer is paying for a connection between two tools, the model is not tight.

The offer is wrong.

## S07: A retainer needs a responsibility

Claim IDs: C007, C008

### Narration

Now comes the part every service business finds irresistible.

Add a monthly fee and call it recurring revenue.

Recurring revenue for what?

Who responds when an incident happens? How quickly? Which vendor failures are included? What happens when the client changes a field, replaces a tool, or revokes access?

If the responsibility is real and the buyer will fund it, monitoring becomes a separate offer.

If it is not, transfer ownership and stop billing.

A retainer without recurring responsibility is a subscription-shaped invoice.

It is not a service.

And some processes should never enter a generalist pilot: health, employment, credit, insurance, legal, safety, or any workflow where an automated mistake creates a high-impact decision without qualified review.

Ambition does not excuse bad boundaries.

## S08: Sell discovery before architecture

Claim IDs: C007, C008, C009

### Narration

So what do you do in the first thirty days?

Do not build the agency website.

Talk to ten owners or operations leads in one service segment you can reach. Require a real process walk-through. Map three handoffs.

Reject unstable rules, high-risk judgment, inaccessible systems, missing ownership, and anything without a measurable baseline.

Offer two paid diagnostics.

Only offer the sprint after the diagnostic says the build is responsible. Use the price and scope declared before the test.

Track outreach, discovery, proposal, delivery, tests, failures, support, and buyer acceptance.

Continue only when buyers expose a recurring consequential handoff, authorize safe discovery, and one of them funds the full reliability job.

Stop or narrow when nobody pays, the process cannot be bounded, or support breaks the model.

This can become a broader reliability company. More handoffs. More systems. More recurring responsibility.

But expansion is earned one reliable assignment at a time.

The first deliverable is not an automation.

It is evidence that one handoff deserves to be automated, that the buyer will pay for reliability, and that somebody owns the work when the group project stops being cute.
