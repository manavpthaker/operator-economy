## S03: the second doorway

### Narration

To see the opening, start with the report that's already green.

A customer types a question into search. A page appears, they click it, and the visit shows up in analytics. Maybe they buy something. Now the marketing team has a trail it can follow.

That system isn't perfect, but it's familiar, and it still does the job it was built to do.

The customer has simply added another path.

McKinsey found that roughly half the people it surveyed were deliberately using AI-assisted search. Regular search didn't disappear. Customers added another doorway while they research.

And what appears inside that doorway can come from a different mix of places. The company's site may be one source. Reviews, publishers, forums, and comparison pages may be there too. That mix can change with the question, the category, and the platform.

So the reports may be watching different parts of the same customer journey. The marketing team has a dashboard for one part and almost nothing for the other.

That doesn't make the old report wrong. It's watching one doorway while the customer is using two.

So this isn't a story about regular search dying. The missing view is the opening, but an opening isn't a company.

What should one operator own between the answer a customer sees and the decision the buyer has to make?

## S04: from evidence to responsibility

### Narration

To answer that, separate what the tools can collect from what the buyer still needs.

Products already track prompts, brand mentions, competitors, citations, and source patterns. Researchers were able to measure this surface, but the effects they found varied by domain.

So yes, you can collect a sample. You can compare it, and you can watch it change.

But here's the problem. A new measurement doesn't appoint anyone to decide what it means.

And the buyer isn't starting from zero. SEO firms already sell audits, competitive analysis, projects, and reports. So this kind of purchase won't feel completely foreign. That doesn't mean buyers will want this one, or pay SEO prices for it.

Put yourself back in the marketing lead's chair.

One report shows clicks. Another product shows mentions. Another view shows which sites were cited. The content team knows the pages it owns, and the public-relations team knows which publishers it has worked with.

Nobody has to be doing a bad job. They're just answering different questions.

The buyer still has to decide which facts matter, what can be changed, and when the evidence is too weak to justify more activity. That responsibility doesn't belong to the dashboard. It belongs to a person.

That is the part nobody else owns yet, and it is where this company begins.

The company records what appeared, checks the important facts and sources, separates what the client controls from what it can only watch, and tells the buyer what deserves action.

You aren't selling control of the answer. You're selling a better decision about it.

## S05: the company before the offer

### Narration

That missing responsibility tells us what the full company has to look like. Not the first report. The thing you're actually trying to build.

The AI visibility company works with a small group of brands. It keeps track of the questions that matter, records how the agreed answers and sources change, and checks important statements against facts the client has approved. Then it connects the evidence to a content, reputation, planning, or measurement decision.

Over time, the value isn't a weekly ranking score. It's a reliable record of how the market describes the brand, where the facts are drifting, and which decisions deserve attention before the company spends money fixing the wrong thing.

The point isn't to collect a taller pile of screenshots. The point is to tell the buyer what deserves action, what needs another look, and what should be left alone.

Monitoring only belongs in the company when another look helps with another decision. A new month on the calendar isn't a client result.

One operator stays accountable for that loop. AI helps collect and compare the material. Software keeps the record. The buyer approves the facts. A specialist steps in when the work crosses into expertise the operator shouldn't pretend to have.

The operator owns the judgment. Which is why the operator's experience matters more than the software account.

## S06: why your experience matters

### Narration

The judgment has to come from somewhere. So where should that experience come from?

If you've worked in search, content, digital strategy, or intelligence, you know a result can be interesting and useless. A source can look authoritative and answer the wrong question. And the buyer's decision matters more than the amount of data in the report.

AI can gather and compare the evidence. It can't decide which question matters, which client fact settles a dispute, or when the honest answer is to do nothing.

Use that judgment in a field where you've earned context or a path to the buyer. That may come from industry experience, adjacent client work, or a network that opens the conversation.

That is the advantage, not the software account. Now turn it into something the first buyer can inspect.

## S07: the first purchase

### Narration

The first buyer isn't going to hand you the whole function. They need a smaller, complete piece: one brand, one market, ten questions the buyer approves before collection, and two answer surfaces.

What are they buying?

A record of what appeared, and a decision about each important gap.

Before collecting, ask what decision may change. If the buyer can't name one, stop. A strange answer isn't automatically a paid problem.

Agree on the questions, then record the platform, date, answer, brand presence, important claims, competitors, and sources.

Compare the important statements with facts the client has approved. Some differences won't matter. When a source is weak, narrow the conclusion.

Sort each important gap into what the client can change, what it may influence, what should be watched, and what is too uncertain to act on.

The buyer gets the record and a decision: act, investigate, monitor, or leave it alone. The work ends when the buyer accepts the agreed record and those calls.

No action is valid. It proves the buyer paid for judgment, not a disguised optimization sale.

If every report recommends more work from its author, that isn't a decision service. It's a sales pitch with a clipboard.

Implementation and monitoring are separate decisions. Neither one sneaks into the first invoice because it looks good in a revenue model.

That gives you a bounded offer. It still needs a buyer you can actually reach.

## S08: choose the market you can enter

### Narration

Start where you already have a reason to be useful. You need to understand the buyer's decision, speak the language, and have a credible way to reach the person responsible.

Travel is the example here because it makes the customer journey clear. It isn't the market recommendation.

For that example, build a list of 25 brands in one narrow segment. Before asking for a meeting, prepare a three-question screen from public information. Show what appeared, where it came from, and where the sample stops.

Then ask the buyer one question: would this change anything your team is responsible for?

If the answer is no, record why it didn't matter and move on. Don't announce that the brand has vanished into the machine.

If the answer is yes, ask what decision changes and who owns it. Then offer the complete paid diagnostic.

Demand needs a buyer. A product launch and a busy category can't stand in for one. And buyer access still doesn't prove one operator can carry the work.

## S09: make the numbers work aloud

### Narration

Here's the planning model we're testing. Assumptions, not results, and not an earnings forecast.

The smaller case charges two thousand dollars and allows 16 delivery hours. Set aside 960 for the owner's time, 99 for software, and 300 for finding the client and listed overhead. That leaves 641 dollars before tax and unlisted costs.

The working case charges three thousand dollars and allows 24 hours. Set aside 1,440 for the owner's time and 399 for the other listed costs. That leaves 1,161 dollars before tax and unlisted costs.

At two projects, 6,000 dollars comes in. Set aside 2,880 for 48 delivery hours, 198 for software, and 600 for finding the clients and listed overhead. That leaves 2,322 dollars before tax and unlisted costs.

Revenue on a slide is very cooperative. Client work is usually less polite.

So give the model a harder job.

Suppose the business needs to leave 48,000 dollars a year for the owner's labor and support, before tax and unlisted costs. Each project has 2,601 dollars after the listed costs to cover that work.

Divide 48,000 by 2,601 and round up. The model needs 19 paid diagnostics in a year.

Can one person deliver 19?

Nineteen projects take 456 delivery hours. The owner model allows 960 hours for the year, with no more than 24 diagnostics and 576 delivery hours. That leaves 504 hours for selling, administration, support, exceptions, and specialist help.

Then comes reach.

To keep the arithmetic concrete, the model uses 250 named travel brands. Nineteen paid diagnostics would be 7.6 percent of that list if every project came from a different brand.

That is a demanding sales test. We don't know whether the operator reaches those buyers, whether 504 hours cover the rest of the job, or whether anybody pays the modeled price.

The arithmetic works. The business doesn't become proven because the spreadsheet stopped arguing with itself.

## S10: know what breaks

### Narration

The spreadsheet can fit and the company can still fail.

Maybe the buyer sees ordinary SEO work and won't pay separately. Maybe the sample moves too much to support a responsible comparison. Or maybe a tool gives the buyer the whole decision. If your judgment adds nothing, a report and a meeting don't create a company.

Walk away from any buyer who needs a promise of placement, traffic, leads, or revenue. You don't control those outcomes.

Watch the scope too. One report can quietly become research, content, public relations, legal review, and software support. That's not a business of one. It's a team wearing one invoice.

If the hours break the model, narrow the scope, change the price, or stop. If every job needs several specialists, choose a narrower market or admit the model needs a team. Another month on the calendar doesn't earn a monitoring fee.

Each failure tells you what the first 30 days have to test.

## S11: the first 30 and 90 days

### Narration

Build the test around those failures.

In the first 30 days, make the question template, evidence record, delivery boundary, three-question screen, and first prospect list.

Talk to five marketing or organic-growth leaders. Ask what decision this evidence could change and who owns that decision.

With one consenting brand, run the ten-question diagnostic across two answer surfaces. Track every hour, deliver the record and decisions, then make one paid ask for the same work.

Continue only if buyers name a decision that matters, at least one accepts paid work or names the budget condition, and you stay inside the 24-hour boundary.

The next 60 days are where the assumptions meet a client.

If that gate passes, deliver the paid work and record the real sales time, delivery time, tool cost, specialist cost, support load, and buyer response.

Repeat only for another buyer decision. Add monitoring only when the second look changes something useful. Add another buyer only when the scope repeats without hiding your time.

That is construction. You're building the assets, making the offer, doing the work, and replacing assumptions with paid evidence.

Which brings us back to the green report.
