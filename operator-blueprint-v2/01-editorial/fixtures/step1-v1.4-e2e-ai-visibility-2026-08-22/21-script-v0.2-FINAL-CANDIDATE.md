## S00: the green report

Claim IDs: illustrative thread only

### Narration

You run marketing for a travel company.

Search traffic is steady. The sales team isn't sounding an alarm. The dashboard is green, which normally means everybody can get on with their day.

Then a customer asks an AI tool where to stay.

Your company doesn't appear.

Or it does appear, but the description sounds like it came from a page nobody at the company remembers approving.

The dashboard is still green.

So which thing is wrong? The answer, the company, or the report?

## S03: the second doorway

Claim IDs: C001, C002

### Narration

Let's start with what the old report actually does.

A customer types a question into search. A page appears, the customer clicks it, and the visit shows up in analytics. Maybe they buy something, and now the marketing team has a trail it can follow.

That system isn't perfect, but it's familiar. More important, it still does the job it was built to do.

Now some customers have added another path.

In an August 2025 survey of 1,927 U.S. consumers, McKinsey reported that half intentionally used AI-assisted search tools. That doesn't mean everybody stopped using regular search. It means a meaningful group said they use another doorway while they research.

And the information inside that doorway can come from a different mix of places. The company's own site may be one source. Reviews, publishers, forums, and comparison pages may be there too. McKinsey's source analysis found that the mix can change by question, category, and platform.

So the two reports may be looking at different parts of the same customer journey. The marketing team has a dashboard for one part and almost nothing for the other.

That doesn't make the old report wrong. It means the report is watching one doorway while the customer has started using another.

So the question isn't whether search is dead. It isn't.

The question is whether that missing view creates enough responsibility for one experienced operator to build a company around it.

## S04: components aren't a company

Claim IDs: C004, C005, C006, C007

### Narration

We know parts of this job can be done.

Current products can collect prompts, brand mentions, competitors, citations, and source patterns. A technical GEO benchmark also studied visibility and found that the effects it measured varied by domain.

That tells us the surface can be sampled and studied. It doesn't tell us a buyer will pay for this company, and it doesn't tell us the work will improve a client result.

The neighboring SEO market gives us another useful clue. Buyers already understand audits, competitive analysis, projects, and reporting. But that's an adjacent purchase pattern. It's not proof that SEO demand or pricing transfers to AI visibility.

Put yourself back, for a moment, in the marketing lead's chair.

One report shows clicks, one product shows mentions, and another view shows cited domains. The content team knows which pages it owns, while the public-relations team knows which publishers it has worked with.

None of those people or products has to be failing.

They are answering separate questions.

The buyer still has to put the pieces together, decide which facts matter, work out what can be changed, and say when the evidence is too weak to justify more activity.

That final responsibility is where this stops being a collection task and starts looking like a company.

The opportunity isn't that another data source appeared. Marketing teams already have plenty of data sources. The opportunity is that another business decision appeared, and it may not have a clear owner.

So we have tools that gather evidence, a way to study the surface, and a familiar way for buyers to purchase expert work.

What can none of those things decide?

Whether this evidence matters enough for this buyer to change something.

That is the gap.

An AI visibility company records what happened, checks the important facts and sources, separates what the client can control from what it can only watch, and tells the buyer what deserves action.

You aren't selling control of the answer. You're selling a better decision about it.

And if every report somehow recommends more work from the person who wrote the report, you haven't built a decision service. You've built a sales pitch with a clipboard.

## S05: name the mature company

Claim IDs: C007

### Narration

Built out, the AI visibility company becomes a focused outside function for a small portfolio of brands.

It maintains the questions that matter to each buyer. It records how agreed answers and sources change. It checks important statements against facts the client has approved. It explains competitor and category context. Then it connects the evidence to a planning, content, reputation, or measurement decision.

That last part matters.

You aren't being paid to collect a taller pile of screenshots. You're being paid to tell the buyer what deserves action, what deserves another look, what should be watched, and what should be left alone.

Monitoring only belongs in the company when another look serves another decision. The calendar changing months isn't, by itself, a client outcome.

That is the mature company. The diagnostic comes later as the first part a buyer can inspect.

## S06: why your experience matters

Claim IDs: C005, C006, C007

### Narration

So who should own that judgment?

This is where your experience starts to matter.

If you've worked in search, content, digital strategy, or competitive intelligence, you already know that a result can be interesting and still be useless. You know a source can look authoritative and still answer the wrong question. You know the buyer's decision matters more than the amount of data in the report.

AI can help collect and compare the material. Software can preserve it and show changes. But neither one knows which question matters to this buyer, which client document is the factual authority, or when the evidence is too weak to recommend action.

That judgment is the advantage.

Not access to the same software account everybody else can buy.

## S07: bring the company down to the first purchase

Claim IDs: C002, C005, C007, C008

### Narration

Okay, built out, the company owns the ongoing decision function. But you don't ask the first buyer to trust all of that.

You sell the first part they can inspect.

One brand. One market. Ten questions the buyer approves before collection. Two answer surfaces.

So what is the buyer actually buying?

A completed evidence record and a decision about each important gap.

First, ask what decision the evidence might change. If the buyer can't name one, stop. An odd answer may be worth noticing, but it isn't automatically worth buying a report about.

Then agree on the questions and record the sample. Preserve the platform, date, answer, brand presence, important claims, competitors, and sources where they appear.

Next, compare material statements with facts the client has approved. Don't treat every difference as an error. Some differences won't matter. Some sources won't be clear enough to support a conclusion.

Then classify each important gap. The client controls some facts directly. It may influence some outside sources through ordinary content, public-relations, or reputation work. Other results sit outside the client's control and should only be watched.

Finally, deliver the evidence record and the decision briefing. Act on it, investigate it, monitor it, or leave it alone.

The work is complete when the buyer accepts the agreed record and those classifications.

No action is a valid result. In fact, it may be the clearest proof that the buyer paid for judgment instead of a disguised optimization sale.

Implementation is another decision. Monitoring is another decision. Neither one sneaks into the first invoice because it would look good in a revenue model.

## S08: find the first buyer

Claim IDs: C004, C007, C008, C009

### Narration

For the first test, choose one consumer-travel segment you can reach.

Travel is a test bench here, not a declaration that it's the winning market. Build a list of 25 brands where you can identify the person responsible for search, content, or organic growth.

Before asking for a meeting, prepare a three-question screen using public information. Show what appeared, the sources you found, and the limits of that sample.

Then ask one question.

Would this evidence change anything your team is responsible for?

If the answer is no, don't announce that the brand is disappearing into the machine. Thank the buyer, record why it didn't matter, and move on.

If the answer is yes, ask what decision changes and who owns it. Then offer the complete paid diagnostic.

Demand needs a buyer. A product launch, a research paper, and a busy category don't get to stand in for one.

## S09: make the model carry its own weight

Claim IDs: C008

### Narration

Okay, now we have to make the model carry its own weight.

Every number here is a planning assumption. None of this is observed performance or an earnings forecast.

Start with one working-case diagnostic at three thousand dollars.

The model allows 24 delivery hours, which puts 1,440 dollars toward the owner's delivery labor. Software and the listed acquisition and overhead take another 399 dollars.

After that delivery allowance, 1,161 dollars remains before tax and costs the model hasn't listed.

Two of those projects in a month leave 2,322 dollars after the delivery allowance and listed costs.

Revenue on a slide is very cooperative. Client work is usually less polite.

Now ask the annual question.

For this fixture, use a 48,000-dollar owner-support target before tax and unlisted costs. Before paying the owner for delivery, each working-case engagement has 2,601 dollars available for owner labor and support.

Divide the target by that amount and round up. The model needs 19 paid diagnostics in a year.

Next, ask whether one operator can deliver them.

Nineteen diagnostics use 456 delivery hours. The planning ceiling is 24 diagnostics and 576 delivery hours inside a 960-hour owner year.

So on paper, 19 projects fit. They leave 504 hours for selling, administration, support, exceptions, and specialist coordination.

Now ask whether the buyers are reachable.

The model uses 250 named brands. Nineteen paid diagnostics would mean a 7.6 percent share if every project came from a different brand.

The target, owner hours, reachable set, required count, and share are all fixture assumptions. We don't know whether 504 non-delivery hours are enough. We don't know whether the operator can reach those buyers. And we don't know whether any of them will pay the modeled price.

The arithmetic reconciles. The business doesn't become proven because the spreadsheet stopped arguing with itself.

## S10: know what breaks

Claim IDs: C002, C005, C007, C008

### Narration

There are five early ways this company fails.

First, the buyer sees ordinary SEO work and won't pay separately.

Second, the sample changes so much that a repeat can't support a responsible comparison.

Third, a tool gives the buyer the full decision without you. If your interpretation adds nothing, adding a report and a meeting doesn't create a company.

Fourth, the buyer only cares when you promise placement, traffic, leads, or revenue. Walk away. You don't control those outcomes.

Fifth, the scope keeps growing until one report quietly includes research, content, public relations, legal review, and software support.

That's not a business of one. It's a team wearing one invoice.

If actual hours break the model, narrow the scope, change the price, or stop. If every engagement needs a specialist bench, choose a narrower segment or admit the entry model needs a team.

Those aren't disclaimers. They're operating decisions, which brings us to what you build first.

## S11: the first 30 and 90 days

Claim IDs: C008

### Narration

In the first 30 days, build the question template, evidence registry, delivery boundary, three-question screen, and first prospect list.

Talk to five marketing or organic-growth leaders. Ask what decision this evidence could change and who owns that decision.

With one consenting brand, run the manual ten-question diagnostic across two answer surfaces. Track every hour. Deliver the evidence record and classifications. Then make one paid ask for the same bounded scope.

Continue only if buyers can name a consequential decision, at least one accepts paid work or gives a specific budget-backed condition, and you can deliver responsibly inside the 24-hour boundary.

The next 60 days are funded proof.

Deliver the paid work if the first gate passes. Record the real sales time, delivery time, tool cost, specialist cost, support load, and buyer response.

Repeat the sample only when the buyer has another decision to make. Add monitoring only if that second look changes something useful. Add another buyer only when the first scope repeats without hiding your time.

That is construction. You're building the assets, making the offer, delivering the work, and replacing assumptions with paid evidence.

## S12: the report can stay green

Claim IDs: C007, C008, C010

### Narration

Go back to the marketing lead at the beginning.

The search report can stay green. It wasn't necessarily wrong. It was watching one doorway while the customer used another.

The company worth building sits between that missing view and the decision the buyer has to make.

It records the agreed answer sample. It checks the important facts and sources. It explains what the buyer controls, what the buyer may influence, and what should be left alone.

That responsibility is larger than one diagnostic and focused enough for one experienced operator to enter.

So build the AI visibility company. Start with one paid diagnostic. Prove that the evidence changes a real decision and that the work fits the owner. Earn every responsibility after that.

The old report can keep watching the first doorway. Build the company that helps the buyer decide what the second one deserves.
