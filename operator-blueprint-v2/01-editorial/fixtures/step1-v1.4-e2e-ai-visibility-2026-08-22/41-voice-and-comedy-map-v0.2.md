## Company language lock

Public title: AI Visibility

Spoken name: AI visibility company

Plain definition: Helps brands understand how they appear in AI answers and decide what deserves action.

Internal description: AI visibility-intelligence function

Category status: proposed editorial label

First spoken use:

> We're building an AI visibility company. It helps brands understand how they appear in AI answers and decide what deserves action.

Prohibited first-listen substitutions: AI visibility-intelligence company, discovery intelligence agency, generative search optimization function, or any stack of abstract labels.

## Governing performance relationship

Base role: a seasoned operator, career advisor, and business strategist giving another professional a company blueprint they can evaluate and act on.

Internal performance shorthand: Nate Bargatze doing 60 Minutes.

That shorthand means:

- Reporting bones: the facts and causal chain have been investigated and can survive inspection.
- Ordinary surface: the narrator uses familiar words and sounds like a person talking across a table.
- Patient timing: let the listener catch up before the next abstraction or number.
- Dry observation: expose the absurdity in a system without mugging for a joke.
- Conviction: take a position once the evidence earns it.
- Practical payoff: every major section brings the viewer closer to building, testing, refusing, or stopping.

It does not mean an imitation, a comedy routine, or a neutral correspondent reading a report.

Viewer: experienced search, content, digital-strategy, or intelligence professional.

Why the narrator can advise: the episode gives away the buyer decision, evidence boundary, market-selection method, delivery loop, economics, refusal rules, and construction test instead of selling a tool list.

Opening temperature: curious and mildly bemused.

Middle temperature: patient, useful, and increasingly decisive.

Ending temperature: direct and commercially sober. BUILD is a recommendation, not a dare or promise.

## Conviction boundary

Be confident about:

- The episode will show a company worth building and testing.
- The company sells judgment about evidence, not control of an answer.
- The operator should begin where they have earned context or buyer access.
- The first purchase is one paid diagnostic.
- No-action can be a valid paid result.
- Outcome promises, hidden teams, and empty monitoring should be refused.

Remain uncertain about:

- Existing demand for this exact service.
- Accepted price and conversion.
- Actual acquisition, delivery, support, and specialist hours.
- Repeat value, sustainable economics, and owner income.

Do not let commercial uncertainty weaken the episode promise. The correct distinction is `build this bounded test` versus `we know the business will work`, not `perhaps there is a company` versus `perhaps there is not`.

## Opening voice map

Cold-open details: steady traffic, green report, customer asks AI, company missing or stale, report still green.

Notice without explaining: two views of the same customer decision can disagree because they watch different paths.

Question: which part of the customer's decision can the report not see?

Verdict withheld: no category or operator instruction before the identity break.

Identity and promise: exact brand string, short company name, plain definition, market-selection lesson, and first-proof promise. Do not use `Today, we're...`.

Context runway: explain the old search chain in ordinary language, defend it, add bounded survey evidence, then source variation.

Earned reveal: products can gather evidence, but the decision about what deserves action still needs an owner.

## Evidence voice

The narrator interprets evidence. The screen and show notes cite it.

Preferred pattern:

> McKinsey found that roughly half the people it surveyed were deliberately using AI-assisted search. Regular search did not disappear. Customers had added another doorway.

On-screen receipt at the same moment:

> McKinsey | U.S. consumer survey | Aug. 2025 | n=1,927 | 50% | C001

Description or show notes: full report title, direct link, publication date, survey date, population, sample, pages, methodology note, access date, and limitation.

Do not make the voiceover say:

> In an August 2025 survey of 1,927 U.S. consumers, McKinsey reported...

That sentence is accurate but sounds like a student presenting a source card. Lead with the finding and let the receipt carry the filing details.

Rules:

- Keep the source name audible when it lends necessary credibility.
- Keep the population limit audible in familiar language, such as `the people it surveyed`.
- Speak an exact non-money figure only when the precision changes the conclusion.
- Keep money, forecasts, constructed examples, and material outcome limits audible.
- Do not repeat the same source introduction when the next finding comes from the same report and passage.
- Never depend on an on-screen label to legalize an overbroad spoken claim.

## Market instruction

Travel is the recurring example because the green-report scene makes the problem easy to picture and the approved fixture economics use a 250-brand travel set.

The viewer instruction is different:

> Start with a field where you have earned experience, adjacent knowledge, or a real path to the buyer. You should understand the decision, speak the buyer's language, and be able to name companies you can actually reach.

Only after that rule is clear may the narration say that travel is the example used to run the test.

Prohibited implication: everybody should begin with consumer travel because the episode did.

## Reporting and operator plan

Carrier: marketing lead and green report.

Business mechanism: question set, answer sample, source and fact review, what can be changed, recommendation, and optional repeat.

Hidden consequence: a dashboard can record activity without giving the buyer the decision they need.

Portable rule: collection is not ownership. The company begins where someone becomes accountable for the decision.

Operator blueprint: choose a field through experience and access, sell the fixed diagnostic, accept no-action as valid, measure every hour, refuse outcome promises, and add monitoring only for a continuing decision duty.

## Thesis language

Opportunity thesis:

> The second doorway can be measured. The decision behind it still needs an owner.

Operator thesis:

> The advantage is not the software account. It is knowing which question matters, which source deserves trust, and when the evidence is too weak to act on.

Entry-wedge thesis:

> The first purchase is one evidence-and-decision diagnostic, not a promise to improve the answer.

Governing question:

> What should one operator own between the evidence and the buyer's next move, and how do you turn that responsibility into a company?

BUILD verdict:

> Build the AI visibility company. Start with one paid diagnostic in a market you understand and can reach. Earn every responsibility after it.

Prohibited central question: whether the missing view creates enough responsibility to build a company.

## Governing analogy

Family: two doorways.

Literal explanation first: conventional search reports one path while an agreed AI-answer sample may show another.

| Mapping | Analogy | Business meaning |
|---|---|---|
| What moves | customer through a doorway | research behavior and evidence |
| What blocks view | report facing one door | measurement boundary |
| Who owns consequence | marketing lead inside the room | buyer decision |
| What changes | view of the second door | evidence plus accountable recommendation |

First use: after the conventional search chain and source variation are clear.

Development: the diagnostic does not build or control the second doorway. It gives the buyer a recorded view through it.

Ending: the green report keeps watching the first door while the company helps the buyer decide what the second deserves.

Excluded metaphor families: radar, plumbing, flight deck, map, or immune system. Do not crowd the governing image.

## Understatement and humor

Default: quiet recognition, not outrage.

Systemic target: dashboards or reports that turn collection activity into a claimed result without owning the decision.

Fair explanation first: current products and search reports perform useful jobs inside their scope.

Planned decodes:

1. After explaining no-action: if every diagnostic recommends more work, the operator has made a sales pitch with a clipboard.
2. After the economics: revenue on a slide does not deliver the client work.
3. At the capacity boundary: when one report quietly becomes research, content, public relations, legal review, and software support, that is not a company of one. It is a team wearing one invoice.

Each decode must clarify a mechanism already understood. Cut it if it becomes a detachable joke.

## Manav language map

Direct address: speak to one operator as `you`.

Ask and answer:

> So what is the buyer paying for? The decision.

Negate and replace, only after the wrong frame is understood:

> You are not selling control of the answer. You are selling a better decision about it.

Long thought: allow economics and responsibility explanations to run with natural connectors when the narrator is working something out.

Short ruling:

> If the evidence changes nothing, do not sell more work.

Analogy cadence: use an ordinary comparison at a difficult handoff, hidden cost, or absurd failure mode. Do not add an analogy merely because a scene lacks one.

Personal marker rule:

- `Okay`, `right`, `here's the thing`, and similar markers are not quotas or scene-open labels.
- The full script may use `Okay` once when it marks a genuine turn in thought. Zero is acceptable.
- Remove it if the scene still begins coherently without it.

## Causal transition voice

A transition is not a phrase. It is a consequence.

Useful connectors include `so`, `because`, `which means`, `that gives us`, `but`, and `and then`, only when the rest of the sentence carries a specific inheritance.

Examples of the required logic:

- The second doorway creates the opening, **but** an opening is not a company. Identify the missing owner.
- The components stop at evidence, **which means** the company begins with the buyer decision.
- That company runs on judgment, **so** the operator's experience matters.
- Experience creates trust, **but** the first buyer needs a piece they can inspect.
- The offer is buyable, **so** choose a market where you can reach the buyer.
- Buyer access is not capacity, **which means** the model has to carry its own weight.
- The spreadsheet cannot expose every failure, **so** name what breaks in delivery.
- Those failures become the 30 and 90-day test.

Prohibited transitions: `Now let's move to`, `Next`, `The next section`, a bare `Okay`, or a recap that does not create a new consequence.

## Cadence plan

Opening: medium sentences with one short unresolved observation.

Context: patient explanation, one conversational source, then a breath.

Opportunity: alternate evidence, plain implication, and the recurring buyer. Do not sound like a report summary.

Turn: one practical question and answer.

Build: longer causal explanations broken by earned refusal rules.

Market selection: principle first, travel example second.

Economics: one case at a time, with a plain restatement after each. Say `planning model`, not `fixture`.

Ending: return to the opening and land direct construction advice without a disclaimer spiral.

Formal language to rewrite: `represents an expanding divergence`, `decision-intelligence layer`, `commercially actionable evidence architecture`, `completed evidence record`, and any sentence a smart operator would understand but never say to a colleague.

## Acceptance tests for v0.3

The script cannot receive a voice recommendation from mechanical marker checks alone.

1. **Owner cold read:** the owner reads or listens once without annotations. Any sustained `memo being read aloud` reaction is a fail and overrides every automated or role recommendation.
2. **Evidence read:** C001 begins conversationally. Exact date and sample live in the receipt. The material population limit remains understandable in audio.
3. **Conviction read:** the episode never asks whether the promised company exists. BUILD remains decisive while commercial claims stay bounded.
4. **Market read:** a listener can repeat the experience, network, access, and buyer-decision rule before hearing travel as the example.
5. **Marker read:** no more than one purposeful `Okay`; no scene uses it as a chapter label.
6. **Seam read:** remove headings and read every last-two/first-two line pair. Each next scene must inherit a specific cause, question, image, or decision.
7. **Mouth test:** flag every phrase the narrator understands but would not say to a colleague. Rewrite before role review.
8. **Tap test:** sentence and paragraph lengths vary; evidence passages are not metronomic.
9. **Lexical hygiene:** zero em dashes, semicolons, unresolved planning jargon, banned hype, or LLM-watermark language in narration.

A reviewer must quote evidence for every pass. `The markers are present` is not evidence that the voice works.
