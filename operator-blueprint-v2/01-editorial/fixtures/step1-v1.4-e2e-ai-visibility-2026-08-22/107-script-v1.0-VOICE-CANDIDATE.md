## S03: the second doorway

Claim IDs: C001, C002

### Narration

To understand the opening, start with the thing that already works.

Someone types a question into regular search. A page shows up. They click it. Analytics catches the visit. Maybe they buy. Now the marketing team has a trail it can follow.

That system isn't perfect, but the marketing team knows what it's looking at.

McKinsey found that roughly half the people it surveyed were deliberately using AI-assisted search. Regular search didn't disappear. Some surveyed customers had simply added another place to do the same research.

And here's the detail that matters more. The answer doesn't have to come from the company's website.

It may use the company site. It may also use reviews, publishers, forums, and comparison pages. That mix can change with the question, the category, and the platform.

So now the reports are watching different parts of the same customer journey. The marketing team has a dashboard for one part and almost nothing for the other.

The old report can be completely honest and still miss the thing the buyer cares about. It's watching one doorway while the customer uses two.

That's the opening, but an opening isn't a company. A company needs a job someone will pay it to own.

So what is that job?

## S04: from evidence to responsibility

Claim IDs: C004, C005, C006, C007

### Narration

Start with what the tools can already do.

Products can track prompts, brand mentions, competitors, citations, and source patterns. That's useful, but collection alone doesn't tell you what a buyer will value or what result the service can produce.

We know the surface can be measured because researchers have done it in a benchmark. The catch is that the results changed from one domain to another. So you can study the pattern, but you still can't predict what will happen for a client.

In other words, you can collect a sample, compare it, and watch it change. What the measurement doesn't do is tell the buyer what any of it means.

And this kind of purchase isn't coming out of nowhere. SEO firms already sell audits, competitive analysis, projects, and reports. That gives buyers a familiar way to buy this kind of work. It does not tell us they want this offer or will pay SEO prices for it.

Put yourself in the marketing lead's chair for a minute.

One report shows clicks. Another shows mentions. Another shows which sites were cited. The content team knows the pages it owns. The public-relations team knows the publishers it has worked with.

Nobody has to be doing a bad job. Everybody can be doing exactly what they were hired to do and still be answering a different question.

Someone still has to decide which facts matter, what can be changed, and when the evidence is too weak to justify more activity.

The dashboard doesn't own that responsibility. A person does.

And that's the responsibility this company would own.

## S05: the company before the offer

Claim IDs: C007

### Narration

Okay, so now we can name the company.

And I don't mean the first report. I mean the full thing you're actually trying to build.

The AI visibility company works with a small group of brands. It keeps an agreed list of questions, records what each sampled answer says and which sources it uses, and checks important statements against facts the client has approved.

Then the operator tells the buyer what to change, what to investigate, what to watch, and what to leave alone.

You're not selling control of the answer. You're selling a better decision about it.

Over time, the value isn't a weekly score. It's a reliable record of what the agreed sample says about the brand, where an important fact has drifted, and whether anything is worth changing.

The buyer does not need a taller pile of screenshots. They need to know where attention and money belong, including when the right move is to do nothing.

And monitoring only belongs here when another look helps with another decision. A new month on the calendar isn't a client result.

AI helps collect and compare the material. Software keeps the record. The buyer approves the client facts. The operator stays accountable for the sample, the source judgment, the exceptions, and the final recommendation.

If the work crosses into expertise the operator shouldn't pretend to have, a specialist steps in.

This is a judgment business, which is why your experience matters more than the software account.

## S07: the first purchase

Claim IDs: C002, C005, C007, C008

### Narration

Here's the first test model. These are assumptions to test, not buyer behavior or results.

Don't ask the first buyer to hand you the whole function. Start with one brand, one market, ten questions the buyer approves before collection, and two AI answer surfaces.

What are they buying?

Not a prettier dashboard. They're buying a record of what appeared and a decision about each important gap.

Before you collect anything, ask what decision may change. If the buyer can't name one, stop. A strange answer isn't automatically a paid problem.

Agree on the questions. Record each answer, the date, important claims, competitors, and sources.

Then check the important statements against facts the client has approved. Some differences won't matter. A weak source needs a narrower conclusion.

Sort each gap into what the client can change, influence, watch, or leave alone because the evidence is too uncertain.

For every important gap, give the buyer the record and one of four recommendations: act, investigate, monitor, or leave it alone. And leave it alone has to be a real answer.

The job ends when that record is clear enough for the buyer to use.

If every report somehow ends with more work for the person who wrote it, the buyer isn't getting independent judgment. They're getting the next sales call.

Implementation and monitoring stay separate. Neither slips into the first invoice because it looks good in the revenue model.

That's the first offer. Now who can you actually sell it to?

## S08: choose the market you can enter

Claim IDs: C004, C007, C008, C009

### Narration

Start with a market where you have a reason to be in the room.

You understand the buyer's decision. You speak the language. And you have a credible path to the person responsible.

Don't pick a market because AI made it look interesting this week.

Travel is our example because the customer journey is clear to follow. It is not the market recommendation.

For this example, the planning model starts with 25 brands in one narrow segment.

Before you ask for a meeting, prepare a three-question public preview. Show what appeared, where it came from, and where the sample stops.

Then ask the buyer one question: would this change anything your team is responsible for?

If the answer is no, record why and move on. No drama, you learned that the evidence did not create a buying decision.

If the answer is yes, ask what decision changes and who owns it. Then offer the complete paid diagnostic.

That gives you a possible buyer. It still does not tell you whether the work fits inside one person's week.

## S09: make the numbers work aloud

Claim IDs: C008

### Narration

A possible buyer is one thing. Fitting the work inside one person's week is the next problem.

So put money around the test and remember that you're the one doing the work. Everything here is a model. These are assumptions, not results or an earnings forecast.

Start with validation: say you charge two grand and cap delivery at 16 hours. Set aside 960 dollars for your time, 99 for software, and 300 for finding the client and the other costs included in this model.

That leaves 641 dollars before tax and unlisted costs, but the first decision is whether you can finish the job in 16 hours. If you can't, the scope or the price has to change.

Then take the working case: three grand and 24 hours. Set aside 1,440 dollars for your time and 399 in listed costs. That leaves 1,161 dollars before tax and unlisted costs.

This is the case the annual decision uses. It fails if buyers won't pay three grand or the work won't stay inside 24 hours. So how many jobs does the model need?

The target is 48,000 dollars a year for the owner's labor and support, before tax and unlisted costs.

Each three-grand project leaves 2,601 dollars for that labor and support after the listed costs, before tax and unlisted costs.

Eighteen projects fall short. Nineteen gets past the target.

So the model requires 19 paid diagnostics. That is a requirement, not a forecast.

Can one person deliver 19?

At 24 hours each, that's 456 delivery hours.

The model gives you 960 hours for the year. That leaves 504 for selling, administration, support, exceptions, and specialist help. We don't know whether all of that fits.

Then comes reach. Nineteen different buyers out of 250 travel brands is 7.6 percent. That's the hurdle for this example list. It is not a conversion forecast or the size of the market.

And this is where I want to be clear. The arithmetic fits. We still don't know whether you can reach those buyers, sell at that price, keep delivery inside 24 hours, and handle everything outside delivery in the time that's left.

The model can't answer that for you. You have to go sell and deliver the thing.

## S10: know what breaks

Claim IDs: C002, C005, C007, C008

### Narration

And that is how a model that works can still become a bad business.

Maybe the buyer sees ordinary SEO work and won't pay separately. Maybe the sample moves too much to support a responsible comparison. Or maybe a tool gives the buyer the whole decision.

If your judgment adds nothing, stop. A report and a meeting do not create a company.

Walk away from any buyer who needs a promise of placement, traffic, leads, or revenue. You don't control those outcomes.

And be honest about the scope, because one report can quietly become research, content, public relations, legal review, and software support.

That's not a business of one. It's a team wearing one invoice.

If the hours break the model, narrow the scope, change the price, or stop. If every job needs several specialists, choose a narrower market or admit the model needs a team.

And another month on the calendar doesn't earn a monitoring fee. Another decision does.

Every one of those failures becomes a question for the first 30 days.

## S11: the first 30 and 90 days

Claim IDs: C008

### Narration

Those failures are the questions the first 30 days have to answer, so test them. Don't start by proving you can build a nice website.

In the first 30 days, make the question template, a clear record, a firm scope, a three-question preview, and the first prospect list.

Talk to five marketing or organic-growth leaders. Ask what decision the evidence could change and who owns it.

Use the preview to earn the conversation. When a buyer names a consequential decision, ask them to pay for the ten-question diagnostic across two AI answer surfaces.

Ask first. Deliver second.

If one accepts, run the paid work.

If nobody accepts but one buyer names a budget and tells you what would have to be true for approval, run one manual test with a willing brand. That still isn't paid demand.

And before you keep going, three things have to be true.

At least three of the five buyers independently describe the problem as consequential.

At least one accepts a paid diagnostic, or names the budget and what would have to be true for approval.

And the manual delivery produces repeatable evidence within 24 hours of work.

That's the gate. If it doesn't pass, you stop or change the offer.

If it does pass, the next 60 days are funded work only.

Record the time, costs, specialist help, support, and buyer response. Then repeat the work for another buyer decision.

Add monitoring only when a second look changes something. Add buyers only when the scope repeats without hiding your time.

That's construction. You're making the assets, asking for the sale, doing the work, and replacing assumptions with what actually happened.

Now we can go back to the green report.
