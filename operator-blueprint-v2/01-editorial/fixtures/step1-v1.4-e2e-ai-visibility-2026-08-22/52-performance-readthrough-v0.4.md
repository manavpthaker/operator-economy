## S00: the green report

### Narration

You run marketing for a travel company.

Search traffic is steady. Sales isn't sounding an alarm. The dashboard is green, which normally means everybody can get on with their day.

Then a customer asks an AI tool where to stay.

Your company doesn't appear.

Or it does, but the description sounds like it came from a page nobody at the company remembers approving.

The dashboard is still green.

So what part of the customer's decision can that report not see?

## S03: the second doorway

### Narration

To see the opening, start with the report that's already green.

A customer types a question into search. A page appears, they click it, and the visit shows up in analytics. Maybe they buy something. Now the marketing team has a trail it can follow.

That system isn't perfect, but it's familiar, and it still does the job it was built to do.

The customer has simply added another path.

McKinsey found that roughly half the people it surveyed were deliberately using AI-assisted search. Regular search didn't disappear. Some surveyed customers added another doorway while they research.

And what appears inside that doorway can come from a different mix of places. The company's site may be one source. Reviews, publishers, forums, and comparison pages may be there too. That mix can change with the question, the category, and the platform.

So the reports may be watching different parts of the same customer journey. The marketing team has a dashboard for one part and almost nothing for the other.

That doesn't make the old report wrong. It's watching one doorway while the customer is using two.

So this isn't a story about regular search dying. The missing view is the opening, but an opening isn't a company.

What should one operator own between the answer a customer sees and the decision the buyer has to make?

## S04: from evidence to responsibility

### Narration

To answer that, separate what the tools can collect from the decision a buyer still needs.

Products already track prompts, brand mentions, competitors, citations, and source patterns. That shows collection is possible. It doesn't show what a buyer will value or what result the service can produce.

Researchers measured AI visibility in a benchmark, and the effect changed from one domain to another. So the surface can be studied. The benchmark can't predict a client's result.

So yes, you can collect a sample. You can compare it, and you can watch it change.

But here's the problem. A new measurement doesn't appoint anyone to decide what it means.

And the buyer isn't starting from zero. SEO firms already sell audits, competitive analysis, projects, and reports. That gives buyers a familiar purchase shape. It doesn't show that buyers want this offer or will pay SEO prices for it.

Put yourself back in the marketing lead's chair.

One report shows clicks. Another product shows mentions. Another view shows which sites were cited. The content team knows the pages it owns, and the public-relations team knows which publishers it has worked with.

Nobody has to be doing a bad job. They're just answering different questions.

The buyer still has to decide which facts matter, what can be changed, and when the evidence is too weak to justify more activity. That responsibility doesn't belong to the dashboard. It belongs to a person.

That is the responsibility this company would own, and it is where the company begins.

## S05: the company before the offer

### Narration

That responsibility tells us what the full company has to look like. Not the first report. The thing you're actually trying to build.

The AI visibility company works with a small group of brands. It keeps an agreed list of questions, records what each sampled answer says and which sources it uses, and checks important statements against facts the client has approved.

Then the operator tells the buyer what to change, what to investigate, what to watch, and what to leave alone.

You aren't selling control of the answer. You're selling a better decision about it.

Over time, the value isn't a weekly ranking score. It's a reliable record of what the agreed sample says about the brand, where important facts have drifted, and whether anything is worth changing.

The point isn't to collect a taller pile of screenshots. The point is to help the buyer spend attention and money on the right problem, including when the right move is to do nothing.

Monitoring only belongs in the company when another look helps with another decision. A new month on the calendar isn't a client result.

AI helps collect and compare the material. Software keeps the record. The buyer approves the client facts. The operator stays accountable for the sample, the source judgment, the exceptions, and the final recommendation. A specialist steps in when the work crosses into expertise the operator shouldn't pretend to have.

The operator owns the judgment. Which is why the operator's experience matters more than the software account.

## S06: why your experience matters

### Narration

The judgment has to come from somewhere. So where should that experience come from?

If you've worked in search, content, digital strategy, or intelligence, you know a result can be interesting and useless. A source can look authoritative and answer the wrong question. And the buyer's decision matters more than the amount of data in the report.

AI can gather and compare the evidence. The operator still owns which question matters, which client fact settles a dispute, and when the honest recommendation is to do nothing.

Use that judgment in a field where you've earned context or a path to the buyer. That may come from industry experience, adjacent client work, or a network that opens the conversation.

That's the advantage, not the software account. Now turn it into something the first buyer can inspect.

## S07: the first purchase

### Narration

Here is the first test model. These are assumptions to test, not buyer behavior or results.

Do not ask the first buyer to hand you the whole function. Start with one brand, one market, ten questions the buyer approves before collection, and two AI answer tools.

What are they buying? A record of what appeared, and a decision about each important gap.

Before collecting, ask what decision may change. If the buyer can't name one, stop. A strange answer isn't automatically a paid problem.

Agree on the questions. Record each answer, its date, important claims, competitors, and sources.

Check important statements against client-approved facts. Some differences won't matter. A weak source needs a narrower conclusion.

Sort each gap into what the client can change, influence, watch, or leave alone because it is too uncertain.

The buyer gets the record and four calls: act, investigate, monitor, or leave it alone. The job ends when the buyer accepts both.

No action is valid. It proves the buyer paid for judgment, not a disguised sale.

If every report recommends more work from its author, that isn't a decision service. It's a sales pitch with a clipboard.

Implementation and monitoring are separate. Neither sneaks into the first invoice because it looks good in a revenue model.

Now you have a first offer. It still needs a buyer you can reach.

## S08: choose the market you can enter

### Narration

Start where you already have a reason to be useful. Understand the buyer's decision, speak the language, and have a credible path to the person responsible.

Travel is the example here because it makes the customer journey clear. It isn't the market recommendation.

For that example, the planning model starts with 25 brands in one narrow segment. Before asking for a meeting, prepare a three-question public preview. Show what appeared, where it came from, and where the sample stops.

Then ask the buyer one question: would this change anything your team is responsible for?

If the answer is no, record why and move on. Don't announce that the brand vanished into the machine.

If the answer is yes, ask what decision changes and who owns it. Then offer the complete paid diagnostic.

Demand needs a buyer. A product launch and a busy category can't stand in for one. Access still doesn't prove one operator can carry the work.

## S09: make the numbers work aloud

### Narration

Now put money around the test model. These are assumptions, not results or an earnings forecast.

Three decisions: validation, the working case, and the annual requirement.

First, validation. Two grand, with delivery capped at 16 hours. Set aside 960 dollars for the owner's time, 99 for software, and 300 for finding the client and counted overhead. That leaves 641 dollars before tax and unlisted costs.

If the test won't fit into 16 hours, the scope or price has to change.

Next, the working case. Three grand and 24 hours. After 1,440 dollars for the owner's time and 399 in listed costs, 1,161 dollars remains before tax and unlisted costs.

The annual decision uses this case. It fails if buyers won't pay or the job exceeds 24 hours.

Revenue on a slide is very cooperative. Client work is usually less polite.

Now the annual decision. Suppose the business needs to leave 48,000 dollars for the owner's labor and support, before tax and unlisted costs. Each three-grand project has 2,601 dollars for that job after 99 for software and 300 for finding the client and counted overhead, before tax and unlisted costs. Divide and round up. The model needs 19 paid diagnostics.

That makes 19 the annual sales requirement, not a forecast.

Can one person deliver 19?

At 24 hours each, 19 diagnostics use 456 of the operator's 960 annual hours. That leaves 504 for selling, administration, support, exceptions, and specialist help. The ceiling of 24 diagnostics would use 576 delivery hours.

The calendar fits only if everything else fits into those 504 hours. We don't know that yet.

Then comes reach. Nineteen different buyers out of 250 travel brands is 7.6 percent. That's the hurdle for this example list, not a conversion forecast or the size of the market.

We don't know whether the operator reaches those buyers, fits the rest of the job into 504 hours, or sells at the modeled price.

The arithmetic works. The business doesn't become proven because the spreadsheet stopped arguing with itself.

## S10: know what breaks

### Narration

The spreadsheet can fit and the company can still fail.

Maybe the buyer sees ordinary SEO work and won't pay separately. Maybe the sample moves too much to support a responsible comparison. Or maybe a tool gives the buyer the whole decision. If your judgment adds nothing, a report and a meeting don't create a company.

Walk away from any buyer who needs a promise of placement, traffic, leads, or revenue. You don't control those outcomes.

Watch the scope too. One report can quietly become research, content, public relations, legal review, and software support. That's not a business of one. It's a team wearing one invoice.

If the hours break the model, narrow the scope, change the price, or stop. If every job needs several specialists, choose a narrower market or admit the model needs a team. Another month on the calendar doesn't earn a monitoring fee.

Each failure tells you what the first 30 days have to test.

## S11: the first 30 and 90 days

### Narration

Build the test around those failures.

In the first 30 days, make the question template, a clear record, a firm scope, a three-question preview, and the first prospect list.

Talk to five marketing or organic-growth leaders. Ask what decision the evidence could change and who owns it.

Use the preview to earn a conversation. When a buyer names a consequential decision, ask them to pay for the ten-question diagnostic across two AI answer tools. Ask first. Deliver second.

If one accepts, run the paid work. If nobody accepts but one gives a specific budget-backed purchase condition, run one manual test with a willing brand. That still isn't paid demand.

Continue only if at least three of the five buyers independently describe the problem as consequential, at least one accepts a paid diagnostic or gives a specific budget-backed purchase condition, and the manual delivery produces repeatable evidence within 24 hours of work.

The next 60 days are where the assumptions meet a client.

If that gate passes, deliver funded work only. Record the time, costs, specialist help, support, and buyer response.

Repeat for another buyer decision. Add monitoring only when a second look changes something. Add buyers only when the scope repeats without hiding your time.

That is construction. You're building the assets, making the offer, doing the work, and replacing assumptions with what actually happened in paid work.

Which brings us back to the green report.

## S12: the report can stay green

### Narration

The search report can stay green. It wasn't necessarily wrong. It was watching one doorway while the customer used another.

The company worth building sits between that missing view and the decision the buyer has to make.

It records the agreed sample, checks the important facts and sources, and explains what the buyer controls, what it may influence, and what should be left alone.

The diagnostic is the first sale. The company is the repeatable work of turning each agreed sample into a decision the buyer can use.

Build the AI visibility company. Start with one paid diagnostic in a market you understand and can reach. Earn every larger responsibility after it.
