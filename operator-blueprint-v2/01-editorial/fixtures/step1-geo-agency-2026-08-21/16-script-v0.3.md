## S01: Search looks fine. The answer doesn't.

### Narration

Okay, imagine you run marketing for a travel brand.

Search looks fine. Your pages rank. Traffic looks normal. Everybody thinks discovery is covered.

Then a customer asks an AI assistant which travel company fits their needs.

Your brand isn't there.

Or it is there, but the description's old. Or the answer leans on a comparison page nobody on your team has ever reviewed.

Now, that travel brand is hypothetical. I'm not telling you this happened to one specific company.

But the shift underneath it is real.

McKinsey surveyed 1,927 U.S. consumers in August 2025.

Half said they intentionally used AI-powered search.

Gartner separately reported changes in how surveyed consumers research products.

Does that prove your travel brand has a problem? No.

Does it prove somebody will pay you to solve it? Also no.

What it does establish is a discovery surface that normal reporting doesn't fully show.

And that's the opening.

Not an agency promising rankings. A diagnostic that helps a buyer decide what, if anything, is worth fixing.

## S02: You're not measuring one permanent ranking

### Narration

Here's the thing. Traditional search reporting tells you where a page ranks, which query sent the traffic, and what happened after the click.

Useful information.

But an AI answer may pull from the brand's site, publishers, review sites, forums, and comparison pages.

McKinsey's source analysis found that the mix can change by model and category. Academic GEO research also suggests visibility can be measured, with results varying by domain.

So what are you recording?

Not one permanent ranking. You're recording a sample.

For our hypothetical travel brand, the question isn't, “How do we control the answer?”

You probably can't.

The useful question is, “What did the answer say, where did it get that information, and does any of this give the buyer a responsible decision?”

Some gaps are yours to fix. Some can only be influenced. Some are outside the brand's control.

That's not a disclaimer attached to the service.

That's the service boundary.

## S03: The buyer is purchasing a decision

### Narration

So what does the buyer actually purchase?

The neighboring SEO market already sells audits, competitive analysis, projects, and recurring reporting.

Current AI-visibility products can collect prompts, mentions, competitors, citations, and source patterns.

You're combining those pieces into one narrow diagnostic.

One brand. One market. Ten questions the buyer approves. Two answer surfaces.

And the buyer gets three things.

First, a recorded baseline of what those answers actually said.

Second, a map of the sources and factual gaps shaping them.

Third, a decision. Correct this fact. Strengthen this owned source. Investigate this third-party gap. Or leave the result alone because the evidence doesn't justify action.

That's the product.

Evidence the buyer can use.

Not placement. Not traffic. Not leads. Not revenue.

## S04: Run the diagnostic in three passes

### Narration

All right. How do you deliver it?

Start with the decision.

Ask the marketing lead what they might do differently after seeing the evidence. If they can't answer that, stop. An interesting screenshot is not a business.

Then run three passes.

Pass one, capture the answer.

Agree on the questions. Record the answer surface, date, response, brand presence, and competitors.

Pass two, trace the sources.

Log the important citations. Compare the important statements with documents the client has approved as factual.

Pass three, classify the gap.

Is this an owned fact the brand can correct? A source relationship it may be able to influence? Or a result it should watch without pretending to control it?

Tools can collect and compare some of this.

But the operator still owns the research design, fact check, source judgment, permissions, and limits.

The tool gathers the pieces. The operator decides what they mean.

## S05: Test one reachable buyer segment

### Narration

Who do you test this with first?

Not every company that has a website.

Pick one segment where you can reach the buyer and digital discovery already matters to the category.

This model uses consumer travel brands as an example. The research does not prove travel is the best segment.

Build a list of twenty-five brands that already have someone responsible for SEO or content.

Prepare a three-question screen using public information. Make the sampling limits obvious.

Then ask one question: does this evidence change a real content, reputation, source, or measurement decision?

If yes, offer the full fixed-scope diagnostic.

If no, don't manufacture urgency by telling the buyer their brand is disappearing.

No changed decision, no useful diagnostic.

## S06: The math is supposed to feel fragile

### Narration

Okay. Let's talk about the math.

These are test assumptions. They are not observed service results.

Start with a two-thousand-dollar validation price and sixteen hours of delivery.

The model assigns nine hundred sixty dollars to labor, ninety-nine dollars to measurement software, and three hundred dollars to acquisition and listed overhead.

That leaves six hundred forty-one dollars before tax and before anything the model left out.

Now take the working case.

Two diagnostics at three thousand dollars each. Twenty-four delivery hours for each client.

After the labor allowance, software, and listed overhead, modeled contribution is two thousand three hundred twenty-two dollars.

Modeled scenario. Not observed performance. Not an earnings forecast.

And look, the number matters because it's fragile.

More delivery time, another tool, harder sales, refunds, specialist review, or a buyer demanding an outcome guarantee can wipe it out quickly.

The point isn't that this business prints money.

The point is to find out whether responsible delivery leaves a business at all.

## S07: Four things can kill the offer

### Narration

What kills it?

First, the buyer sees ordinary SEO and won't fund it separately.

Second, the same questions don't produce evidence you can repeat responsibly, so there is no trustworthy baseline.

Third, the tool already gives the buyer everything they need, which means your interpretation adds no value.

Fourth, the buyer only cares if you promise traffic or revenue. If that's the sale, the responsible version may be impossible to sell.

Platform behavior, terms, coverage, and pricing will keep moving. Comparative or regulated claims may need specialist review.

These aren't the warnings you read at the end.

They're the things that decide whether the business works.

## S08: Test the decision before you build the agency

### Narration

So don't start with a new tool stack and an agency website.

Talk to five marketing leaders in one segment you can actually reach.

With one consenting brand, run the manual ten-question baseline across two answer surfaces. Track every hour. Build the evidence package without selling optimization.

Then offer that same scope at a fixed validation price.

Continue only if three things happen.

Buyers can name the decision the evidence changes.

At least one accepts a paid diagnostic or gives you a specific budget-backed purchase condition.

And you can repeat the work responsibly within twenty-four hours.

Stop, redesign, or fold it into existing SEO work if any of this happens.

Buyers see no material value. The sample is unstable. The sale needs a guarantee. Or the price can't cover responsible delivery.

The first product isn't AI visibility optimization.

It's evidence about whether a useful service should exist at all.
