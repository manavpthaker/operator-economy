# Repaired Canvas: operations-reliability company

Status: revised Gate E3 candidate; fixture only

Overall evidence class: observed service-category components with a modeled entry offer and expansion path

## 1. Operator

An operations, implementation, product, RevOps, or systems generalist who can observe real work, map state, distinguish rules from judgment, configure fit-for-purpose tools, test exceptions, document the system, and communicate limits to a buyer.

Beginning responsibilities: buyer discovery, process observation, diagnostic decision, safe solution design, testing, documentation, and handoff.

Required boundaries: Refuse high-impact or regulated decisions without qualified review. Do not hold unnecessary client credentials or make material decisions through an unsupervised general-purpose agent.

Evidence status: `OBSERVED/PARALLEL` capabilities; exact operator fit remains testable.

## 2. Buyer and beneficiary

First segment: 10-100-person marketing or creative agencies reachable through the operator's existing network.

Economic buyer: Owner or operations lead responsible for client onboarding and delivery readiness.

Day-to-day users: Sales, operations, finance, and project-delivery staff.

Ultimate beneficiary: A newly signed client waiting for a confident start.

Buyer trigger: A signed proposal enters a fragmented onboarding process with unclear next-step ownership.

Not a fit: Regulated or high-impact workflows, unstable rules, inaccessible systems, no measurable starting state, no named recovery owner, or a buyer seeking unsupervised AI judgment.

Evidence status: First segment and workflow are `MODELED`; buyer roles are supported by the approved professional-service territory.

## 3. Costly problem

First workflow: Signed proposal to acknowledged kickoff ownership.

Current failure: The accepted client state fragments across email, CRM, intake, calendar, project, and billing systems. Staff carry missing state manually and the client cannot see who owns the next action.

Primary consequence to measure: Time from signed proposal to acknowledged kickoff ownership.

Guardrails: Missing required information, duplicate actions, unavailable downstream systems, unowned exceptions, and recovery time.

Unknown: Frequency, baseline, and buyer cost must be measured during diagnosis. No savings or customer result is assumed.

## 4. First offer and build decision

### Offer A: paid reliability diagnostic

The buyer purchases observation, a current-state map, baseline, state and exception inventory, access and ownership map, and a build/no-build decision.

Valid outcomes: build, narrow, defer, or do not automate.

### Offer B: bounded reliability sprint

Offered separately only when the diagnostic confirms stable rules, safe access, understood exceptions, a named human recovery owner, a measurable baseline, and buyer acceptance criteria.

Includes: Minimum safe path, representative failure tests, logs, alerts, retry or stop behavior, runbook, access inventory, training, ownership transfer, and thirty days of bounded monitoring.

Excluded initially: Unbounded optimization, general AI decision authority, regulated processes, broad multi-workflow transformation, ongoing incident response, and custom software platform development.

Evidence status: `MODELED` offer assembled from observed and parallel service components.

## 5. Buyer result and acceptance

Diagnostic acceptance: Buyer receives an accurate map and a decision they can approve: build, narrow, defer, or no build.

Implementation acceptance: The selected handoff passes the agreed normal and failure tests; time to acknowledged kickoff ownership is recorded against the baseline; exceptions produce visible state and route to a named person; the buyer receives the runbook and access inventory.

The operator may influence process reliability. The operator does not guarantee savings, revenue, customer retention, or error elimination.

## 6. Delivery system

1. Obtain buyer consent and observe the people performing the current handoff.
2. Record inputs, states, decisions, systems, access, exceptions, consequences, and current ownership.
3. Establish the primary measure and guardrails.
4. Issue build, narrow, defer, or no-build decision.
5. If approved, implement only the stable path in client-controlled accounts where practical using least-privilege access and test data.
6. Test ordinary flow, missing information, duplicate action, and unavailable downstream system.
7. Add logs, alerts, retry or stop behavior, and a named human recovery route.
8. Train users, transfer runbook and access inventory, and monitor for thirty days.
9. Recommend maintain, expand, redesign, or retire.

Human-only decisions: Ambiguous approvals, material client commitments, regulated or high-impact decisions, exception adjudication outside preapproved rules, and changes to access or scope.

Manual fallback: Staff can continue the documented handoff and recovery process when automation is stopped or unavailable.

## 7. Capabilities and stack

| Capability | Required job | Tool class | Human responsibility | Ownership boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery | Observe actual work and state | Interview and mapping tools | Define reality before architecture | Buyer approves scope |
| Execution | Move stable state | Native integration, workflow engine, or bounded code | Select simplest safe fit | Client-controlled account where practical |
| Access | Limit credentials and data | Secrets and permission controls | Approve least privilege and revocation | Buyer owns credentials |
| Testing | Exercise acceptance and failure cases | Test records and controlled data | Define tests and stop conditions | No production customer data unless authorized |
| Observability | Expose state and incidents | Logs, alerts, error paths | Name recovery owner | Buyer receives access |
| Handoff | Return control | Runbook, training, inventory | Confirm support boundary | Buyer accepts ownership |

Named products are implementation choices, not the offer. No operator-owned platform is required for the first test.

## 8. Go-to-market path

1. Identify twenty-five small agencies reachable through the operator's network or direct professional relevance.
2. Interview ten owners or operations leads and require a real onboarding walk-through.
3. Map three handoffs without proposing architecture.
4. Reject unsafe, unstable, inaccessible, or unmeasurable cases.
5. Offer two paid diagnostics at a predeclared test price.
6. Offer one reliability sprint only if a diagnostic passes and the buyer accepts the modeled scope and price.
7. Track outreach, diagnostic acceptance, delivery time, exceptions, implementation decision, buyer acceptance, and support burden.

Continue only if buyers reveal a recurring consequential handoff, permit safe discovery, pay for the diagnostic decision, and at least one qualified workflow supports a separately purchased build.

## 9. Entry wedge and conditional expansion

Scope invariant: Make an important handoff observable, bounded, recoverable, and owned.

| Stage | Offer | Proof required before advancing | Stop condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry | Paid diagnostic for one handoff | Buyer pays for the decision and supplies a measurable process | No consequential or measurable problem |
| Implementation | One reliability sprint | Stable rules, safe access, accepted tests, named recovery owner | No-build decision or unbounded exceptions |
| Portfolio | Adjacent handoffs for the same buyer | First result accepted; repeated failure pattern; capacity remains viable | Every workflow is bespoke or support load breaks delivery |
| Managed reliability | Monitoring and recovery | Real incidents, funded response duty, service expectations, support economics | No recurring duty or buyer keeps responsibility |
| Productized hybrid | Reusable controls plus expert service | Repeated modules, stable interfaces, security posture, support economics, multi-client demand | Premature platform, unsafe custody, or insufficient repeatability |

The aspirational business remains an operations-reliability company. The first handoff is the learning and commercial wedge, not the ceiling.

## 10. Modeled economics and capacity

Every number below is a test assumption, not observed performance or an earnings forecast.

### Diagnostic

- Price: $750.
- Delivery time: 6 hours.
- Labor allowance: 6 × $60 = $360.
- Tools and administration: $50.
- Acquisition allocation: $100.
- Modeled contribution before owner compensation beyond the allowance and tax: $240.

### Reliability sprint

- Price: $3,000.
- Delivery time: 24 hours.
- Labor allowance: 24 × $60 = $1,440.
- Tools: $100.
- Acquisition and listed overhead: $300.
- Modeled contribution before owner compensation beyond the allowance and tax: $1,160.

### Capacity case

A modeled 60 monthly delivery-hour ceiling permits two six-hour diagnostics and two twenty-four-hour sprints. That mix produces $7,500 of modeled gross revenue and $2,800 of modeled contribution after the listed labor allowances and direct costs, before tax and unlisted costs.

Stress case: If each sprint takes 32 hours, the same mix requires 76 delivery hours and fails the 60-hour ceiling. The operator must reduce volume, narrow scope, increase price after buyer validation, or reject the model. The test may not hide this by omitting labor.

Most sensitive assumptions: Buyer willingness to pay for diagnosis, actual sprint hours, exception and support burden, and safe repeatability.

Omitted from the initial model: Managed-service revenue and product revenue. They require separate evidence and economics.

## 11. Risks, mitigations, and kill conditions

| Risk | Early warning | Mitigation | Kill condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| No costly repeatable handoff | Interviews stay abstract | Require real walk-throughs | No qualified workflow after fifteen targeted conversations |
| Unsafe judgment or data | Material decisions cannot be bounded | Keep human review; refuse high-impact scope | Qualified review cannot make delivery safe |
| Access and custody | Buyer will not provide bounded client-controlled access | Least privilege, revocation, inventory | Operator must hold unsafe credentials or infrastructure |
| Exception burden | Failures dominate monitoring | Narrow path and define stop rules | Fixed scope or capacity cannot survive |
| Buyer wants outcome guarantee | Purchase depends on savings or revenue promise | Sell process evidence and acceptance tests | Responsible offer cannot close |
| Vendor instability | Connectors, terms, or costs change | Manual fallback and change control | No viable safe delivery path |
| Premature recurring service | No funded incident duty exists | Transfer ownership after bounded monitoring | Retainer depends on invented responsibility |

## 12. First 30-day test

Question: Will a reachable agency buyer pay for a bounded diagnostic decision, and can one resulting handoff qualify for a safely scoped implementation?

Test: Ten buyer interviews, three observed handoff maps, two paid diagnostic offers, and one separately offered sprint only after a pass decision.

Success signal: At least one buyer pays for the complete diagnostic decision and either funds a qualified sprint or provides a specific budget-backed purchase condition.

Failure signal: No paid diagnostic, no consequential measurable handoff, unsafe access, unstable rules, unmanageable exceptions, or modeled support and delivery above the capacity ceiling.

Maximum modeled risk: Thirty operator hours and $300 of direct test spend before the day-30 decision.

Day-30 decision: Continue, narrow, revise, or stop. Do not build a shared platform.
