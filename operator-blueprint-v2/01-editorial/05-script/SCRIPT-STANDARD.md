# Script standard

Status: approved V2 Step 1 v1.5 authority

## Editorial register

The narrator sounds like Manav hosting The Operator Economy: a warm strategist giving away the working model to another capable professional, with investigative movement and dry skepticism when the evidence exposes a broken incentive. The voice is commercially literate, down to earth, analogy-led, direct, specific, and willing to take a position after earning it.

The episode may be ambitious about the opportunity. It must remain conservative about evidence, certainty, and financial outcomes.

Content OS remains the live authority for fact eligibility and the Operator Economy house voice. `studio/config/speech-profile.md` is the live observed source for Manav's personal spoken-language fingerprint. The episode-specific claims map controls how the approved Step 0 evidence may be stated.

Record the reviewed hashes of both voice sources in the script package. Apply every word-changing voice decision before lock. Step 2 owns vocal delivery and audio, not a rewrite pass.

## Apply the full voice architecture

The base persona is the operator-advisor. On top of it, apply the distinct functions in `VOICE-ARCHITECTURE.md`: reported story discipline, a plainspoken and mildly bemused human surface, business context and causal economics, a restrained reveal and callback, and Manav's own analogy, judgment, and conversational cadence.

These are high-level functions, not permission to copy the exact wording, jokes, mannerisms, vocal patterns, or recognizable routines of any named reference.

Before drafting the first full script, approve `04-narrative/EPISODE-INVESTMENT-THESIS.template.md`, `04-narrative/EPISODE-BEAT-SHEET.template.md`, the episode outline, and `04-narrative/VOICE-AND-COMEDY-MAP.template.md`. The script must implement those artifacts without treating any of them as a phrase quota.

## Sound like Manav without impersonating a checklist

Across the full script, naturally use several observed patterns where they make the business easier to understand:

- ask a practical question and answer it;
- reject the wrong frame, then replace it;
- stack short conclusions;
- address one viewer directly;
- pivot sections conversationally;
- preempt a real objection; and
- use one governing analogy family with meaningful callbacks;
- react with restraint when the operating system is genuinely absurd; and
- land a clear verdict after the evidence.

Do not insert a signature phrase merely to win a test. Reusing “Okay,” “here's the thing,” “right?”, “look,” or any other discourse marker to open multiple scenes is a voice failure, not a voice match. A marker may appear when it reflects a genuine turn in thought; it may not substitute for the causal handoff from the prior scene.

Spoken narration uses no em dashes or semicolons. Prefer contractions, familiar connectors, short declaratives, and concrete actions. Avoid formal transitions and report vocabulary defined in `EDITORIAL-VOICE-STANDARD.md`.

## Write for speech

- Use contractions and familiar words.
- Keep one main thought per sentence.
- Vary sentence length and rhythm.
- Use short sentences when a conclusion or distinction needs room.
- Let natural connectors such as “and,” “but,” and “so” do real work.
- Put a new thought or breath on a new line in the review copy.
- Explain a technical term when it first becomes necessary.
- Prefer concrete actions, objects, decisions, and consequences to abstractions.
- Read every revision aloud before approval.

The final script contains words, not acting markup. Step 2 may create a performance copy with pauses, emphasis, and approved pronunciation substitutions, but it must prove lexical conformity to the locked script.

## The opening is a ladder

The cold open should quickly establish:

1. One recognizable person, transaction, workflow, customer, or consequence.
2. What looks normal.
3. One strange, costly, or unresolved result.
4. The specific operator or business payoff that makes the episode worth watching.
5. A question or open loop the viewer wants answered.

The payoff teaser may preview the company the viewer could build, the responsibility they could own, or the practical decision they will learn to make. It must not explain the mechanism, claim proof, or deliver the final thesis before the case earns it. Do not begin with a greeting, category definition, abstract thesis, episode agenda, evidence dump, or request to subscribe. Do not require a number merely to satisfy a formula; use a number only when it makes the situation easier to understand.

After the cold open, include a distinct silent identity sting with no narration, then the fixed brand string:

> This is The Operator Economy, where we show you how to use AI to build, own, and operate a sustainable business of one.

Follow it with one episode-specific sentence using the approved short spoken company name and its one-sentence plain definition. The precise internal operating description may appear later when it improves understanding. It must not replace the short name or force the listener through a stack of abstract nouns.

The brand promise is a sustainable business of one, not merely an interesting service. The script must show what AI makes cheaper, faster, or newly possible; what judgment and accountability remain with the operator; how many customers or transactions the modeled livelihood case requires; and why that reachable share could be enough without presenting income as an assured outcome.

Then give the viewer a context runway: how the normal system works, why people use it, what changed, who is affected, and why the question matters. Ask the central investigation question after the viewer has the vocabulary to understand it. Deliver the main thesis only after the context and evidence earn it.

Write the cold open after the body is structurally sound. It creates tension and previews the practical destination; the later brand and episode string sharpen the promise without repeating an agenda.

## Investment structure and company naming

The approved Episode Investment Thesis controls five distinct layers:

1. **Show thesis:** why a capable professional could use AI to build, own, and operate a sustainable business of one around a narrow share of a large, costly problem.
2. **Opportunity thesis:** what changed, why the problem matters, and which complete company should exist.
3. **Operator thesis:** why this viewer's experience, access, relationships, or judgment creates a credible advantage.
4. **Entry-wedge thesis:** the first bounded purchase through which the operator begins building the larger company.
5. **BUILD verdict:** the evidence-safe recommendation to begin constructing the company under explicit proof and stop conditions.

The operator thesis also controls where the viewer should begin. Teach the viewer to start from at least one earned advantage: relevant experience, adjacent work, a trusted relationship, or a real network. Then require enough buyer-language fluency to understand the problem and decision-makers the operator can realistically reach. Do not prescribe the episode's illustrative or fixture industry as the universal entry market. When one industry carries the explanation, state that it is the example unless the approved thesis explicitly makes it the recommendation.

Do not let the first audit, diagnostic, sprint, or implementation become the episode thesis merely because it is easier to describe. Establish the mature company first. Then bring it down to the first purchase a buyer can inspect and trust.

The public category title and spoken company name should usually use two or three familiar words. Follow the spoken name with one plain sentence explaining the buyer and owned job. Keep the precise operating description in editorial metadata or introduce it later when the listener has enough context. A proposed public label is an editorial name, not evidence that buyers recognize an established category or already purchase the exact model.

The episode targets 55 percent of its spoken words for the opportunity pitch and 45 percent for the operator blueprint. An opportunity share from 52 to 58 percent passes without an exception. The opportunity act earns belief through the normal system, market change, costly problem, established budget or responsibility, comparable-business ladder, evidence limits, qualified buyer, missing ownership, and complete company. The build act covers operator advantage, AI and human responsibility, the wedge, delivery, go to market, modeled economics, required sales activity, the hard part, conditional expansion, and the first 30-day and 90-day construction path.

For a horizontal opportunity, define the qualified buyer before introducing examples. Use no more than three short cases, each clarifying a different customer decision. The company must remain understandable with every industry name removed. An example may not control the cold open, company definition, total market, prospect denominator, or economics unless the approved thesis selects it as the market.

Market scale is a structured argument. Establish the underlying costly behavior, adjacent established budget, at least two observed operating layers, and the proposed operator synthesis. Do not use funding, valuation, total company revenue, vendor marketing, or one top-down estimate as proof that buyers want the exact offer.

State annual engagement and capacity requirements without inventing a conversion rate. The first validation cycle measures acceptance. Only then may the episode use the observed rate to estimate the qualified prospect pool required.

A published Operator Blueprint requires an honest **BUILD** verdict. If the approved evidence and operating model cannot support that recommendation, return the candidate to the Investment Thesis, Canvas, or Step 0, or park it before script and production. Do not publish a blueprint whose final advice is merely to keep considering the idea.

This rule governs the company thesis. An individual buyer engagement may still end with a no-build, refuse, narrow, defer, or stop decision. That result can demonstrate the operator's judgment without weakening the episode's recommendation to build the company.

## Teach the business, not the research process

Research supports the argument but is not the plot. Use evidence when it changes what the viewer should believe, clarifies a mechanism, or bounds an assumption. Do not narrate a bibliography or stack case studies merely to prove work was done.

Carry one concrete human situation, transaction, or buyer decision through the part of the script that explains the mechanism. An illustrative scene may not receive an invented company identity, measurement, quotation, fact, or result. State its status aloud only when a reasonable viewer could mistake it for a documented or measured case. Routine labeling may live in script metadata, a source card, or a visual label.

The script must distinguish:

- What has been observed.
- What adjacent parallels suggest.
- What the episode models.
- What remains unknown.

Spoken qualifications should be natural and audible when they change meaning: “reported,” “using public pricing,” “here's the model,” “we don't know yet,” or similarly plain language approved in the claims map. State each boundary once and do not turn the script into a running apology.

For every material external fact, separate evidence delivery into three layers:

1. **Voiceover:** state the finding conversationally, name the source or population where credibility or scope requires it, and keep any limitation that changes the conclusion audible.
2. **On-screen source receipt:** carry the exact source, artifact, date, population or sample when relevant, figure, locator, and claim ID.
3. **Show notes:** carry the full citation, direct link, publication details, access date, locator, methodology note, known limitation, and Step 0 provenance needed for independent review.

The voiceover must remain honest for an audio-only listener, but it should not recite receipt metadata. An on-screen card or show-note link cannot cure a misleading spoken claim.

Money claims, forecasts, constructed cases, and outcome claims still require an audible plain-English qualifier. A source card cannot turn an unqualified earnings or outcome statement into an acceptable one.

Step 1 approves the source-receipt and show-note content. It does not direct the receipt's layout, timing, animation, or production treatment.

## Reported explanation, understatement, and conviction

Every major act should move from a specific situation or claim through evidence, a fair explanation, the hidden consequence, a ruling, and a practical operator implication. A restrained observation or comedic decode may help after the fact is understood. Not every beat needs a joke. Every major evidence block does need an implication.

The script must state:

- the central thesis after orientation;
- what the common framing gets wrong;
- the strongest reasonable counterargument;
- a clear ruling after that counterargument;
- at least one position a reasonable person could dispute;
- what the operator should sell, refuse, test, stop, or wait on; and
- why the company deserves the approved BUILD verdict.

Dry wit, sarcasm, and contempt may target broken incentives, fake certainty, meaningless activity, software replacing judgment, and recurring fees without recurring responsibility. They may not target the viewer, customer, worker, or under-resourced operator. Humor follows comprehension and must explain, compress, expose, or help the listener retain the mechanism. Decorative or premature punch lines fail.

## First-listen density

The listener cannot reread the paragraph.

- Give the familiar picture before the abstract label.
- Introduce one unfamiliar mechanism at a time.
- Do not combine a new term, several numbers, a new analogy, and a joke in one beat.
- Use a plain restatement or breath after a difficult number or causal turn.
- Prefer the sentence a colleague would naturally say over the tighter sentence a writer would admire.
- Flag three consecutive sentences of six words or fewer and more than roughly 15 percent of sentences at four words or fewer.
- Use no more than one deliberately aphoristic landing in a major act.

Mechanical rhythm can pass while the script still feels written. The continuous first-listen review decides.

## Economics

Economics are a transparent scenario, not an income forecast.

The script must expose the assumptions that drive price, volume, delivery time, direct cost, capacity, and contribution. It should explain which assumption matters most and what the viewer would need to test. Avoid “you could make,” assured outcomes, the most favorable math presented as typical, and revenue without the operating cost or capacity required to produce it.

## Structure

There is no required seven-section script. The approved narrative spine determines the order. The script must still satisfy the working promise and cover every material Canvas question.

Each sequence should:

- Answer a real viewer question.
- Add a new piece of understanding.
- Use only approved claims.
- Preserve forward movement.
- Hand naturally to the next idea.

Test every adjacent narrated sequence by reading the outgoing sequence's last two spoken lines followed immediately by the incoming sequence's first two spoken lines, without headings. The incoming lines must inherit and advance a specific noun, question, decision, image, or consequence. Revise any seam that resets like a deck heading, reintroduces the subject, redefines the company, or could follow almost any sequence. The approved silent identity sting and brand reset are the only routine exception and must be identified rather than silently counted as a pass.

Avoid mechanical micro-open-loops at every section boundary. Forward tension should come from the actual unresolved business question, not teaser language.

If discovery determines whether implementation should be sold, the script must preserve that decision. Do not narrate diagnosis and build as one inevitable package. A responsible no-build recommendation may be the proof that the operator is selling judgment rather than tools.

When the larger business exceeds the entry offer, establish the complete company before narrowing to the wedge. Make the first purchase concrete, then explain later scope as conditional expansion. The broader ambition may provide aspiration and a strong closing case, but it must remain conditional on buyer, delivery, repeatability, safety, capacity, and economic proof. Do not front-load every future service to make the business sound substantial, and do not imply that beginning narrowly fixes the permanent ceiling of the company.

The first 30 days begin construction. The operator should create a buyer-facing asset or delivery capability, reach a bounded prospect set, make a paid ask, and record labor, cost, exceptions, and buyer decisions. The next 60 days deliver funded work, replace modeled inputs with observed operating evidence, and add scope only when a buyer and the delivery record support it. This is bounded construction with kill conditions, not indefinite validation theater.

## End with resolution before asking

The final sequence follows this order:

```text
opening callback
→ promised payoff delivered
→ evidence-safe BUILD verdict
→ first construction action
→ optional Canvas mention or brand sign-off
→ one natural like-and-subscribe sentence as the final spoken beat
```

The audience ask must follow delivered value, give a reason to return for more Operator Economy blueprints, and introduce no new promise. Combine or remove competing Canvas, download, slogan, like, and subscribe requests rather than stacking them. The payoff teaser and final CTA are locked Step 1 words. Step 2 may direct their delivery but may not invent, rewrite, or improvise either one.

## Human judgment

The script may be drafted with model assistance, but no placeholder such as `[POV]`, `[VERIFY]`, `[SOURCE]`, `[TBD]`, or an unresolved editorial note may remain at lock. The owner's judgment should be integrated into the argument and language, not pasted into preselected slots.

An adversarial review should ask:

- Does this sound more certain than the evidence?
- Does the viewer know what is happening before the narrator tells them what to think?
- Does the opening separate the situation, pre-sting operator or business payoff, show identity, episode promise, context, central question, and thesis?
- Does the payoff tease a useful destination without explaining the mechanism, claiming proof, or spending the complete thesis?
- Would a colleague naturally say each sentence, or does it only read well?
- Does any beat ask the viewer to process too many new ideas at once?
- Does the model actually solve the buyer's problem?
- Does the script name the complete company before narrowing to its first offer?
- Can a listener repeat the short company name and explain it after one hearing?
- Do the opportunity case and operator blueprint receive enough room to do different jobs?
- Would a skeptical operator find the steps usable?
- Is any paragraph generic enough to belong in another episode?
- Is the strongest objection treated fairly?
- Does the narrator actually rule on the objection?
- Does the governing analogy map the mechanism and deepen through callbacks?
- Could the joke be removed without making the mechanism harder to understand?
- Is contempt aimed at the system rather than a person trapped inside it?
- Does any uncertainty line repeat a boundary that the viewer already understands?
- Does any source sentence sound like a report citation that belongs in the on-screen receipt or show notes?
- Does the script advise the viewer to begin where experience, relationships, access, and reachable buyers overlap, or does it accidentally prescribe the example industry?
- Do the last two spoken lines of each narrated sequence causally hand to the first two of the next?
- Does the same discourse marker open more than one scene?
- Does the script accidentally become a software tutorial, company profile, or motivational speech?
- Does the ending recommend BUILD with conviction while keeping modeled and unknown elements honest?
- Does the ending deliver the teased payoff and end with one natural like-and-subscribe sentence after the first action?

## Claims and citations

Every load-bearing claim receives a stable claim ID in the claims map. Editorial review copies may show claim IDs outside the spoken text. The clean performance read-through removes them.

No new fact, number, company example, legal assertion, or market statement may enter the script without either:

1. Existing authorization in the Step 0 package; or
2. A Step 0 amendment with a new approval and hash.

Changing the certainty or interpretation of an approved item may create a new claim. In particular, a modeled price does not become a `market price`, and a scenario does not become `conservative`, `realistic`, `reasonable`, or `achievable` without evidence supporting that meaning. Similarly, `can`, `may`, `many`, `most`, `around`, and `up to` do not make an unsupported factual statement admissible.

Remove unsupported language when the episode does not need it. If the central argument truly requires it, request only the exact missing evidence through a bounded Step 0 amendment.

## Items deliberately excluded from Step 1

The script package does not contain:

- Final title or thumbnail decisions.
- Asset hints.
- Shot lists.
- B-roll searches.
- Camera or layout directions.
- Motion or transition instructions.
- Music or sound-effect cues.
- HyperFrames, Resolve, or tool-specific production instructions.
- Estimated visual beats based on unrecorded narration.
