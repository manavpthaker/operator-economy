## Refinement from the legacy premise

The 2026-08-21 legacy screen found the EP004 premise ("a solo subscription design agency", any buyer, any deliverable, the DesignJoy shape) too dependent on one operator's self-reported revenue and too broad to test. This candidate bounds it without changing the underlying business:

- **One buyer:** the founder or first marketing hire at a seed-stage B2B software startup (roughly 2-20 people) with no designer on staff.
- **One job:** recurring marketing design — landing pages, sales and pitch decks, ad and social creative — not product UI, not brand identity, not video.
- **One deliverable cadence:** one active request at a time, 48-hour first-draft turnaround, month-to-month, pause or cancel any time. This is the exact operating contract the category's reference operator publishes.
- **One 30-day test:** thirty named startups, three unsolicited demo redesigns, demo-first outreach, one paid month at a predeclared price, hours logged per request, renewal asked at day 30.

The legacy "$1M-$3.1M a year, one person" story is not the premise. The premise is whether one designer-operator can win and keep five of these clients at a price above the category's commodity floor.

## Opportunity in one sentence

A working designer, or a design-literate operator with a real portfolio, sells seed-stage software startups an ongoing marketing-design subscription — one request at a time, 48-hour turnaround, flat monthly fee — producing the landing pages, decks and campaign creative those companies need continuously but cannot yet justify a hire for, with AI tooling carrying production and the operator's taste and judgment carrying the price.

## Viewer outcome

- Viewer promise: The viewer understands what the subscription-design model actually sells (capacity and turnaround, not "unlimited"), why the category's largest operators retreated from that promise while the solo version kept it, and whether they personally can run it.
- Operator decision: Build it, run the 30-day demo-first test, fold a subscription tier into an existing freelance practice, or reject it because they lack the design skill or the distribution.
- Practical capability: Set a request-cadence contract, price against a published $499-$5,995 category ladder, model capacity at one request at a time, and run a demo-first outreach loop with a predeclared success and kill condition.
- Expected Operator Canvas: Operator fit (this one requires design ability), buyer and trigger, offer contract, delivery workflow and tool jobs, capacity arithmetic, pricing ladder, churn exposure, acquisition loop, disqualifiers.

## People

- Viewer/operator: A designer with a portfolio, or a product or marketing generalist who can genuinely design — **this is not a business a non-designer can run with AI tools alone**, and the episode must say so.
- Buyer: Founder or first marketing hire at a seed-stage B2B software startup, 2-20 people, no in-house designer.
- End customer or beneficiary: The startup's prospects and investors, who see the pages, decks and ads.
- Guest or outside participant required: no

## Problem

- Costly problem: Early-stage startups need a continuous stream of marketing design (a new landing page for every campaign, a deck for every raise, creative for every ad test) but average around six equity-holding employees and defer full-time hires; design is among the last roles filled.
- Why it matters: Each un-designed launch, deck or ad test either ships looking unfinished, waits on a founder's evenings, or goes through a project quote that takes longer than the campaign. The consequence is delay and lost credibility, not a documented dollar loss — the research brief must not pretend otherwise.
- Why now: AI production tools (Figma Make, v0, Lovable, Framer) have compressed the time to produce a page or deck; the same tools are reaching the buyer directly; and in the last fifteen months the category's largest operators pulled back — Design Pickle formally ended "unlimited" in June 2025 and Superside cut staff in March 2026 — while the solo reference operator kept its one-request-at-a-time contract and its price.
- Existing alternatives and budget: Do-it-yourself with templates and AI builders; freelancers by project; commodity subscription vendors from roughly $499-$1,199 a month; premium subscription vendors at $4,995-$5,995; enterprise subscription at $15,000 a month minimum. Budget exists as a category; whether *this* buyer pays a *solo* operator above the floor is the open question.

## Proposed business

- Offer: Month-to-month marketing-design subscription. One active request at a time, unlimited queue, 48-hour first-draft turnaround, unlimited revisions, pause or cancel any time, work delivered as Figma files plus Framer or Webflow pages where in scope.
- Customer outcome: A queue of shipped marketing assets every month, each delivered within a published turnaround, with source files owned by the client.
- Delivery hypothesis: Request board (Trello or Notion) → operator scopes each request against the contract → AI-assisted production (Figma Make / v0 / Lovable for layout and variants, Midjourney for imagery, Claude for copy variants) → operator selects, edits and finishes → client review → ship. Human judgment is the product; tools are production.
- Revenue hypothesis: Flat monthly fee, charged in advance, pause and cancel any time. Introductory price in the low thousands; category premium price near $5,000.
- Most important unproven assumption: **That a seed-stage startup with the same AI tools will pay one unknown designer a monthly fee above the commodity floor, and keep paying after month one.** AI production speed is not evidence of either.

## Initial synthesis hypothesis

- Parallel A: Designjoy (solo, $4,995/month, one request at a time, 48-hour delivery, "run entirely by Brett") — the exact offer contract and price ceiling for a solo operator.
- Parallel B: The commodity and mid-market subscription vendors (ManyPixels, Penji, Kimp, a 50-company directory) — the price floor, the deliverable scope buyers accept, and proof the category has many sellers.
- Parallel C: Design Pickle's June 2025 end of "unlimited" and Superside's March 2026 restructuring — evidence about what the model cannot promise at scale, transferred as the capacity constraint a solo operator must publish rather than hide.
- New combination: Not new — this is an observed model. What the package adds is the bounded buyer, the capacity contract as the real product, and honest economics that exclude the self-reported revenue the category is usually sold on.
- Suspected transfer risk: Designjoy's price and client count are self-reported and vary by telling (roughly 20 clients in one 2025 interview, roughly 35 in secondary write-ups); its growth channel is an audience on X that a new operator does not have. The price ceiling may not transfer without the audience.

These are research directions, not evidence. The completed research brief and analogy map decide whether the transfers are valid.

## Narrative potential

- Starting state: A seed-stage founder needs a landing page for Tuesday's launch; there is no designer, the last freelancer quote took a week, and the founder is doing it in Canva at midnight.
- Inciting change: AI tools make a first draft nearly free — for the founder as much as for any agency — and the biggest subscription vendors respond by retreating from "unlimited."
- Causal mechanism: Subscription design sells capacity, not design. "Unlimited" breaks when many clients queue against a shared bench; one operator with one request at a time is the only version where the promise and the capacity are the same number.
- Operator build: A one-person subscription with a published cadence contract, a demo-first outreach loop, and a request queue the operator can actually clear.
- Stakes and tradeoffs: Price against a $499 floor or a $4,995 ceiling; take a sixth client and break the 48-hour promise; lose one client and lose a fifth of revenue; watch the buyer's own Figma Make button get better every quarter.
- End state: The viewer knows whether they are a designer who can sell this, what to charge, how many clients one person can honestly serve, and the condition under which to stop.
- Visual evidence: Pricing pages side by side ($499 → $4,995 → $15,000); a request board and a 48-hour clock; Design Pickle's own "unlimited" retraction; the Figma Make adoption line; a demo redesign next to the prospect's live page; the outreach tracker.

## Known blockers

1. **Every solo economic figure in circulation is one operator's self-report.** Revenue is told at $1M, $1.5M, $1.7M, $2M+ and $3.1M depending on the outlet; client count at roughly 20 or roughly 35. Not usable for industry economics under `EVIDENCE-STANDARD.md`.
2. **No churn or retention evidence exists for the category.** Agency-churn benchmarks found online publish no methodology. Retention is the business and it is a hypothesis.
3. **The buyer has the tools too.** Figma Make, v0 and Lovable are sold to the startup directly. Disintermediation is not a risk to footnote; it is the central threat.
4. **Acquisition is unproven.** Designjoy's growth is attributed by its founder to an audience on X; cold-outreach benchmarks conflict by an order of magnitude between vendors; BusyLobby has zero clients.
5. **The operator must be able to design.** This narrows the channel's generalist audience and must be stated as a disqualifier, not softened.
6. **Subscription cancellation terms are under active FTC attention** (click-to-cancel rule vacated July 2025, rulemaking restarted March 2026, ROSCA enforcement ongoing). Manageable, but the Canvas must specify honest cancellation mechanics.

## Intake decision

Decision: research

Reason: The business is real and observed — a 50-company category with published prices and an exact solo reference contract — and the bounded premise is testable in thirty days. But the reason it was "strong" in the legacy screen (the headline economics) is exactly the part that is not usable, and the two things that decide whether it works — retention and acquisition — have no independent evidence yet. Research must establish buyer behavior, the price ladder, the capacity model and the incumbents' retreat from primary sources, and must score the economics on what can be modeled, not on what one operator says.
