## Opportunity in one sentence

An operator sells task-order overflow processing to a small public agency — collect, de-duplicate,
AI-review, pre-redact, and package responsive records into a release-ready installment with a draft
exemption log — while the agency's records officer and counsel make every disclosure, exemption,
and denial decision.

## Viewer outcome

- Viewer promise: Understand why cheap AI-written requests pushed labor onto the one clerk who
  answers them, what agencies already pay outside firms to absorb it, and where an operator's work
  must stop.
- Operator decision: Test, adapt, or reject a records-processing overflow service after seeing the
  observed contract rates, the procurement path, the data-handling burden, and the decision line.
- Practical capability: Scope one task order; separate processing labor from disclosure decisions;
  estimate hours per request with and without AI review; name the records classes to refuse.
- Expected Operator Canvas: Buyer, costly job, bounded offer, decision boundary, substitute map
  (staff, counsel, e-discovery firms, portal software), procurement path, task-order economics, and
  falsification threshold.

## People

- Viewer/operator: Someone with records-management, paralegal, e-discovery, or public-administration
  experience who can work inside an agency's systems under its policies. Not a generalist.
- Buyer: Records officer, clerk, or superintendent/administrator of a school district, township,
  small city, or small county with one records officer and no in-house e-discovery capacity.
- End customer or beneficiary: Requesters (residents, journalists, businesses) and the agency's
  staff and counsel.
- Guest or outside participant required: no. The episode can be shown with a synthetic records set
  and public contract documents.

## Problem

- Costly problem: Voluminous requests must be searched, reviewed, redacted, and produced inside
  statutory deadlines by one person who also has another job.
- Why it matters: Methacton School District (PA) reported about $126,627 of staff and legal cost for
  64 requests in 2025–26 (CLM-003, reported at a public meeting, secondary transcription). Longview
  School District (WA) reported $96,556.58 of outside legal support for records processing through
  2026-08-31 (CLM-004, same limit). Washington agencies reported $128 million and 1.7 million hours
  responding to 483,861 requests in 2024 (CLM-005, primary).
- Why now: AI request tools cut the cost of filing broad requests (CLM-007, CLM-008), while AI review
  and redaction tools cut the hours to process them. Complexity (video, large digital files) is also
  cited independently of AI (CLM-006).
- Existing alternatives and budget: staff overtime and new specialist posts, outside counsel, portal
  software (NextRequest, GovQA and others), request-policy changes (anonymous-request bans, fees),
  and — observed — outside e-discovery consultancies on task-order contracts (CLM-001, CLM-006).

## Proposed business

- Offer: Overflow records processing on task orders under a master services agreement: intake of
  collected files, de-duplication, AI-assisted responsiveness review, first-pass redaction,
  installment packaging, draft exemption log, and a reporting log.
- Customer outcome: A release-ready installment and log delivered to the records officer for
  approval; measured as backlog count and days to installment before and after.
- Delivery hypothesis: The operator works inside the agency's own systems or an approved review tool;
  AI does the bulk reading and pre-marking; the operator verifies every mark; the records officer and
  counsel approve all releases and denials.
- Revenue hypothesis: Hourly or per-task-order fee under a not-to-exceed agreement, as observed in
  CLM-001. Price for a solo operator is unknown.
- Most important unproven assumption: A small agency will contract a small AI-enabled operator —
  rather than add staff, lean on counsel, buy software for its clerk, or hire an established
  e-discovery firm — and that operator's verified hours per request are low enough to undercut those
  options while carrying the needed insurance and data safeguards.

## Initial synthesis hypothesis

- Parallel A: E-discovery consultancy on a county task-order contract for public-records processing
  (CLM-001) → offer scope, pricing unit, contracting vehicle.
- Parallel B: A city RFP seeking a firm or individual to supplement records staff while the city
  keeps final disclosure decisions (CLM-006) → buyer behavior and decision boundary.
- Parallel C: Michigan FOIA's contracted-labor provision for separating exempt material (CLM-002) →
  statutory recognition of contracted separation labor and a partial fee pass-through.
- New combination: AI review makes a one-person operator a credible substitute for part of the
  e-discovery-firm model at small-agency volumes.
- Suspected transfer risk: Parallel A is a mid-sized, well-funded county buying from an established
  firm with a certified e-discovery specialist; a small district may not buy the same thing from a
  solo operator.

These are research directions, not evidence. The completed research brief and analogy map decide
whether the transfers are valid.

## Narrative potential

- Starting state: One records officer, a handful of requests a quarter.
- Inciting change: Requests arrive by the dozen, some written by AI tools, each asking for months of
  email or video.
- Causal mechanism: Asking got cheap; answering did not. Deadlines and penalties sit on the agency.
- Operator build: A bounded processing desk: intake, AI review, human verification, installment and
  log, decision hand-back.
- Stakes and tradeoffs: A wrongful release exposes private data; a missed deadline triggers
  penalties; AI marks must be verified, which is the operator's real labor.
- End state: A synthetic 2,000-document request processed on screen, hours logged, and the viewer
  deciding whether the unit economics beat the county's observed contract rate.
- Visual evidence: Public contract rate schedule, board-meeting cost slides, a synthetic inbox export,
  redaction review screens, before/after backlog chart.

- Exact or adjacent viewer questions: "AI public records requests", "how to handle voluminous FOIA
  requests", "public records backlog", requester-side questions about unresponsive agencies (pool

- Initial interest signals: repeated local news and board-meeting coverage of surges and costs
  (CLM-003, CLM-004, CLM-006, CLM-008); requester-side coverage (CLM-009).
- Timely tension: AI on both sides of a legal deadline.
- Coverage gap: Coverage is requester rights (CLM-009), agency policy (CLM-007), or vendor software

  observed rates.
- Honest working premise: "AI made public-records requests nearly free to send. Here is who is
  getting paid to answer them, and whether a one-person operator could."

## Known blockers

- Procurement: public agencies buy through RFPs, cooperative contracts, and insurance requirements
  that may exclude a solo operator.
- Data handling: CJIS (police), FERPA (school records), HIPAA-adjacent material, and agency system
  access. Police records are out of scope; school work needs a school-official designation.
- Liability for wrongful release even when the agency approves.
- Durability: some surges are event- or litigation-driven (CLM-004, CLM-001 staff reasons).
- The strongest paid parallel is an established firm, not a solo operator.
