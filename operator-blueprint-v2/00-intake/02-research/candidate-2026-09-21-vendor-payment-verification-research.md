## Executive finding

- Opportunity: A qualified AP or payments operator installs a documented and tested vendor-change
  control for a small US business that originates ACH payments.
- Evidence class: adjacent synthesis
- Provisional verdict: plausible with bounded assumptions
- Main reason: The current Nacha duty, consequential buyer problem, recognizable control methods,
  adjacent service delivery, and software substitutes are real; target-buyer payment and safe
  provider economics are not.
- Largest unresolved question: Will the target buyer pay an independent operator before a loss
  instead of relying on its bank, existing adviser, or software?
- Required public framing: Separate the current rule and observed adjacent models from transferred
  delivery logic, modeled economics, and the unproven standalone-service hypothesis.

## Buyer reality

- Buyer: Owner or controller of a small US business that originates ACH payments to recurring
  vendors and lacks a dedicated treasury or fraud-operations team.
- End customer or beneficiary: Finance staff, bookkeeper, approver, vendors, and bank.
- Recurring problem: Vendor onboarding and bank-detail changes arrive through ordinary communication
  channels without an independent verification, role separation, exception, or evidence process.
- Consequence and frequency: Payment fraud, BEC, and vendor impersonation are consequential in
  corporate data and case studies. Frequency for this exact small-business segment is unknown.
- Existing alternatives or budget: Bank controls, an internal policy, bookkeeper, CPA, fractional
  controller, MSP, insurer, AP consultant, and vendor-verification software. Buyer budget is unknown.
- Why now: Nacha Phase 2 became practically effective June 22, 2026 for all covered non-consumer
  Originators and related parties, while current fraud guidance still depends on controls that must
  be implemented and used.

## Research question

Can an experienced AP or payments operator build a defensible fixed-scope business installing vendor-payment-change controls for small US businesses that originate ACH payments, or will banks, existing advisers, and software absorb the value?

## Evidence landscape

### Direct evidence

- Nacha directly establishes the covered buyer's current risk-based process-and-procedure duty and
  names payment-change controls as one possible method.
- FTC guidance and one anonymous small-business discussion directly establish the underlying invoice
  and informal-process problem. Neither establishes a purchase.
- No independent source located in this pass proves a completed purchase of the exact bounded offer.

### Adjacent business evidence

- Inlight IT reports a named BEC response and hardening engagement combining technical and

  service in Canada.

These sources support delivery and packaging hypotheses only. Seller pages and press releases do not

## Facts

- Nacha's Phase 2 fraud-monitoring rules became practically effective June 22, 2026 and cover all non-consumer Originators, Third-Party Senders, and Third-Party Service Providers regardless of volume. They require risk-based processes and procedures reasonably intended to identify entries initiated due to fraud, with annual review.
- Nacha explicitly lists controls around vendor and payroll payment changes as an example. It does not require every-entry screening, a particular technology, or a specific call-back procedure.
- Nacha says a covered party may use internal controls, bank-provided controls, or a third-party tool. Responsibility for the rule obligation is not eliminated by outsourcing.
- The Association for Financial Professionals reported that 79% of 521 corporate-practitioner respondents experienced attempted or actual payments fraud in 2024; 63% cited business email compromise and 45% cited vendor imposters. The sample is not small-business-specific.
- The FBI's 2025 IC3 report separately recorded more than 22,000 complaints containing AI-related information and more than $893 million in adjusted losses; businesses reported more than $30 million in BEC losses involving AI. This is an AI-referenced subset, not the total BEC market.
- The FTC currently advises small businesses to define purchase and invoice-approval procedures because fake invoices and phishing can reach the person who pays bills.
- Adjacent sellers publicly offer AP assessments, vendor-payment control reviews, verification toolkits, and vendor-bank-change software. Public list prices exist, but public list prices do not prove sales.
- A named Australian MSP case study describes a real BEC incident at OYA Financial Services and a
  seller-reported response combining Microsoft 365, identity, and payment-process controls. It is a
  broader engagement than the proposed service, and the page does not disclose price.

## Assumptions

- A meaningful subset of covered small businesses lacks a documented, tested vendor-change process.
- These buyers understand or can be shown the value before a loss occurs.
- A qualified operator can deliver value without accessing bank credentials, releasing payments, validating ownership, or certifying compliance.
- The project can be sold as a finite operations install rather than an open-ended security engagement.
- An existing bookkeeping, controllership, accounting, bank, broker, or insurer relationship can produce a viable acquisition path.
- The buyer will not treat a bank feature, policy template, or low-cost software product as sufficient.

## Proposed synthesis

- Parallel A and supported component: AP/P2P consulting -> assessment, workflow mapping,
  documentation, fixed-project delivery, and professional expertise.
- Parallel B and supported component: BEC response and control hardening -> attack-path analysis and
  the combination of technical boundaries with payment-process controls.
- Parallel C and supported component: insurer and payments-rule control evidence -> recognizable
  control questions, evidence artifacts, and annual review.
- New combination: A preventive, small-business-scale install of the minimum testable control at the
  point where payment instructions change, with payment authority and assurance claims explicitly
  excluded.
- Primary transfer risk: The source models are broader, post-incident, mid-market, security-led, or
  productized. Banks, software, and existing advisers may absorb the narrow service.
- Analogy map: not created; evidence is insufficient for a useful formal transfer verdict.

## Proposed Operator Blueprint

- Offer: Fixed-scope vendor-payment verification and control-install sprint.
- Buyer outcome: A documented and tested vendor-onboarding and payment-detail-change process with
  verification, approval roles, exceptions, a change log, incident handling, and an annual-review
  checklist.
- Delivery workflow: Scope one workflow; map current handoffs; identify higher-risk changes; install
  trusted-contact verification and role controls; document exceptions and evidence; run a synthetic
  tabletop; hand ownership back; review after 30 days.
- Technology and human stack: Buyer decision rights, staff training, existing bank and accounting
  controls, a minimal change log and evidence record, and optional software only after the control
  job is defined.
- Required skills or credentials: AP, bookkeeping, controller, treasury, payments, or cyber-risk
  experience; the exact payments/compliance, insurance, and legal boundary remains unknown.
- Go-to-market path: Hypothesis only - existing bookkeeping, fractional-controller, accounting, bank,
  broker, or insurer relationships. No acquisition channel is verified.
- Pricing model: Fixed fee; observed adjacent seller anchors inform a test, but target price is
  modeled and not market evidence.
- Defensibility: Accountable workflow implementation, a tested evidence trail, and clear decision
  boundaries rather than tool resale. Durability remains unproven.

## Hypotheses

- The highest-value deliverable is a tested process and evidence record, not a policy document.
- “Vendor-payment verification install” will be easier for a buyer to understand than “fraud-readiness consulting.”
- The first viable operator will likely already have AP, bookkeeping, controller, treasury, payments, or cyber-risk credibility.
- A referral or embedded channel through bookkeepers, fractional controllers, accountants, banks, brokers, or insurers will outperform cold direct acquisition.
- Annual review may support a later repeat engagement, but recurring monitoring is unnecessary and may create avoidable duty and liability.
- The correct narrow outcome is a control that the buyer owns and tests, not a promise that fraudulent payments will stop.

## Unknowns

- How many target small businesses are covered non-consumer ACH Originators and lack adequate controls?
- Which segment has the highest combination of payment-change frequency, loss exposure, and ability to pay?
- Will a buyer pay an independent operator before a loss or failed audit?
- What qualifications, professional insurance, contract language, and legal review are needed?
- Can a useful assessment be performed without collecting sensitive data?
- Which controls are already supplied by the buyer's bank, accounting platform, bookkeeper, MSP, or insurer?
- What price, delivery time, sales cycle, and acquisition cost produce a viable contribution margin?
- Does an annual review create enough repeat work to matter?
- Is the 2026 rule a genuine buying trigger for small businesses, or mainly a bank/compliance talking point?

## Narrative and audience hypothesis

- Operator protagonist: An experienced AP or payments practitioner deciding whether to turn a
  current control gap into a bounded service.
- Starting state: A small finance team handles vendor details and change requests through ordinary
  email and informal trust.
- Inciting change: A convincing impersonation reaches the workflow while a current rule makes the
  missing process visible.
- Stakes: A high-consequence payment error, slower legitimate payments if the control is badly
  designed, and professional liability if the operator crosses the wrong boundary.
- Causal mechanism: The request and confirmation use the same channel, one person can act alone, and
  there is no trusted-contact or exception record.
- Build transformation: Install the smallest independent, testable, documented process and run a
  synthetic change request through it.
- Decisions and tradeoffs: Which changes are high risk, when a callback or dual approval is
  appropriate, what the bank/software already handles, who owns exceptions, and what the operator
  must refuse.
- End state and payoff: The control passes a synthetic tabletop and the viewer can decide whether
  buyer payment and provider qualification justify a live pilot.
- Visual evidence classes: Before/after workflow, fictional vendor records, trusted-contact script,
  approval matrix, change log, exception tree, tabletop, and responsibility boundary.
- Working audience promise: See how to test a vendor-payment control-install business without
  pretending the rule or fraud statistics prove demand.
- Honest packaging territory: “The boring payment control small businesses now have to think about”
  rather than a fraud-proofing, compliance, or income promise.

## Risks and constraints

- Factual uncertainty: Exact small-business frequency, current controls, and target-buyer purchase
  behavior.
- Analogy-transfer uncertainty: Adjacent AP and cyber engagements are broader, larger, and often
  post-incident.
- Legal or regulatory boundary: No legal advice, compliance certification, account-ownership
  verification, cybersecurity assurance, or interpretation beyond cited Nacha materials.
- Permissions, privacy, or guest dependency: No real bank details, credentials, invoices, vendor
  records, incident correspondence, or client controls without permission and redaction. No guest is
  required.
- Platform or vendor dependency: Bank capabilities and accounting/software integrations vary and may
  eliminate the service.
- Ethical failure mode: Selling fear, overstating a rule, handling credentials, taking payment
  authority, or promising fraud prevention.
- Claims that must not be made: “All small businesses need this”; “Nacha requires a callback or this
  service”; seller prices prove market price; fraud-loss statistics prove purchase intent; the owner
  has relevant experience; the modeled economics are observed; or the service certifies compliance.

## Analogy candidates

1. **AP and procure-to-pay consulting**
   - Observed behavior: AP assessments, vendor-management work, fraud controls, and project pricing.
   - Useful transfer: delivery shape, expertise requirement, and fixed-project economics.
   - Structural difference: broader transformation work can justify a larger fee and may sell to mid-market buyers.

2. **BEC incident response and control hardening**
   - Observed behavior: a seller performs technical investigation plus identity and payment-process remediation for a named client.
   - Useful transfer: buyers can purchase outside help around a real payment-control failure.
   - Structural difference: post-incident urgency, cybersecurity scope, and technical credentials are materially different from a preventive small-business install.

3. **Insurance and payments-rule control evidence**
   - Observed behavior: insurer forms and Nacha guidance name independent verification, separation of duties, dual authorization, and risk-based procedures.
   - Useful transfer: the deliverables can map to recognizable control questions.
   - Structural difference: a control requirement does not prove an independent operator is the preferred provider.

4. **Vendor-verification software**
   - Observed behavior: low-cost tools package bank-change intake, approval, and verification.
   - Useful transfer: shows what must be faster, cheaper, or more automated after the human install.
   - Structural difference: software is a substitute and may eliminate the service rather than validate it.

The analogy set is adequate to show category adjacency, but not adequate to establish target-buyer demand or exact economics. A formal analogy scorecard would manufacture precision at this stage.

## Thirty-day validation plan

No outreach is authorized by this brief.

1. Interview five target buyers or close buying advisers: small-business controllers, full-charge bookkeepers, fractional controllers, or small accounting-practice owners who support ACH-paying clients.
2. For each, document current vendor-onboarding and bank-change workflow, change frequency, recent near misses, bank controls, insurer questions, owner of the process, and whether outside implementation is permitted.
3. Ask for a purchase decision on one bounded paid pilot, not abstract interest. Test a predeclared fixed price and exact deliverables.
4. Obtain a qualified payments/compliance and insurance boundary review covering scope language, credentials, data handling, and exclusion of payment authority or compliance certification.
5. Run one synthetic delivery rehearsal and record actual hours, artifacts, failure modes, and any sensitive-data requirement.
6. Recheck whether the buyer's bank or existing accounting software can deliver the same outcome at lower cost.

### Success signal

At least one target buyer accepts a paid pilot or a comparably strong purchase commitment, the boundary review identifies a safe operator profile and contract scope, and the synthetic rehearsal can be completed without credentials or payment authority inside a contribution-positive time budget.

### Kill or redesign signal

Reject the standalone service if buyers defer entirely to banks/software/existing advisers, no target buyer will pay before a loss, or the work requires security/compliance credentials and liability that the narrow project cannot support. Redesign toward an enablement package for qualified bookkeepers or controllers only if that buyer and channel are independently evidenced.

## Research verdict

**Verdict:** continue research.

**Evidence class:** adjacent synthesis with a current implementation trigger.

**What is established:** the current rule, the payment-fraud problem, recognizable control
mechanisms, adjacent service offers, and software substitutes.

**What is not established:** target-buyer paid demand, price, acquisition, safe provider qualifications, or contribution margin.

The evidence supports admission into Step 0, not eligibility or promotion. The next consequential question is buyer payment, not more general fraud statistics.
