## Executive finding

- Opportunity: A generalist with hospitality judgment sells 20-40-room US independent properties a paid direct-booking audit and a flat monthly retainer that keeps the property findable, makes the direct path work, and re-books past guests from consented first-party data, reporting direct share of bookings monthly.
- Evidence class: **adjacent synthesis**
- Provisional verdict: **plausible with bounded assumptions — and the bound is tight.**
- Main reason: The buyer's problem is real and documented from four independent directions (a vendor dataset of 90 million bookings, a 3,000-hotel association study, the OTAs' own SEC-filed take rates, and a 15,000-hotel damages action). The delivery components are cheap and mostly commission-free. What the research adds is the arithmetic the V1 episode skipped: at a modeled 20-room property a realistic 10-point mix shift recovers roughly $1,000-1,400 a month net of direct costs, which caps what the property can rationally pay. The retainer that clears an operator's cost of time exists only near the top of the band.
- Largest unresolved question: **Will a 20-40-room owner pay $600-1,250 a month for recovery work when the recoverable commission is of the same order?** No observed operator sells this bundle at a published price; the adjacent agencies price at $1,500-6,000 and size for 20-60+ keys.
- Required public framing: OTA share, cancellation-by-channel, and programme mechanics are **observed but vendor- or OTA-published and must be attributed**; the European direct-share and litigation figures are **observed** from an association and a court filing; the service format is **transferred** from outsourced revenue management and independent-hotel marketing agencies; every price, mix shift, hour, and contribution figure is **modeled**; the claim that the service raises direct share is a **hypothesis**. The 2026 Booking.com partner pages were not opened directly and must be before any script lock.

## Buyer reality

- Buyer: Owner or owner-operator of a US independent lodging property of roughly 20-40 rooms (inn, B&B, small boutique hotel) with no revenue-management or marketing hire. AHLA counts 64,000+ US lodging properties including 33,200+ small-business properties (CLM-012); an independent size-band count was not obtainable without a Census API key and is left as a research gap.
- End customer or beneficiary: The property's returning guests, and the front desk that currently carries the unowned handoff.
- Recurring problem: A majority of independent-property reservations arrive through OTAs (63.4% globally in 2025 per Cloudbeds, CLM-001; European direct channels still held 50.9% of overnight stays in 2023 per HOTREC, CLM-002 — the two conflict and both are kept visible), each paying a commission; the OTA masks the guest's email and closes the messaging window after checkout (CLM-008), so the property has no durable contact with a guest it has already served and pays to meet the same guest again.
- Consequence and frequency: Booking Holdings earned $26.9 billion on $186.1 billion of gross bookings in 2025, a blended take rate of about 14.5% (CLM-003); Expedia earned $14.7 billion on $119.6 billion, about 12.3% (CLM-004). Those are the platforms' blended figures across all products, not the hotel commission, and they are the independent floor under the commission conversation. OTA bookings cancelled at 21.8% versus 10.6% direct in the Cloudbeds dataset (CLM-001, vendor). Sixty-five percent of surveyed US hotels report staffing shortages and 71% have openings they cannot fill (CLM-012).
- Existing alternatives or budget: Do nothing; buy a PMS bundle (Cloudbeds quote-only; Little Hotelier about $29-39 to $109 per month, CLM-010); hire a hotel marketing agency at a published $1,500 per month and up (CLM-011); engage an outsourced revenue manager, mostly quote-only (CLM-011). A budget category exists; the fit at 20-40 rooms is the open question.
- Why now: Booking.com moved Genius visibility to relevance-based ranking in early 2026, reducing guaranteed placement for the minimum 10% discount (CLM-005); 15,000+ European hotels registered for a collective damages action over 2004-2024 parity clauses following ECJ ruling C-264/23 of 19 September 2024, with a claim filed at the Amsterdam District Court on 30 January 2026 and a third expansion planned for autumn 2026 (CLM-007); Google's free booking links place a commission-free "official site" link beside OTA rates for any property connected through an integration partner (CLM-009); and OTA share of independent bookings rose in 2025 rather than fell (CLM-001).

The exact solution is a synthesis. The underlying problem — paying rented discovery repeatedly for a relationship the channel withholds — has usable direct evidence.

## Evidence landscape

### Direct evidence

No independent source was found disclosing fees, hours, or outcomes for an individual operator selling a recovery-first audit-plus-retainer to 20-40-room properties. The V1 episode's own finding that reviewed providers quote privately was re-checked and holds (CLM-011): HotelMinder's former service page now redirects to a marketplace with no public prices; Catala Consulting is quote-only and publishes a "+9% average first-year RevPAR uplift" claim that is not usable as evidence. The one published price found is an agency's "plans start at $1,500 monthly" aimed explicitly at independents, inns, and small resorts and listing past-guest email and direct-share reporting among its deliverables (CLM-011) — that is the closest observed offer, and it is the agency version, not the solo version.

### Adjacent business evidence

Two established practices share the structure. **Outsourced hotel revenue management** sells an ongoing commercial-management retainer to independents that cannot justify a hire (CLM-011). **Independent-hotel marketing agencies** sell retainers for search, metasearch, past-guest email, and direct-share reporting, with one published entry price and self-published tier guidance of $3,000-6,000 per month for 20-60 keys (CLM-011). Both establish that the buyer buys ongoing outside commercial help; neither establishes what a 20-40-room property will pay a solo operator, and the tier guidance is agency-authored with no stated basis.

### Component feasibility evidence

- Google free booking links carry no fee and no commission; eligibility runs through a Hotel Center account or an integration partner (CLM-009).
- Small-property booking engines exist without per-booking commission at a fixed monthly price (Little Hotelier Pro about $109 per month; Basics about $29-39 plus a 1% booking fee) (CLM-010). Cloudbeds states it charges no added commission on its booking engine or channel manager, pricing on quote (CLM-010).
- Booking.com's guest email is an `@guest.booking.com` alias, messaging runs until seven days after checkout, and threads become unreadable a year after checkout; Expedia threads stay visible 45 days after checkout with masked phone numbers (CLM-008). This is the component constraint that forces first-party capture at the property.
- Direct distribution cost is reported at 3.5% for a vendor's own client base against "12-28% typically charged by OTAs" (CLM-014, vendor) — used only as the low bound for direct cost in the model.

### Scaled relevance

The scale of the underlying behavior is not in question: $186 billion and $120 billion of 2025 gross bookings at the two largest OTAs (CLM-003, CLM-004); Booking Holdings holding about 71% of the European OTA market (CLM-002); 90 million bookings across tens of thousands of properties in the vendor dataset (CLM-001); more than 10,000 hotels in a filed claim expected to reach about 18,000 (CLM-007). Skift Research forecasts direct digital channels overtaking OTAs by 2030 ($409 billion versus $333 billion) (CLM-013, category research, context only). None of this proves a 20-40-room property will pay a solo operator.

## Proposed synthesis

- Parallel A and supported component: Outsourced hotel revenue management → ongoing outside commercial-management retainer bought by independents (offer/delivery)
- Parallel B and supported component: Independent-hotel marketing agency → budget category, published retainer floor, direct-share reporting deliverable (budget/current alternative, outcome measurement)
- Parallel C and supported component: Commission-free direct components (free booking links, fixed-fee booking engine, first-party consent capture) → low-fixed-cost direct path (delivery, economics)
- New combination: A recovery-first retainer that begins with the guests the property has already served, treats OTA data masking as the design constraint, prices under a computed ceiling, and reports one number.
- Primary transfer risk: **Size.** Agencies and revenue managers are bought by properties large enough to fund them. The ceiling arithmetic says a 20-room property funds a few hundred to about a thousand dollars a month; the parallels may not transfer below roughly 30-40 rooms.

## Proposed Operator Blueprint

- Offer: Fixed-fee direct-booking audit; flat monthly retainer covering three jobs (findable, bookable, remembered); monthly direct-share scorecard.
- Buyer outcome: Documented baseline (booking mix, commission, cancellation by channel, missed calls, website conversion, repeat stays); a direct path tested as a guest and fixed; a consented first-party guest list that grows every month; a monthly direct-share number.
- Delivery workflow: (1) Pull OTA extranet and PMS reports, compute the commission line and the ceiling; (2) test the direct path as a guest on a phone — rate versus OTA, availability, cancellation terms, checkout, phone at a busy hour; (3) fix findability — Google Business Profile, free booking links through the property's connectivity partner, photography and offers; (4) design consent capture at check-in and on WiFi, with the registration card asking for the guest's own email; (5) build the post-stay sequence — thank-you, review request, seasonal return offer, referral prompt — with the operator approving voice and every sensitive send; (6) report direct share before and after, monthly.
- Technology and human stack: Jobs first. Findable: Google Business Profile (free), free booking links via the PMS or channel manager. Bookable: the property's own booking engine (Little Hotelier, Cloudbeds, Mews, or similar), a call-forwarding or voice-agent fallback for missed calls. Remembered: PMS guest records with a consent field, a transactional email tool, Make or n8n for scheduling, a language model for first drafts. Human: the operator owns the audit judgment, the offer, the voice, consent handling, and the owner conversation.
- Required skills or credentials: Reading a booking-mix report; operating a booking engine and Google Business Profile; hospitality-grade writing; consent and email-law hygiene (CAN-SPAM; GDPR for EU guests); no licence or credential required.
- Go-to-market path: Start with properties the operator can visit. Lead with the ceiling calculation as the diagnostic — four inputs the owner already knows. Channels: innkeeper associations (the Association of Lodging Professionals, state B&B associations), PMS and channel-manager partner directories, and referrals from the property's own vendors. No audience assumed.
- Pricing model: **Bounded hypothesis.** Audit $1,200; retainer $600 per month at 20 rooms rising to about $1,000-1,250 at 40 rooms, each set below the property's modeled ceiling. Not transferred from agency tiers.
- Defensibility: Judgment about what hospitality sounds like, the consent-capture design, and the honesty of the scorecard. None of it is software resale.

## Narrative and audience hypothesis

- Operator protagonist: A generalist with hospitality judgment deciding whether a small property's commission line can fund a service worth their time.
- Starting state: An innkeeper gives a guest a good stay; the reservation carries an `@guest.booking.com` alias; the messaging window closes seven days after checkout.
- Inciting change: The guest returns a year later through the same OTA and the property pays to meet them again — while the OTA's 2026 programme change asks for a deeper discount to stay visible.
- Stakes: The largest controllable distribution cost, a booking that cancels at twice the direct rate, and a relationship the channel withholds; on the operator's side, a ceiling that decides whether the business exists.
- Causal mechanism: Discovery is rented; the post-stay relationship is nobody's job; the data to keep it is masked by the channel; the desk is short-staffed.
- Build transformation: Audit → direct-path test and fix → consent capture at the property → re-book known guests → one-number scorecard.
- Decisions and tradeoffs: Price under the ceiling or lose the renewal; automate drafting but not judgment; use OTA discovery, never fight it; refuse properties below the band.
- End state and payoff: The viewer can compute a property's ceiling in four inputs, knows the consent-capture design, and can decide build / test / reject on their own cost of time.
- Visual evidence classes: the masked alias on a reservation; the two-stay guest journey in two colours; the four-input ceiling arithmetic resolving on screen; a phone-side direct-path test; a registration card with the consent line; the before-and-after direct-share scorecard; the Amsterdam claim and the ECJ reference as documents.
- Working audience promise: A small hotel pays to meet the same guest twice, and the booking site keeps the guest's email. Here is the service that gives the relationship back — and the arithmetic that decides whether anyone can afford to sell it.
- Honest packaging territory: "The hotel pays twice for the same guest" against "the number that decides if this business exists." No earnings claim; no "cancel Booking.com."

## Risks and constraints

- Factual uncertainty: OTA share by US property size is not independently sourced; the 63.4% figure is global and vendor-published; the all-in commission band rests on secondary sources quoting Booking.com partner pages that returned HTTP 403 on 2026-09-03; no observed operator price, hours, or renewal rate.
- Analogy-transfer uncertainty: Agency and revenue-management parallels are bought by larger properties; the 20-40-room transfer is exactly what the validation plan tests.
- Legal or regulatory boundary: OTA partner terms prohibit marketing to masked addresses and require communication on-platform (CLM-008); US CAN-SPAM governs the first-party list; GDPR applies to EU guests' data; state consumer law on rate display. All manageable by consent-first design. US rate-parity contract terms still exist and are not a regulatory question here.
- Permissions, privacy, or guest dependency: Extranet and PMS access, guest data handling, and a property-side consent process are required for any real engagement. No guest is required for the episode.
- Platform or vendor dependency: Booking.com and Expedia change programme rules (the 2026 Genius change is the live example); Google can change free booking links; PMS pricing changes.
- Ethical failure mode: Telling a property to leave OTAs; automating hospitality into spam; claiming credit for seasonal lift; pricing above the ceiling.
- Claims that must not be made: any figure for commission saved or direct share gained as an observed result; the $135,000 or any commission-line figure without its four inputs and the word "illustrative"; the RevPAR-uplift claims of revenue-management vendors; ZipRecruiter salary data as a client price or an income figure (the page could not be re-opened and the figure is retired from this package); "I ran a hospitality business"; that Manav has sold or delivered this service.

## Thirty-day validation plan

1. Run the audit on three properties in the 20-40-room band, one unpaid and two offered at the audit fee. Record booking mix, commission line, the ceiling, the direct-path test results, and whether the property currently captures the guest's own email at check-in. Record actual hours.
2. Put the ceiling number and a retainer below it in front of each owner and record the answer, the objection, and what the owner does today about the commission line.
3. Ask two PMS or channel-manager partner managers and one innkeeper-association contact whether they refer this kind of work and to whom; ask two independent-hotel agencies what their smallest retained property is.

Success signal: At least one owner pays the audit fee; at least one owner agrees to a retainer at or below the modeled ceiling; audit delivery lands within 15 hours.

Kill or redesign condition: No owner in the band will fund a retainer that clears $600 per month; agencies already serve 20-40-room properties at prices the owner prefers; or the audit reveals the recoverable commission is systematically below the model because properties in the band are on base commission with low OTA share. Redesign candidate: move the band to 40-80 rooms or sell the audit alone.
