## Recommendation

Decision: **continue research**

Evidence class: **observed model**

Required disclosure: Hands-on automation services for small firms are **observed** — the platforms define the practice, count it (700+ Zapier Solution Partners), set its threshold (n8n: services as main revenue, three active customers) and list practitioners with hundreds of buyer reviews; the adoption-integration gap is **observed in adjacent surveys** (US federal, German public bank, UK government) but not measured for this buyer; the failure that makes reliability sellable is **observed** in a dated vendor notice and **reported** in one agency's account of its own work. The reliability sprint's scope, its $3,500 fee, its 28 hours, its capacity and its contribution are **modeled**, and no independent price for this work exists — every 2026 range in circulation is seller-authored. The monitoring retainer is a **hypothesis** earned only by incidents in the thirty-day window. No historical EP003 price, income or "few hours a week" figure may return, and no platform valuation, funding round, builder count or category size may be offered as proof that a small firm will buy.

Rationale: This scores 69 and lands in the 65–69 calibration band with every hard gate passing. The bounded premise fixes what the V1 episode got wrong: the buyer is one firm type with one handoff, the retainer is demoted from premise to hypothesis, the seller-authored pricing bands are excluded by name, and the market-size ladder that carried the old hook is rejected as evidence. What replaces them is real: a primary federal survey for the gap, a dated vendor notice for the failure, partner counts and review volumes for the spend, and a documented delivery shape.

It falls one point short for a reason the package cannot argue away. **Nothing in the evidence tells the operator what a small firm pays for reliability work.** The economics factor scores 5 because the model is honest about a number it cannot support, and go-to-market scores 5 because the channels that demonstrably work — vendor directories and tiers — are the ones an entrant does not have. The audience factor is held at 10 by an unmeasurable query signal and by the fact that the loudest attention belongs to people selling the opportunity, not buying the service.

The POV factor is scored at 4 under Step 0.3 and that score is a finding in its own right. Manav's builder-side experience is real, facts-approved and bounded; the synthesis is a good reframing of a mechanism that agencies already write about. It is honest editorial work, not a channel-exclusive claim, and the score says so.

The decision is **continue research** rather than eligible-with-override because the missing evidence is cheap and specific. Ten screen-share interviews with owners and a browser-session sample of marketplace asking prices are a week's work. If they show a nameable consequence and a price band that covers the hours, this candidate clears 70 on its own evidence. If they do not, the owner has learned the thing the old episode never asked.
