## Base relationship

Primary viewer: someone who has done bookkeeping, AR, office management, or customer accounts, deciding whether that can be sold.

Why the narrator has earned the right to advise this person: not from having done this job. No such claim is made in either direction. The right is earned by doing the arithmetic in front of them, reading the sellers' pages and the statutes, and saying where it breaks. The viewer likely knows the work better than anyone selling them a tool, and the script says so.

What the narrator gives away: the split, the ladder, the rules, the arithmetic, the state line, the stop rule, the first move.

What the narrator refuses to pretend is known: whether an owner pays a person when the tool is $99, how many hours a client takes, how often each stuck cause occurs, what any state's law means for this viewer.

What the narrator is curious about at the start: why the money didn't move when the machine did everything right.

What the narrator is prepared to rule by the end: BUILD, bounded, with the stop rule.

## Report and business plan

### Reporting spine

Person, transaction, workflow, place, object, or consequence carrying the story: the unanswered reply.

What changes for that carrier across the episode: unanswered, then explained, then a two-minute fix the skeptic scoffs at, then sorted in a queue, marked on a list, a line on a report, then answered.

Evidence that changes the viewer's understanding: sellers charging for the disputed line and the call while giving away the email.

Question the report answers: what's left to sell once sending is free.

How the opening is resolved: PO corrected, invoice resent, one known state on Friday's report. Not shown paid.

### Business context

Normal economics or operating logic: the owner's time, a clerk by the hour, an agency for a share late.

Popular story: AI solved invoice chasing, or AI killed the job.

Hidden mechanism: the sellers split the bill. Sending is free and the person is priced.

Buyer consequence: cash and a customer, while a reply sits.

Portable operating principle: when the machine makes a job's visible part free, price the part a person still owns, and stop if the buyer doesn't want it.

### Operator blueprint

Buyer: owner or office manager of a small firm billing other businesses on terms, no AR staff.

Offer: a flat monthly fee for working the past-due list under the firm's name.

Result: every late invoice has an owner and a known state each week.

Delivery mechanism: the weekly loop, S13 and S15.

Entry wedge: one client's list for one paid month.

Mature company: about 24 firms in one niche.

Internal operating description: never spoken.

Why the wedge is only the entrance: templates, dispute patterns, and reputation exist only after several clients.

Commercial constraint: the human part has to clear the tool's price, under about 1.6 hours a week.

Operator-market selection rule based on experience, relationships, earned access, buyer understanding, and reachable decision-makers: start in the niche you already worked in, with people who already know you.

Qualified high-consideration buyer rule stated without an industry: as in the beat sheet.

Unsuitable buyer rule: as in the beat sheet.

Three explanatory cases and the distinct customer decision each clarifies: as in the beat sheet.

Company definition with all example industries removed: a monthly desk that owns a small firm's late invoices after the software sends the reminder.

Industry used as the episode's concrete example: none.

Example status: not applicable.

First test: your state's law, five free sorts and offers, one paid month.

First 30-day construction move: the state note, rules sheet, follow-up ladder, sample report, and hours log.

## Governing analogy

Analogy family: **the doorbell.**

Why this family fits the mechanism: a bell only asks for attention. It can't have the conversation that follows. That's the difference between the reminder and the reply.

| Mapping question | Analogy object | Business mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| What moves? | the bell, then the answer at the door | information, then money |
| What blocks it? | nobody home when the customer rings back | missing owner for the wrong PO, W-9, or dispute |
| Who owns the consequence? | the household that never answered: the owner, still unpaid | buyer |
| What changes? | someone is home | the handoff, and who answers |

Geometry, so the image holds on one listen: the firm's software rings the customer's bell. The customer answers and rings back with the problem: wrong PO. That reply is the firm's bell ringing, and nobody is home at the firm. The spine's "nobody home on your side" and the owner-approved "someone has to be home" both read correctly this way. The script must say the ring-back. Without it, "home" points at the wrong house.

Literal explanation that must appear before or with the analogy: the reminder and its price (S04), then the reply and the sellers' plan lists (S06), each before the image does any work.

First use: S04, "The bell is free now."

Callback 1 and the new understanding it adds: S06 and S07, the customer rang back and nobody's home: the reminder isn't the job.

Callback 2: S10, "If a bell is all this door needs, buy the bell": a valid no.

Callback 3: S13, "the doors you never ring": locked accounts as judgment. This is the first to cut if the image tires.

Ending callback and what it resolves: S21, the same reply is answered. Someone is home.

How the ending delivers the operator or business payoff teased before the sting: "That's what they'd be paying for, and now you know what it has to sell for."

Exact natural like-and-subscribe sentence: "If you want more businesses priced against the tools trying to replace them, leave a like, and subscribe for the next one."

Why the audience ask is earned and sounds like the same speaker: it names what the episode just did and asks once.

Other asks deliberately omitted or combined: Canvas, download, sign-off, next-episode tease.

Metaphors excluded to prevent analogy confetti: chasing and hunting language beyond the plain word "chase", collections-as-fishing, the leaky bucket for cash flow, "AR desk as air traffic control", the barber on sellers' own figures.

The beat sheet asked whether five appearances is too many in 3,300 words. Ruling: five beats is acceptable, because each adds a new meaning. S13 is the one to drop if the read says otherwise.
