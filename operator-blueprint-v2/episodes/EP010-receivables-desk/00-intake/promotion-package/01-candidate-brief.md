## Opportunity in one sentence

One operator running AI agents sells a monthly receivables desk to small businesses that invoice
other businesses on terms: every past-due invoice is followed up under the client's own name until
it is paid, disputed, or handed back, so overdue cash comes in without the owner doing the chasing.

## Viewer outcome

- Viewer promise: Understand why small firms let overdue invoices sit, what a human-staffed
  receivables service already sells, what agents change about its cost, and where the legal line
  sits before anyone contacts a client's customers.
- Operator decision: Test, adapt, or reject a receivables desk for one niche, based on the viewer's
  own first test of price, client trust, and collection results.
- Practical capability: Map one client's aging report into a follow-up schedule, draft the escalation
  ladder and hand-back rules, sketch the per-client economics, and run the state licensing check for
  their own state.
- Expected Operator Canvas: Buyer and niche, costly job (cash stuck past 30 days), bounded offer
  (pre-collection follow-up only), agent workflow and human escalations, licensing boundary,
  acquisition path, flat-fee economics, and falsification threshold.

## People

- Viewer/operator: Someone who has done bookkeeping, AR, office management, or customer-account work
  and can set up and supervise email and phone agents. Not safe for someone who cannot read an aging
  report or judge when a customer relationship needs a human.
- Buyer: The owner or office manager of a small US business that invoices other businesses on net
  terms (service firms, trades, agencies, distributors, small manufacturers, professional firms) and
  has no full-time AR staff.
- End customer or beneficiary: The buyer's owner and staff (cash, time); the buyer's customers
  receive the follow-ups.
- Guest or outside participant required: no

## Problem

- Costly problem: Invoices go 30+ days past due and follow-up stops after the first reminder because
  chasing a customer feels personal and nobody owns it.
- Why it matters: Intuit QuickBooks (2026) reports 59% of small businesses with invoices 30+ days
  overdue (47% a year earlier), an average of $17,700 owed among those waiting, and more reliance on
  credit cards among firms with overdue invoices.
- Why now: The overdue share rose sharply in a year; agents can now read a thread, write the next
  message, and place a call for each invoice every day without a clerk's hours.
- Existing alternatives and budget: Free reminders in invoicing software; AR software (Daylit,
  Kolleno, Chaser, Upflow, Paidnice) the business runs itself; a human-staffed service, CollBox, at
  $599–$1,399 a month flat for law and accounting firms (seller terms, context); part-time AR clerks
  at $17–$30 an hour for 5–25 hours a week (2026 postings, old-way spend); collection agencies at
  25–50% contingency after about 90 days.

## Proposed business

- Offer: A monthly receivables desk for invoices past due but not yet placed for collection.
- Customer outcome: Every open past-due invoice on a written schedule; disputes and missing documents
  (wrong PO, W-9, billing address) found and passed to the client; payment promises logged; a weekly
  aging report showing the 30+ day balance and days-sales-outstanding before and after.
- Delivery hypothesis: Agents read the client's aging report and each customer's email thread, send
  follow-ups in the client's name and voice, read replies, classify disputes and promises, and
  place scripted reminder calls where the client allows. The operator sets each client's rules and
  locked accounts, handles escalations, and owns the weekly report. Accounts unpaid after a fixed
  window go back to the client. No threats, no legal action, no settlements, no consumer debts, no
  handling of funds.
- Revenue hypothesis: Modeled scenario. A flat monthly fee of about $300–$500 per client (below
  CollBox's $599 entry tier), or a small share of overdue cash collected. About 30 clients per
  operator.
- Most important unproven assumption: Small owners will let an outside operator contact their
  customers about money, and keep paying for it when free reminders exist.

## Initial synthesis hypothesis

- Parallel A: CollBox, a human-staffed past-due follow-up service for small law and accounting firms
  (flat monthly fee, 90-day window, client can lock accounts, disputes handed back). Supports offer,
  pricing unit, boundary, and hand-back design.
- Parallel B: Part-time AR clerk roles (2026 postings). Supports old-way spend and the scope of the
  job.
- Parallel C: AI AR platforms (Daylit, Kolleno) that send email, calls, and texts. Supports
  component feasibility of agent follow-up.
- New combination: CollBox's done-for-you service, delivered by one operator with agents, for
  businesses outside law and accounting, at a lower flat fee, with dispute triage included.
- Suspected transfer risk: CollBox may depend on professional-firm billing habits; agent follow-up
  may read as robotic and damage relationships; state collection-agency laws may treat the desk as a
  collection agency.

These are research directions, not evidence. The completed research brief and analogy map decide
whether the transfers are valid.

## Narrative potential

- Starting state: An owner with $17,700 owed, a free reminder that already went out, and a second
  email nobody wants to send.
- Inciting change: The overdue share jumps from 47% to 59% in a year; owners lean on credit cards.
- Causal mechanism: Follow-up is unowned, awkward, and lumpy; software sends the first nudge but not
  the third, and does not fix the wrong PO number.
- Operator build: An agent desk that works every invoice daily under the client's name, with human
  escalation and hand-back rules.
- Stakes and tradeoffs: Cash versus customer relationships; flat fee versus share of collections;
  scale versus the state licensing line.
- End state: A before/after aging report on a synthetic client and the viewer's go/no-go test.
- Visual evidence: Aging report, follow-up ladder, agent log, dispute classifier output, state
  licensing map, unit-economics table.

## Audience pull

- Exact or adjacent viewer questions: "how to chase unpaid invoices", "accounts receivable
  outsourcing", "AI collections agent", "start a bookkeeping side business".
- Initial interest signals: Google Trends "unpaid invoices" at a five-year high in mid-2026 (low
  absolute volume, per the 2026-10-13 scout); recurring Indie Hackers and UK forum threads on
  invoice chasing; heavy AR-agent launch activity in 2026.
- Timely tension: Overdue invoices rising while AR agents are being sold to larger finance teams.
- Coverage gap: Coverage is vendor guides and enterprise AR-agent pitches; none walks a small
  operator through selling the outcome, the hand-back rules, and the state licensing check.
- Honest working premise: "Small businesses are owed more and chase it less. Here is how one person
  with agents could run the follow-up for them, and where the line is."

## Known blockers

- State law: Florida requires registration for anyone collecting commercial claims owed to another;
  Washington's collection-agency definition covers commercial claims and its statement-mailing
  exemption ends where other collection efforts begin; Illinois exempts notice in the creditor's
  name only (from 2026-01-01). The boundary must be stated state by state.
- Buyer payment to an outside provider is not yet evidenced beyond seller terms (viewer's first
  test).
- Agent substitute: AR platforms may let owners skip the operator.
