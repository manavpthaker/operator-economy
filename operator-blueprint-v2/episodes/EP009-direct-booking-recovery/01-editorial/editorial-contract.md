## Viewer

Primary viewer/operator: A marketing-and-operations generalist with hospitality judgment. Someone who has worked in or around a property, sold or supported hotel software, or knows an innkeeper well enough to be let into the back office. Can read a booking-mix report, work a booking engine and a Google profile, write a guest message an innkeeper would be proud to send, and hold a money conversation with an owner.

Audience situation: employed and exploring / already building (folding this into an existing hospitality-marketing or PMS-consulting practice)

What this person already knows: That small properties depend on Booking.com and Expedia and pay commission on most stays. That the tools to book direct are cheap. They do not know what the booking site withholds after checkout, and they have never computed what a small property can rationally pay for recovery work.

What this person is trying to decide or accomplish: Whether a 20 to 40 room property's commission line can fund a service worth their time, and how to find out in thirty days.

Starting belief or confusion: That the business is "install the direct-booking stack and price against the commission saved", and that the commission line ($100,000 plus at a 20-room property) is the number that matters.

Practical capability they should have after the episode: Compute a property's commission line and recovery ceiling from four inputs. Run a direct-path test as a guest. Design consent capture at the front desk. Price an audit and a retainer under the ceiling. Decide build, test, or reject on their own cost of time.

## Episode job

One-sentence working promise: A small inn pays a booking site to meet the same guest twice, and the site keeps the guest's email. Here is the practice that gives the inn its guest book back, and the four-number arithmetic that decides whether anyone can afford to sell it.

Episode-specific show string in plain language: A practice you can build for small inns that keep paying to meet their own returning guests.

Concrete situation suitable for a cold open: An innkeeper with twenty rooms tries to send a thank-you note to a good guest. The email on the reservation is a relay alias at guest.booking.com. The relay expires. A year later the same guest returns through the same site and the inn pays the same commission again.

Specific operator or business payoff the cold open will tease before the silent sting: There is a service a small inn would pay for hiding inside that second commission, and one piece of arithmetic that decides whether it is worth the viewer's time to sell it.

Mechanism, proof, and final thesis the cold open must withhold: rented discovery, the unowned post-stay path, the guest-data policy as a design constraint, the ceiling arithmetic, the company name, the BUILD verdict.

Central argument: **The booking site is paid per introduction and keeps the guest's contact, and the front desk is short staffed, so nobody at a small property is paid to earn the second stay. That job can be sold from outside, but only for what it recovers, and what it recovers is a number the viewer can compute from four inputs.**

Narrative question: Who gets paid to stop a small inn paying twice for the same guest, and can that job pay somebody from outside?

Evidence and context required before the central argument can be stated: the commission layers (Booking.com partner help, trade reporting), the rising OTA share (Cloudbeds, attributed) beside HOTREC's lower European figure, the guest-alias policy (Booking.com partner help, primary), and the staffing shortage (AHLA).

More than 15,000 European hotels joined a damages claim. Google's free booking links give a commission-free direct placement that small properties rarely configure. The tooling is cheap and the capacity to run it is absent.

What is at stake for the buyer, operator, or market: For the property, a layered commission on most of its bookings and a guest relationship the channel withholds. For the operator, a ceiling of roughly $1,000 to 1,400 a month at a modeled 20-room property that decides whether the business exists at that size.

The concrete business being taught: A fixed-fee direct-booking audit followed by a flat monthly retainer that keeps a small property findable, bookable, and remembered, and reports direct share of bookings monthly.

Short public category title: **Direct-booking recovery**

Short spoken company name: **a direct-booking practice**

One-sentence plain definition: It helps a small hotel get its returning guests to book direct, instead of paying a booking site to meet them again.

Precise internal operating description: A fixed-fee direct-booking audit followed by a flat monthly retainer under which one operator keeps a 20 to 40 room US independent property findable (Google Business Profile, free booking links, accurate rates), bookable (direct rate parity, mobile booking flow, missed-call fallback), and remembered (consent-based first-party guest capture at check-in and on wifi, post-stay thank-you, review request, seasonal return offer), inside OTA terms and consent law, reporting one number monthly, direct share of bookings before and after, priced under the property's own modeled recoverable-commission ceiling.

Opportunity thesis: what changed, why the problem matters, and what company belongs in the gap: The booking sites are paid per introduction and withhold the guest's contact. The desk is short staffed. So a small property pays to be introduced to its own past guests. The company that belongs in the gap owns the post-stay relationship for a handful of small properties and is paid from what it recovers.

Operator thesis: why this viewer has a credible advantage beyond tool access: The job is hospitality judgment (what a thank-you sounds like when an innkeeper is proud of it), booking-engine literacy, consent hygiene, and the owner conversation. The advantage is being let into the extranet and the guest book, which is a relationship built from hospitality experience, an innkeeper connection, or prior hotel-software work. Tools are commodity.

How AI makes this viable or more viable for one accountable operator: A language model drafts the post-stay messages, an automation tool schedules them off checkout dates, and a report pulls direct share monthly. The drafting and scheduling got cheap. That is what makes six properties a one-person job.

Human judgment and accountability AI does not replace: Voice. Offers. Who gets a message and who does not. Consent handling. The audit recommendation, including no. The owner conversation when the number does not move.

The episode says plainly that at 20 rooms this is a foothold, not a living, and that the business exists up the band if the audits show the ceiling clears the operator's time. ## Operator Economy point of view

What common coverage gets wrong: It is written for hoteliers by the companies selling the tools, it treats the guest-data masking as a nuisance rather than the first design constraint, and it never computes what a small property can rationally pay for recovery work.

What a seasoned operator would notice: The channel that introduces the guest keeps the guest's contact, and the desk has no capacity to build its own. The commission line is the wrong number. The recoverable slice is the right one, and it is small at 20 rooms.

What we believe, stated plainly: A small property pays an introduction fee to meet its own regulars. That is a job. It can be sold from outside for what it recovers, and at the bottom of the band that is a foothold, not a living.

What a reasonable person could disagree with: That a 20 to 40 room owner will pay a retainer at all when the recoverable commission is of the same order. That is the whole business, and it is untested.

Broken incentive, fake solution, or conventional framing we are willing to judge: "Increase your direct bookings" content that sells tools to a desk with no time to run them. The commission line presented as what the property loses.

What would change our mind: Three audits showing properties in the band on base commission with low site share. Agencies already retaining 30-room properties at prices owners prefer. No owner funding a retainer that clears $600 a month.
