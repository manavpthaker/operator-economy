## Story in one sentence

A small inn pays a booking site to meet the same guest twice because the site keeps the guest's email and the desk has no time to ask for it, and the job of earning the second stay can be sold from outside for exactly what it recovers, which at twenty rooms is a foothold and up the band might be a business.

## Point of view and voice architecture

Short spoken company name: a direct-booking practice

One-sentence plain definition: It helps a small hotel get its returning guests to book direct, instead of paying a booking site to meet them again.

Precise internal operating description: in the contract and Canvas, not spoken.

Opportunity thesis: the site is paid per introduction and keeps the contact, the desk is short staffed, so the property pays to meet its own regulars. That job is unowned and can be sold from outside for what it recovers.

Operator thesis: the job is judgment and access. Hospitality experience, an innkeeper relationship, or hotel-software work earns the access. Tools do not.

Entry-wedge thesis: the audit is the test the business rests on, sold as a product with a valid no.

Central thesis to be earned after orientation: nobody at the inn is paid to earn the second stay, and somebody from outside can be, but only for what it recovers, and what it recovers is a number the viewer can compute.

## Tension

What appears true at first: a $100,000-plus commission line is a rich buyer.

What complicates it: the inn needs the sites and will only ever move a slice.

What the evidence changes: the recoverable slice is about a thousand a month at 20 rooms.

What remains uncertain: whether any owner pays, and how wide the viable band is.

Why the viewer should keep listening after the problem is established: the arithmetic that decides whether this is worth their time has not been done by anyone selling it.

## Counterargument and fairness

Fair incumbent case, delivered before any criticism: the sites rent the inn an audience it could never build. That is a fair trade, and the inn should never leave them.

Strongest reasonable objection: if this were worth paying for, somebody would already be selling it.

What the objection gets right: somebody is, at two scales.

Where the proposed model still holds: those providers are bought by properties big enough to fund them, and nobody found sells to the 20-room inn, because the inn can only pay what the job recovers.

Second objection, ruled: if the parts are cheap, why doesn't the inn do it itself? Because the desk has no time, and the list of small jobs belongs to nobody.

What would invalidate the thesis: no owner in the band funds $600 a month, agencies already retain 30-room inns at $1,500 and owners prefer it, or audits show base commission and low share.
