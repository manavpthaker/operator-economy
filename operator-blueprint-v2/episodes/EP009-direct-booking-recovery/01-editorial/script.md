Working promise: A small inn pays a booking site to meet the same guest twice, and the site keeps the guest's email. Here is the practice that gives the inn its guest book back, and the four-number arithmetic that decides whether anyone can afford to sell it.

Short public category title: Direct-booking recovery

Short spoken company name: **a direct-booking practice**

One-sentence plain definition: It helps a small hotel get its returning guests to book direct, instead of paying a booking site to meet them again.

Internal operating description: A fixed-fee direct-booking audit followed by a flat monthly retainer under which one operator keeps a 20 to 40 room US independent property findable, bookable, and remembered, re-books past guests from consented first-party data inside OTA terms and consent law, and reports one number monthly, direct share of bookings, priced under the property's own modeled recoverable-commission ceiling.

Show thesis: A capable professional with hospitality judgment can use AI to run the post-stay relationship for a handful of small properties as a business of one, because the drafting and scheduling got cheap and the judgment did not.

Opportunity thesis: The booking sites are paid per introduction and keep the guest's contact, the front desk is short staffed, so a small property pays to be introduced to its own past guests. That gap is an unowned job, and it can be bought from outside for what it recovers.

Operator thesis: The job needs hospitality judgment, booking-engine literacy, and the ability to hold a money conversation with an owner. Tool access is not the advantage. Being let into the extranet and the guest book is, and that is a relationship.

Entry-wedge thesis: A fixed-fee audit that computes the property's ceiling on real inputs, tests the direct path, and returns a retainer, audit-only, or not-this-property recommendation is the responsible first sale, because it is the test the whole business rests on.

## S03: context runway

Purpose: how a small inn fills rooms, why the sites are a fair trade, how commission layers, what changed, and who feels it

Claim IDs: C001, C004, C005, C006, C013, C037

Required qualification: Cloudbeds named. Booking.com partner help named for mechanics. The base-rate range attributed to trade reporting. The European claim marked as Europe. The staffing survey attributed to the association.

New terms introduced in plain language: commission, preferred partner, visibility booster, Genius

### Narration

Start with how a small inn fills its rooms, because it's worth being fair to the system before we pick at it.

A traveller opens Booking dot com or Expedia, types a town and some dates, and compares. So the inn lists there. The site puts the property in front of people the inn could never reach on its own and takes a commission on every stay. She rents an audience she couldn't build, and that's a fair trade.

The commission is where it gets layered. Booking dot com's own partner help says it's a set percentage of the whole booking, fees included, charged at checkout, and that it goes up if the property joins one of its visibility programmes. It doesn't publish the base rate. Trade reporting puts that around fifteen percent in most European markets. The preferred badge and the visibility booster add to it, and the Genius programme is a discount the hotel funds from its own rate.

Then three things changed. Cloudbeds, which sells hotel software, reports the share of independent hotel reservations coming through these sites rose in twenty twenty five, to about sixty three percent worldwide. Early in twenty twenty six, Booking dot com changed how Genius placement works, so the minimum discount no longer buys the visibility it used to. And in Europe, more than fifteen thousand hotels have joined a damages claim against Booking dot com over price parity clauses. That's Europe, not here, but hotels are acting at scale.

Who feels it? The owner running twenty to forty rooms with no marketing hire and a front desk that's short staffed. About two thirds of the hotels the American Hotel and Lodging Association surveyed reported staffing shortages.

## S06: how much of the inn is rented

Purpose: size the rented share with Cloudbeds and HOTREC both visible, and turn the unknown US figure into the first audit measurement

What it does not establish: what a US property in the band actually runs

Sequence ruling: the inn is renting most of its demand and does not know its own number

### Narration

First, how much of the inn's business is actually rented.

Cloudbeds, from about ninety million bookings, puts the share of independent hotel bookings arriving through the sites at about sixty three percent, worldwide. Cloudbeds sells the software hotels use to take bookings direct, so it has a view on this the way a dentist has a view on flossing.

For balance, HOTREC, the European hotel association, studied three thousand hotels and found direct channels still carried about half of overnight stays there. So somewhere between half and two thirds of a small property's bookings arrive with a commission attached. And nobody has published what a thirty room inn in the United States actually runs. That's the first thing the audit measures.

Same data, one more thing. Bookings through the sites cancelled at about twenty two percent. Direct bookings, about eleven. So the rented booking costs more and shows up less often. The inn is renting most of its demand, at a price, and doesn't know its own number.

## S07: what the site keeps

Purpose: the guest-data constraint, and the guest book analogy introduced after the literal mechanism

Investment-thesis function: costly problem and design constraint

What the evidence establishes: Booking.com hides guest email behind an alias and asks partners to stay on-platform (primary). The window durations are trade reporting (secondary).

What it does not establish: guest consent rates or re-booking behaviour

### Narration

Second, what the site keeps.

Old inns had a guest book by the door. You signed it, and the innkeeper had your name, where you'd come from, and a reason to write to you. That book was the inn's.

Booking dot com's own partner help says it doesn't share private email addresses. Both sides see an alias ending in guest dot booking dot com, and partners are asked to keep the conversation on the platform. The trade press puts the messaging window at about seven days after checkout, and Expedia's at about forty five.

So the guest book now lives at the booking site. The inn served the guest and doesn't have his email.

Which means the recovery job has a design constraint before it has a tool. You can't win a guest back from a relay address, and under the site's rules you're not supposed to try. What the inn needs first isn't an email tool. It's a guest book of its own, at the front desk, that the guest chooses to sign.

### Narration

Fair question here. If the parts to fix this are cheap, why doesn't the inn just do it?

Which raises the obvious objection. If this were worth paying for, somebody would already be selling it.

So the job is real and it's paid for, by properties big enough to fund it. I could not find anyone selling it to the twenty room inn. It's that the inn can only pay what the job recovers, and nobody selling this has published what that is.

## S10: the ceiling

Purpose: the buyer-side arithmetic the V1 episode never computed. Four inputs, a recoverable commission, a ceiling under which any retainer must sit.

Investment-thesis function: costly problem quantified and bounded

Claim IDs: C016, C017, C018, C019, C020, C021

What the evidence establishes: nothing observed. A transparent model on stated inputs.

What it does not establish: that any property achieves a ten point shift, or that the owner reasons this way

Sequence ruling: the ceiling tells the operator in four numbers whether a property can afford them before an hour is spent

### Narration

So let's compute it. Here's the model. It's illustrative, I'm making the inn up, and every input is one you'd swap for the real property's numbers.

Twenty rooms. A hundred and eighty dollars a night on average. Seventy percent full across the year. That's about nine hundred and twenty thousand dollars of room revenue.

Say sixty three percent of that comes through the sites, the Cloudbeds figure. That's about five hundred and eighty thousand dollars of rented bookings. At an all in commission of eighteen to twenty two percent, my assumption from the reported bands, the inn is paying the sites roughly a hundred and five to a hundred and thirty thousand dollars a year.

That's where the old version of this argument stops. It's the wrong number, because the inn is never going to move all of it. It needs the sites.

The real question is what a recovery service can shift. Say it moves ten points of the mix, from sixty three percent rented to fifty three. That's about ninety two thousand dollars of revenue now booked direct. Commission saved on that, at eighteen to twenty two percent, is about sixteen and a half to twenty thousand dollars a year. Take off the cost of the direct channel itself, call it three and a half to five percent, the low end being one vendor's own figure. What's left is roughly twelve to seventeen thousand dollars a year.

A thousand to fourteen hundred dollars a month. That's the ceiling. It's the most a twenty room inn can rationally pay anyone to do this job, and the retainer has to sit under it.

Two things move it. A forty room property doubles it, to roughly two thousand to twenty eight hundred a month. A five point shift instead of ten halves it, to about five to seven hundred.

The ten points is a hypothesis nobody has measured. But the ceiling tells you, in four numbers, whether a property can afford you before you've spent an hour on it.
