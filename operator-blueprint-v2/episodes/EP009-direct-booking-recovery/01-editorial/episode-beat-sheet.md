Working promise: A small inn pays a booking site to meet the same guest twice, and the site keeps the guest's email. Here is the practice that gives the inn its guest book back, and the four-number arithmetic that decides whether anyone can afford to sell it.

Central question: the site introduces the guest and keeps the email. When the guest comes back, who at the inn is paid to make sure the second booking comes to the inn? And if nobody, can somebody from outside get paid to do it?

Central thesis to earn later: nobody at the inn is. It can be sold from outside, but only for what it recovers, and what it recovers is a number.

Opportunity thesis: the unowned post-stay job, sellable for what it recovers.

Operator thesis: judgment and access, not tools.

Entry-wedge thesis: the audit is the test, sold as a product with a valid no.

Company-level verdict: BUILD (recommended, bounded)

Recurring person, transaction, workflow, or customer consequence: one guest's two stays at a twenty-room inn.

## Opening ladder

### B00: cold open

Target range: 20 to 40 seconds (about 165 words, roughly 60 to 70 seconds at conversational pace, accepted as the situation needs the year to pass; see first-listen note)

Ordinary situation, transaction, or consequence: an innkeeper closing out a reservation and sending a thank-you note.

Specific person or role carrying it: the innkeeper, the guest.

What looks normal at first: a good guest, a routine note.

What becomes strange, costly, or unresolved: the email is a relay at guest.booking.com, the relay expires, the same guest returns through the site a year later and the inn pays the same commission again.

Specific operator or business payoff teased before the sting: a service a small inn would pay for hiding inside that second commission, and one piece of arithmetic that decides whether it is worth the viewer's time to sell it.

Why that payoff is concrete enough to create a reason to stay: it names a sellable service and a decision the viewer can make with arithmetic.

Mechanism, proof, and final ruling deliberately withheld: rented discovery, the alias policy as a rule, the ceiling, the company name, BUILD.

Final question or consequence left open: what did that second stay cost her, and who gets paid to stop it?

Facts or outcomes this illustration is prohibited from inventing: any figure for the inn beyond "twenty rooms", any direct-share result, any guest quotation.

Why this is a situation and payoff teaser rather than a fully argued thesis: it shows the event and names the payoff. It does not explain why the site keeps the email, what the job recovers, or what to build.

Runtime note: the cold open runs about 165 words, which at 140 to 165 words per minute is 60 to 70 seconds, above the 20 to 40 second target. The beat needs the second stay to land, and the second stay needs a year to pass. Recorded here as a planned exception for the owner to accept or trim at the cold read.

### B02: brand and episode promise

Target range: 20 to 30 seconds

Fixed brand string present without alteration: yes

Episode-specific business named in plain language: a direct-booking practice, with its one-sentence definition.

Practical viewer result promised: four numbers from any small property, what it can honestly pay, whether that is worth the viewer's time.

Terms deliberately withheld until the viewer has context: extranet, mix shift, direct share, retainer, ceiling.

### B03: context runway

Target range: 60 to 120 seconds (about 285 words, roughly 105 to 120 seconds)

How the normal system works: the sites rent the inn an audience and take a commission per stay.

Why reasonable people use it: reach the inn could never build. A fair trade.

What changed: share rose in 2025 (Cloudbeds), Genius visibility changed in 2026, more than 15,000 European hotels joined a damages claim.

Who now feels the consequence: the owner running 20 to 40 rooms with a short-staffed desk (AHLA).

Why it matters now: the commission is layered on most of the inn's bookings and the desk cannot build its own path.

First evidence that changes the viewer's understanding: Booking.com's own partner help on how commission works, and the reported base band.

Plain-language definition introduced only after its concrete example: "commission" after the trade is described, "visibility programmes" after the layers.

### B04: central question

The one investigation question the episode will answer: who at the inn is paid to earn the second stay, and can somebody from outside be?

What the viewer knows before this question is asked: the trade, the layers, the rising share, the short desk.

Why the answer is not already obvious: the obvious answer is "the inn should do it", and the runway has just shown the desk has no time.

### B05: earned thesis or reveal

Main thesis: nobody at the inn is paid to earn the second stay, because the site is paid per introduction and the desk is short staffed, so the inn pays to meet its own regulars. That job can be sold from outside for what it recovers, and what it recovers is a number.

Evidence and explanation that must appear before it: B03.

Fair case for the existing system: the sites are a fair trade for reach.

Point where that fair case stops being sufficient: after checkout, when the relationship is nobody's job.

Plainspoken ruling: "That gap is a job."

Operator implication: it can only be sold for what it recovers, and the viewer will compute that.
