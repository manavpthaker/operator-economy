## Base relationship

Primary viewer: A capable operations, CX or product generalist who has read a queue and knows a founder or two.

Why the narrator has earned the right to advise this person: He has put AI on a ticket queue as a product manager, run customer experience, and co-founded a store that answered "where is my order." He has not sold this as a service and says so.

What the narrator gives away: The whole job, the buyer band, the never list, the payback arithmetic, the failing base case, the kill conditions and the ten-dollar first move.

What the narrator refuses to pretend is known: Whether a small store will pay; whether the wizard leaves a job; the hours; the lift; Gorgias's per-ticket figure.

What the narrator is curious about at the start: Why a tool that gets paid per resolution handed back the easiest ticket in the business.

What the narrator is prepared to rule by the end: BUILD, with four kill conditions and the case against stated.

## Opening voice map

### Cold open

Ordinary situation: A late-night "where is my order" message.

Specific practical details, in the order revealed: eleven at night; the chat box; the store bought an agent for this; it reads and hands over; no human until nine; the month-end bill with a short line; two people still answering the question.

What the narrator notices without explaining yet: That a machine paid per resolution handed back the easiest resolution there is.

Temperature: curious, mildly bemused

Specific operator or business payoff teased before the sting: "Between the day that store bought the AI and the day it works, there's a job. I think one person can sell it, at a fixed price, to stores the vendor won't set up by hand."

Plain language that makes the payoff concrete without explaining or proving the thesis: a job, a fixed price, a buyer.

Why the final pre-sting line creates a clean handoff into the identity break: "The strange part is who left the job undone." is a question with a person-shaped answer the report will name.

Unresolved question or consequence: who left it undone.

Abstract terms deliberately prohibited: implementation, configuration, onboarding threshold, verified resolution.

Verdict deliberately withheld: BUILD; the vendor's ability to close the gap.

## Report and business plan

### Reporting spine

Person, transaction, workflow, place, object, or consequence carrying the story: the eleven o'clock ticket; the month-end bill; the vendor's two pages.

What changes for that carrier across the episode: handed back → sorted into safe, conditional, never → answered, with the refund carried to a person.

Evidence that changes the viewer's understanding: the go-live guide; sixty days versus 48 hours; the $3M line; the form that does not list a person; the failing base case.

Question the report answers: who does the setup for the store too small to get a person, and can one person sell it.

How the opening is resolved: the same ticket at the same hour, answered.

### Business context

Normal economics or operating logic: the store pays for the tool and for each resolution; the vendor's revenue rides on resolutions.

Popular story: "the AI handles your tickets."

Hidden mechanism: the agent resolves nothing until someone writes its knowledge and rules, and the vendor writes them by hand only above three million.

Buyer consequence: paying for a handful of resolutions and two people.

Portable operating principle: when a vendor prices on outcomes and leaves the outcome-producing work with the customer, the work is a business, until the vendor decides otherwise.

### Operator blueprint

Buyer: a sub-$3M Shopify brand in the 1,000–5,000 ticket band with the agent switched on.

Offer: a paid audit credited against one fixed-fee implementation.

Result: a configured agent, a never list, a before-and-after on the vendor's meter.

Delivery mechanism: audit, decide, draft, build, test, go live, read tickets for two weeks, report, hand back.

Entry wedge: the audit and one implementation.

Mature company: a steady run of brands on both help desks, repeat work, a retainer only if proven.

Internal operating description: Canvas §9.

Why the wedge is only the entrance: the second vendor, repeat work, agency relationships and the retainer each need proof the wedge produces.

Commercial constraint: fixed fee priced against the store's payback; two a month; actions off.

Operator-market selection rule: start with the stores you can already reach and the agencies that serve them; qualify with the vendor's own question and the ticket band.

Qualified buyer rule stated without an industry: as approved (vertical company).

Unsuitable buyer rule: as approved.

Three explanatory cases and the distinct customer decision each clarifies: order status (safe); returns (conditional); broken-item refund (never).

Company definition with all example industries removed: as approved.

Industry used as the episode's concrete example: Shopify DTC brands on Gorgias or Zendesk.

Example status: approved recommendation (no product category named)

Plain line preventing the example from becoming universal market advice: "If they give you a person, you're not my buyer."

First test: run the wizard on a ten-dollar sandbox and write down the residue.

First 30-day construction move: sandbox, three ticket reviews, one unpaid implementation with a stopwatch, two paid offers at a fee written down first.

First 90-day proof move: deliver what got bought; reprice from measured hours; log wrong answers; no retainer until a founder pays for the review.

Final episode verdict: BUILD

Client-level sub-decisions preserved: refuse ("keep the wizard and save the fee"); narrow (email first); stop (any money error).
