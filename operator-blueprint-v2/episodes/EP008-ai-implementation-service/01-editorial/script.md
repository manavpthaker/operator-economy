### Narration

It's eleven at night, and somebody just typed "where is my order" into the chat box on a small online store.

The store has an answer for this. It bought one. The founder switched on the AI agent that came with the help desk, because that's what it was sold to handle.

The agent reads the message. And hands it to a human.

There is no human. There'll be one at nine.

At the end of the month the help desk bills the store for every ticket the AI resolved. It's a short line. The founder is paying two people to answer "where is my order," and paying a machine for the handful it did.

Between the day that store bought the AI and the day it works, there's a job. I think one person can sell it, at a fixed price, to stores the vendor won't set up by hand.

The strange part is who left the job undone.

## S03: context runway

Purpose: explain the normal system, what changed, who is affected, and why the question matters before the main thesis

Claim IDs: C001, C002, C005

Required qualification: "as of when I checked" on the plan ladder; attribute the adoption figures

New terms introduced in plain language: help desk (after the queue); per resolution (after "you pay each time it actually closes a ticket")

### Narration

Start with how support works at a store this size, because the system makes sense before anyone picks at it.

A small brand on Shopify gets the same few questions over and over. Where's my order. Can I return this. One to five people answer them inside a help desk, a tool that pulls email, chat and social into one queue on top of the store. The two big ones are Gorgias and Zendesk, and they run from ten bucks a month for fifty tickets up to about nine hundred a month at five thousand, as of when I checked.

In the last couple of years both of them put an AI agent inside that queue. It reads the ticket, answers if it can, and hands it to a person if it can't. For a founder with two agents and a queue that never empties, switching it on is the obvious move. I'd have switched it on.

Two things changed.

The first is how the AI gets paid for. Both vendors moved to charging per resolution. You don't pay for the agent. You pay each time it actually closes a ticket. So the vendor's revenue rides on resolutions.

The second is who's buying. Fewer than one in five small businesses is using AI at all. That's a government survey and a bank looking at what its small-business customers actually paid for. So these brands are early, and they're finding out what the thing does out of the box.

Who feels that? The founder paying for resolutions that mostly aren't happening. The two agents still answering "where is my order." And the customer at eleven at night.

## S07: what the agent needs

Purpose: the vendor's own guide; the governing analogy after the literal list

Investment-thesis function: opportunity (costly problem)

Claim IDs: C004

What the evidence establishes: the setup is a documented job

What it does not establish: what the wizard does or does not do

Sequence ruling: the handbook is the job

Analogy or comedy job: the temp with no handbook, introduced after the list

### Narration

The vendor answers that question itself, in its own setup guide.

Gorgias's go-live guide says the agent needs at least one source of knowledge before you can turn it on. It says build skills for your highest-volume questions, and it names them. Returns, order status, cancellations. It says decide your handover rules, meaning when the agent gives up and hands the ticket to a person. Test it in a playground first. And in the first week, review at least ten tickets by hand.

That list is a job. Somebody reads the queue, writes down what the store's policies actually are, decides which questions the machine gets to answer, and sits with it for a week.

Here's how I think about it. The store didn't install software. It hired a temp. The temp showed up on day one with no handbook. Nobody told it the return policy, or which questions to bring to the manager. It's fast, it never sleeps, and it will confidently answer a refund question wrong. And the agency that placed the temp bills you every time it closes a ticket.

The handbook is the job. And on a two-person support team, nobody's job is writing the handbook.

## S08: bought, not working

Purpose: the enterprise finding inside its label; buy beats build; callback 1

Investment-thesis function: opportunity (established proof, direction only)

Claim IDs: C003

What the evidence establishes: at large companies, bought AI mostly fails for lack of integration; bought beats built

What it does not establish: any small-brand rate

Sequence ruling: buying is not the finish line

### Narration

The same shape shows up at the top of the market, and it matters here, because the missing handbook isn't a small-store problem.

A group at MIT looked at GenAI projects inside large companies last year. About ninety five percent of them produced nothing you could find on a P and L. Not because the models were weak. Because the tools never got wired into how the work actually happens. And the projects that did work were mostly bought, not built. Purchased tools succeeded about two thirds of the time. Internal builds, about a third as often.

That's big companies. Nobody has measured the same thing for a brand with two support agents, so at this size I'm calling it a hypothesis, not a fact.

But the direction holds. Buying beats building. And buying isn't the finish line. The temp still needs the handbook.

## S09: three tickets

Purpose: safe, conditional, never; the never list as the product; Klarna for the trade-off

Investment-thesis function: opportunity (the three decisions)

Claim IDs: C004 (the named intents), C013

What the evidence establishes: the three intents the vendor names are three different decisions; Klarna's public reversal

What it does not establish: any share of a queue; anything about small-brand demand

Sequence ruling: the never list is the product; the rest is configuration

### Narration

What goes in the handbook, then? Take the three questions the vendor named, because each one is a different decision.

"Where's my order." The agent looks up the tracking and answers. Safe, nearly every time. That's the ticket you automate first, and it's most of the queue.

"Can I return this." Depends. Inside the window, the agent quotes the policy and starts it. Outside it, or if the customer's angry, it hands over. That's a conditional, and the conditions are yours to write.

"I want a refund, it arrived broken." The agent should not touch that. Not because it can't move money. Because a wrong refund is real money out of a small store, and a wrong no is a customer gone. That one goes on the never list until the founder signs for it, per action.

That never list is the product. The rest is configuration.

And if you want to see what happens when nobody writes it, Klarna did the experiment in public. Its AI handled two thirds of customer inquiries in its first month. The following year its CEO said out loud that cost had been too big a factor and quality had gone down, and they started hiring people back. That's a company thousands of times our store's size, so it's not evidence about our buyer. It's the whole trade-off in one sentence. Automate too little and you wasted the fee. Automate too much and you ship wrong refunds.

## S10: who does it today

Purpose: the vendor's own pages; the fair case; the agencies; the shop; the form; no price

Investment-thesis function: opportunity (missing ownership; two observed operating layers)

Claim IDs: C009, C012, C008, C011, C023

What the evidence establishes: the vendor does and charges for this job above $3M; agencies and a specialist shop sell it; the program's form lists agency types; nobody publishes a price

What it does not establish: that solos are refused; any price

Sequence ruling: fair to the vendor; the small store is unowned

### Narration

Fine, so it's a job. Who does it today?

Start with the vendor. Gorgias sells onboarding. Two to five sessions with an implementation manager. A premium program whose pitch is automate half your support in fifty days. Professional services, listed as AI Agent setup, price on request. And its own onboarding page says the average time to launch is sixty days.

Sixty days, and a dedicated person. That's the job, done by the people who built the product.

Who gets that? The vendor's own plan guide draws the line. Under three million in annual revenue, you set it up yourself. From three million, you get a person.

The same guide says most brands go live within forty eight hours on the self-serve resources. Both of those are the vendor, and both are true. Going live and working are two different days, and the sixty-day version is only sold above the line.

It's easy to make the vendor the villain here, and it isn't one. You cannot send an implementation manager to a ten-dollar-a-month customer. The wizard is the fair answer to that. It just leaves a lot of stores holding the handbook.

Go one layer down. The vendor keeps a directory of certified agencies that sell exactly this, setup and optimization, and the partner program promises those agencies prioritized leads whenever a customer needs their services. One of them, a shop in Barcelona, publishes three implementation tiers and a monthly optimization retainer. Prices on a call.

So the job is real, and it's sold, by the vendor and by the agencies. What I cannot tell you is a single price anybody has paid. Nobody publishes one. And I couldn't find one person doing it solo at a price they'd say out loud.

Look who the program is built for. The application form lists development agencies, marketing agencies, call centers, affiliates. It does not list a person.

Which leaves a store doing two thousand tickets a month with a wizard, a sixty-day job, and two agents.

## S13: draft is cheap

Purpose: the AI and human split; the meter counts containment; callback 2

### Narration

Which brings up the split. What does the AI do in this business, and what do you do?

The vendor's agent answers the tickets, and the store already pays for it. The second AI is whatever general model you use to draft. You hand it the store's policies and macros, and it drafts the knowledge base in an hour instead of a week. That's the part that got cheap, and it's why this is a one-person job now.

Then you do the part that didn't get cheap. You read every rule that touches money, shipping promises or cancellations, and you decide it. You leave the money-moving actions off unless the founder signs for each one. And for two weeks after go-live, you read the tickets, because the meter will tell you it's resolving things and it will not tell you whether the answers were right.

Drafting the handbook got cheap. Deciding what goes in it didn't.

### Narration

What do you sell first?

One implementation, for one store, fixed scope, fixed fee. You walk in through a paid audit.

Thirty days of their tickets, sorted and marked safe, conditional, or never. The knowledge base and the guidance, written from their policies. Skills, handover rules, a test suite in the playground. Go live on email first. Two weeks of tuning. Then a report.

And sometimes the honest report is, don't do this yet. If most of their queue is the kind of question the machine should never touch, you tell them to keep the wizard and save the money. That's the reason the next founder trusts you.
