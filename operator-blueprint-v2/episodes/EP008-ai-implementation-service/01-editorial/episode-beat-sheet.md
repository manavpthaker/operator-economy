Working promise: The help desk gets paid every time its AI resolves a ticket, but the work that makes it resolve anything is left with the store, and this episode shows that job, what it is worth to the store, what it costs one person to do, and the one thing that could make it disappear.

Central question: If the vendor only gets paid when the AI resolves a ticket, why is the work that makes it resolve anything left with the store, and who does that work for a store too small to get a person?

Central thesis to earn later: The vendor sells the engine and bills by the mile, installs it by hand above three million, and hands everyone else a wizard; the job between switched on and working is real and already sold; for the small store nobody owns it; that gap is a business on a platform that can close it.

Opportunity thesis: Outcome pricing put the vendor's revenue on resolutions and left the setup with the store; the setup is sold above the line and unowned below it.

Operator thesis: Judgment about tickets and policy, learned on a queue, is the advantage; the settings screen is not.

Entry-wedge thesis: One paid audit and one fixed-fee implementation, measured on the vendor's meter, producing the audit method and the never list.

Company-level verdict: **BUILD**

Recurring person, transaction, workflow, or customer consequence: The eleven o'clock "where is my order" ticket, joined mid-body by the broken-item refund question.

How the promise names or implies one accountable operator: "what it costs you to do."

How AI changes the work in this episode: The vendor's agent is the runtime; a general model drafts the handbook; the operator decides what goes in it and what never does.

What would have to be true for the model to support a sustainable business of one: About 24 implementations a year at about $5,500, or a proven second product, with hours at or under 45; said aloud with the base case recorded as failing.

Reachable-share logic: Two a month against millions of merchants; size is not the constraint, reach is, and it is untested.

Qualified buyer rule stated without an example industry: Sub-$3M Shopify brand on Gorgias or Zendesk, 1,000–5,000 tickets a month, agent switched on, founder signs per action.

Unsuitable buyer rule: Above the line; under ~1,000 tickets; never-dominated queue; no sign-off; wants a build.

Three explanatory markets and the different buying decision each clarifies: Vertical company; the three cases are ticket intents: order status (safe), returns (conditional), broken-item refund (never, per-action sign-off).

Company definition with all example industries removed: A practice that makes a small store's bundled helpdesk AI answer what it should, hand over what it must, and never touch what it must not, for stores below the vendor's hands-on line.

## Opening ladder

### B00: cold open

Target range: 20–40 seconds (about 140 words)

Ordinary situation, transaction, or consequence: A "where is my order" message at eleven at night at a small online store.

Specific person or role carrying it: The customer; the AI agent; the founder who switched it on.

What looks normal at first: The store bought an AI agent for exactly this and switched it on.

What becomes strange, costly, or unresolved: The agent hands the easiest ticket in the business to a human who is not there; at month end the store pays the vendor for the handful of tickets the agent did resolve.

Specific operator or business payoff teased before the sting: Between the day the store bought the AI and the day it works there is a job; one person could sell it at a fixed price to the stores the vendor will not set up by hand.

Why that payoff is concrete enough to create a reason to stay: It names a job, a price shape, and a buyer.

Mechanism, proof, and final ruling deliberately withheld: Why the agent hands tickets back; per-resolution pricing; the $3M line; the job sold above it; the vendor's ability to close the gap; BUILD.

Final question or consequence left open: "The strange part is who left the job undone."

Facts or outcomes this illustration is prohibited from inventing: A store name, product, resolution rate, dollar loss, founder quote, or post-implementation outcome beyond "answered" and "handed to a person."

Why this is a situation and payoff teaser rather than a fully argued thesis: It shows one ticket and one bill and names a job; it does not say why the ticket was handed back or who the vendor sets up by hand.

### B01: silent identity sting

Target range: 3–6 seconds

Narration: none

Editorial purpose: separate the cold open from the report and establish the recurring show identity

### B02: brand and episode promise

Target range: 20–30 seconds

Fixed brand string present without alteration: yes

Episode-specific business named in plain language: a helpdesk setup practice, with its one-sentence definition.

Practical viewer result promised: What the job is worth to the store, what it costs you to do, and the one thing that could make the business disappear.

Terms deliberately withheld until the viewer has context: "verified resolution," "handover," "skills," "onboarding threshold," "never list."

### B03: context runway

Target range: 60–120 seconds (about 290 words)

How the normal system works: One to five agents answer the same few questions inside a help desk that pulls email, chat and social into one queue on top of the store; the two big ones are Gorgias and Zendesk; plans run from ten dollars a month for fifty tickets to about nine hundred at five thousand, as of when checked; both now bundle an AI agent that reads the ticket, looks up the order, answers, and hands over what it cannot.

Why reasonable people use it: The founder with two agents and a queue that never empties switches it on; the host says he would have too.

What changed: Both vendors charge for the AI per resolution, so the vendor's revenue rides on resolutions; fewer than one in five small businesses uses AI at all (government survey; bank transaction data), closer to one in seven in retail, so these buyers are early.

Who now feels the consequence: The founder paying for a handful of resolutions; the two agents; the customer at eleven.

Why it matters now: The vendor's money depends on the thing the store has to configure, and the store is finding out what defaults do.

First evidence that changes the viewer's understanding: The plan ladder (the buyer band) and the adoption figures (the buyer is early).

Plain-language definition introduced only after its concrete example: "help desk" after the queue is described; "resolution" after the bill.

### B04: central question

The one investigation question the episode will answer: If the vendor only gets paid when the AI resolves a ticket, why is the work that makes it resolve anything left with the store, and who does that work for a store too small to get a person?

What the viewer knows before this question is asked: What a help desk is, that the agent is bundled, that it is paid per resolution, and that the buyers are early.

Why the answer is not already obvious: The store assumes switching on is the setup; the vendor is paid to want resolutions.

### B05: earned thesis or reveal

Main thesis: Engine by the mile; installed by hand above three million; a wizard for everyone else; a real, already-sold job that nobody owns for the small store; a gap that is a business, on a platform that can close it.

Evidence and explanation that must appear before it: B03.

Fair case for the existing system: Per-resolution pricing is a genuinely good deal for the store; the wizard is the reasonable answer to a ten-dollar customer.

Point where that fair case stops being sufficient: The vendor's own guide lists work the wizard is not shown to do, and the vendor's hands-on version averages sixty days.

Plainspoken ruling: "For the small store, nobody owns it. That's the gap, and it's a business."

Operator implication: The business has to be built for a platform that can delete it; the rest of the episode says how.
