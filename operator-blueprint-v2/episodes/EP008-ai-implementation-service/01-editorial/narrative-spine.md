## Story in one sentence

A small store switches on the AI agent that came with its help desk, pays the vendor for every ticket it resolves, and watches it hand the easiest ticket in the business back to nobody at eleven at night, because the vendor sells the setup by hand only above three million in revenue and everyone else gets a wizard, and the job left in that gap is one a single experienced person could sell, at a fixed price, until the vendor decides to close it.

## Point of view and voice architecture

Short spoken company name: a helpdesk setup practice

One-sentence plain definition: It makes the AI that came with a small store's help desk actually answer customers, and decides which questions it never should.

Precise internal operating description: Canvas §9.

Opportunity thesis: Outcome pricing put the vendor's revenue on resolutions and left the resolution-producing setup with the store; the vendor does that setup by hand above $3M and hands the rest a wizard; the job is already sold above the line; nobody owns it below.

Operator thesis: The value is judgment about tickets and policy, learned by running a queue, not by owning the settings screen every buyer already has.

Entry-wedge thesis: One paid audit and one fixed-fee implementation, measured on the vendor's meter, producing the audit method and the never list that survive whatever the vendor ships next.

Central thesis to be earned after orientation: The vendor sells the engine and bills by the mile, installs it by hand above three million, and hands everyone else a wizard; the job between switched on and working is real and already sold; for the small store nobody owns it; that gap is a business, and it sits on a platform that can close it, which decides how the business must be built.

Common bad framing: "AI implementation" as a market-size ladder from a consulting giant down to a Medium blog; or "start an AI agency" as a label without a queue.

What a fair skeptic gets right: The vendor ships a wizard and says most brands go live within 48 hours on self-serve resources; nobody has shown a small store paying a solo operator; the vendor could ship a release that does most of this.

Final ruling: BUILD, because the cost of finding out is ten dollars and forty hours and every stop rule fires inside thirty days.

Company-level verdict: **BUILD**

Target of earned contempt: Outcome pricing marketed as alignment while the outcome-producing work is routed to agencies for brands big enough to be worth a lead; and "AI agency" content that sells the label. Used once each, after the fair case.

People protected from that contempt: The founder, the two agents, the customer at eleven, the viewer, and the vendor's implementation managers, who are doing a real job for the brands they are assigned.

Governing analogy family: **The temp with no handbook.** The store did not install software; it hired a temp who showed up on day one with no handbook, nobody told it the return policy, and nobody told it which questions to bring to the manager. The agency that placed the temp bills the store every time it closes a ticket.

What moves, what blocks it, who owns the consequence, and what changes: Tickets move. The missing handbook (knowledge, guidance) and the missing "bring these to me" rule (handover, never list) block them. The founder and the agents own the consequence; the customer feels it. What changes is that somebody writes the handbook, writes the bring-to-me list, and sits with the temp for two weeks.

Planned callbacks and how each deepens the mechanism:
1. **Introduced** after the vendor's own go-live guide is read aloud (S07), so the literal mechanism precedes the comparison.
2. **Callback 1** at the enterprise finding (S08): buying beats building, and buying is not the finish line; the temp still needs the handbook.
3. **Callback 2** at the AI/human split (S13): drafting the handbook got cheap; deciding what goes in it did not.
4. **Ending callback** (S20): the eleven o'clock ticket gets answered and the refund question gets carried to a person, because somebody wrote the handbook and the bring-to-me list. "Same tool, same bill. Somebody wrote the handbook."

## Report opening

Concrete cold-open situation: Eleven at night, "where is my order" arrives at a small online store's chat box.

Person, transaction, workflow, or consequence carrying it: The customer typing; the AI agent reading and handing over; the absent human; the founder's month-end bill.

What appears normal: The store bought an AI agent for exactly this kind of ticket and switched it on.

What becomes strange, costly, or unresolved: The agent hands the easiest ticket in the business to a human who is not there, and at month end the store pays the vendor for the handful of tickets the agent did resolve.

Specific operator or business payoff teased before the identity sting: Between the day the store bought the AI and the day it works there is a job; one person could sell it at a fixed price to the stores the vendor will not set up by hand.

Mechanism, proof, and final ruling deliberately withheld: Why the agent hands tickets back; per-resolution pricing; the $3M line; the job sold above it; the vendor's ability to close the gap; BUILD.

Question left open before the identity sting: The strange part is who left the job undone.

Normal system the context runway must explain: How support runs at a one-to-five-agent Shopify brand; what a help desk is; that both big help desks bundle an AI agent and charge per resolution; that small-firm adoption is under one in five.

Why reasonable people use that system: The help desk is the right tool at this size; switching the agent on is the obvious move; paying per resolution is genuinely a good deal.

What changed: Outcome pricing; early, rising adoption.

Who now feels the consequence: The founder paying for a handful of resolutions; the agents; the customer at eleven.

Why it matters now: The vendor's revenue now depends on the thing the store has to configure, and the store is finding out what defaults do.

Central investigation question: If the vendor only gets paid when the AI resolves a ticket, why is the work that makes it resolve anything left with the store, and who does that work for a store too small to get a person?

Evidence and explanation required before the thesis can be stated: Per-resolution pricing on the vendor pages; the go-live guide's setup list; adoption under one in five.

How the ending returns to or reinterprets the cold open: The same ticket at the same hour is answered, and a refund question that arrives twenty minutes later is carried to a person on purpose. Nothing about the store changed except that somebody wrote the handbook.

## Causal spine

Protagonist: The capable professional deciding whether to build this.

Desired result: Sell the job between bought and working, at a fixed price, to stores the vendor will not set up by hand.

Constraint: The platform sells the same job, ships a wizard, is paid per resolution, and can close the gap.

Inciting change or opening: Outcome pricing made non-resolution visible on the bill while leaving the setup with the store.

Central question: Who does the setup for the store too small to get a person?

Proposed mechanism: Audit the queue; safe, conditional, never; write the handbook; set handover on purpose; test; go live; read tickets for two weeks; report on the vendor's meter.

Strongest proof: The vendor's own guide, onboarding page and plan guide; the job sold at two observed layers.

Weakest assumption: A sub-$3M brand pays a four-figure fee.

Necessary tradeoff or decision: Automate too little and the fee is wasted; too much and the store ships wrong refunds; build on a vendor who can delete the job or do not build at all.

Decision or acceptance measure that authorizes the next operating step: The before-and-after report with the never list and the wrong-answer log.

Resolution: BUILD, bounded, with four kill conditions inside thirty days.

First responsible action: Run the wizard on a ten-dollar sandbox and write down the residue.

## Tension

What appears true at first: The store bought AI for this; it should work.

What complicates it: The agent needs a handbook nobody wrote; the vendor writes it only above $3M.

What the evidence changes: The job is real and sold; the vendor is not a villain; the small store is unowned.

What remains uncertain: Whether the store will pay, whether the wizard leaves a job, and whether thirty hours is real.

Why the viewer should keep listening after the problem is established: To find out whether one person can own that gap and what it costs, and to hear the vendor's release note that could end it.

## Counterargument and fairness

Strongest reasonable objection: The vendor has already solved this. It ships a wizard and says most brands go live within 48 hours.

What the objection gets right: The wizard exists; the vendor says that; and nobody in this package has run the wizard against a real store.

Where the proposed model still holds: The vendor's own go-live guide lists knowledge, skills, handover and a week of ticket review as things somebody does; the vendor's own onboarding averages sixty days with a person; going live and working are two different days. Either the wizard does those things or a person does, and finding out costs ten dollars.

What would invalidate the thesis: The wizard, run on a sandbox, leaves nothing for a person to do; or fifteen qualified conversations produce no buyer.

Second objection, ruled honestly: "A store this size won't pay four thousand for settings." The ruling is that the fee is priced against the store's own payback and that the objection is the whole business; the episode does not pretend to have settled it, and the two paid offers are the test.
