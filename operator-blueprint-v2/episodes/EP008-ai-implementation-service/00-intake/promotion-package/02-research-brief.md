## Executive finding

- Opportunity: A generalist sells small DTC brands a fixed-fee implementation of the AI agent already bundled into their helpdesk, measured before and after on the vendor's own verified-resolution meter.
- Evidence class: **observed model** for the service category (the vendor's partner directory and the vendor itself sell implementation and optimization); **every solo-operator economic figure is modeled**.
- Provisional verdict: **plausible with bounded assumptions**, and one of those assumptions is adversarial: the vendor can close the gap.
- Main reason: The buyer, the deliverable and the measure are each defined by the vendor's own published artifacts — its onboarding tiers, its go-live checklist, and its per-resolution billing — while independent research (MIT NANDA; government and bank adoption data) establishes that AI is being bought faster than it is being made to work. The legacy premise's unaudited agency economics are excluded entirely.
- Largest unresolved question: **Whether a brand small enough to be handed self-serve onboarding will pay a solo implementer a four-figure fixed fee** — rather than using the vendor's wizard, buying the vendor's own professional services, or waiting for the product to configure itself.
- Required public framing: Adoption rates, the enterprise "no measurable value" finding, vendor prices and the vendor's own setup requirements are **observed**. The "adopted but not working" problem is **transferred** from enterprise research to small brands and must be said as such. The fee, hours, capacity, buyer payback and contribution are **modeled**. The legacy $40K/month, 85–90% margin and $2–5K project bands are **excluded and may not appear**.

## Buyer reality

- Buyer: Founder, ops lead or head of CX at a Shopify DTC brand with 1–5 support agents and roughly 1,000–5,000 tickets a month — the Gorgias Pro/Advanced ticket bands (CLM-006) — and, per the vendor's own tiering, small enough to receive self-serve onboarding rather than a dedicated implementation manager (CLM-010, CLM-013).
- End customer or beneficiary: The brand's shoppers and its support agents.
- Recurring problem: The AI agent is switched on with little knowledge, few skills and default handover rules. The vendor's documentation is explicit that the agent needs at least one knowledge source, that skills should be built for the highest-volume intents, and that anything it cannot complete is handed to a human (CLM-005). Left unconfigured, it resolves little and the brand keeps paying agents.
- Consequence and frequency: **Quantified at enterprise scale, not at SMB scale.** MIT NANDA found roughly 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots produce no measurable P&L impact and attributes this to a "learning gap" — tools that do not adapt to workflows — not to model quality (CLM-003). A 2026 CX-industry survey (Zendesk-owned Forethought, 600+ practitioners) reports ~70% AI adoption in CX with about 2% seeing value, and names "the investment of time and effort into setting it up" as the first ingredient (CLM-004). No independent source was found quantifying the cost of an unconfigured AI agent for a small brand; cost-per-ticket figures in circulation are vendor blogs and are excluded (CLM-018).
- Existing alternatives or budget: The vendor's self-serve wizard and webinars (CLM-011); the vendor's own onboarding (2–5 sessions with an implementation manager, a premium "50-in-50" program, and professional services including "AI Agent setup" at custom quote — average time to launch 60 days) (CLM-010); certified partner agencies from the vendor directory (CLM-009); support BPOs; or leaving it. Brands already pay $60–$900/month for the helpdesk plus a per-resolution AI fee (CLM-006, CLM-007, CLM-008). **Budget for the tool is observed; budget for a third-party setup at this brand size is the hypothesis.**
- Why now: Both major ecommerce helpdesks moved AI to outcome-based pricing — Zendesk publicly since August 2024 at $1.50 per automated resolution (CLM-008); Gorgias "pay only when it resolves a conversation" (CLM-007). Small-firm adoption is rising but under 20% (CLM-001, CLM-002, CLM-016), so buyers are mid-adoption. Klarna's public reversal — cost-led automation, lower quality, rehiring humans — put the cost-versus-quality tradeoff on the record (CLM-014).

The exact solo offer is new to this package. The underlying problem (AI bought, not configured) and the professional-service category (implementation and optimization sold by partners and by the vendor) are observed.

## Evidence landscape

### Direct evidence

- **The vendor's service-partner directory** lists certified agencies selling "Gorgias platform setup and optimization" and "AI & automation integration," organized by tier (Elite, Premier, Partner, each with a "Services Certified" variant), filterable by budget, platform and region. The partner program promises "prioritized leads whenever we have a customer that needs your services" and a revenue share, and accepts development agencies, marketing agencies, BPOs and affiliates (CLM-009). This establishes that the exact offer is sold today by third parties — but by agencies, with the vendor's referral, at undisclosed prices.
- **The vendor sells the same implementation itself**: standard onboarding of 2–5 sessions with an implementation manager, a premium "50-in-50" program ("automate 50% of your support in 50 days"), and professional services including "AI Agent setup" at custom quote; "average time to launch is 60 days" (CLM-010). A Gorgias plan guide reports that dedicated 1:1 onboarding starts at roughly $3M annual revenue and self-serve applies below it (CLM-013, not opened at source; verify). This is both validation (the vendor charges for this work) and the competition (the vendor could extend it downward).
- **A specialist implementation shop** (custo.tech, Barcelona, "Best European Gorgias Partner 2024") publishes Starter / Growth / Enterprise implementation tiers ("live in 1 week / 2–3 weeks / 4–6 weeks"), monthly optimization retainers, and a retainer commitment of ">60% automation, CSAT >4" — prices revealed on call (CLM-012). Proves the offer shape and the retainer shape; proves no price.
- An Upwork consultant listing "Zendesk, Intercom & Gorgias Implementation Consultant" with claimed partner tiers appeared in search; the profile could not be opened (HTTP 403) and is **not usable** beyond the fact that such listings exist (CLM-017).

### Adjacent business evidence

- Enterprise AI deployment research: 95% no measurable P&L; purchased vendor tools and partnerships succeed "about 67% of the time" versus internal builds "one-third as often" (CLM-003). Supports "install what exists" over "build," and the existence of a configuration gap. Enterprise sample; transfer to a five-person brand is one inference hop and is stated as such.
- CX-industry adoption-versus-value survey (CLM-004): same shape, vendor-owned publisher, corroborates direction not magnitude.
- Klarna (CLM-014): the tradeoff mechanism — cost-led automation degraded quality — at a scale far above the buyer. Used for the story's stakes, not for SMB demand.

### Component feasibility evidence

- The vendor's own go-live documentation (CLM-005) is the delivery skeleton: prerequisites (AI Agent subscription, connected Shopify store, admin permissions); at least one knowledge source; skills for the highest-volume intents ("returns, order status, and cancellations"); optional actions; tone and handover settings; playground testing; review "at least 10 tickets in the first week." Everything the operator sells is a judgment layered on a documented workflow, which is exactly what makes a bounded test possible today: a Starter plan is $10/month (CLM-006).
- Measurement comes with the tool. Zendesk defines an automated resolution by a 72-hour quiet period with validation, and bills $1.50 for each (CLM-008); Gorgias bills per resolved conversation (CLM-007). **The before/after measure is the vendor's own billing meter.** Third-party reports that Gorgias automation rates run 26–56% and that AI handles an average of 31% of ecommerce interactions (CLM-015) are vendor or competitor material and are context only.

### Scaled relevance

- Shopify's FY2025 10-K states "millions of merchants" across 175 countries, 44% in the US (CLM-019). Gorgias claims 15,000–16,000 brands (CLM-015, vendor). The Shopify listing shows 703 reviews at 4.3 stars since 2017 (CLM-006).
- US Census BTOS, May 2026: overall AI use 17–20% from December 2025 to May 2026; under 20% for firms with fewer than 20 employees, flat over the period; retail trade about 14% (CLM-001). JPMorgan Chase Institute, April 2026, from transaction data on 4.6 million small businesses: about 17.7% had adopted AI by end-2025, accelerating after 2023; 33% of non-adopters cite tool-quality concerns (CLM-002). Federal Reserve note, April 2026: adoption about 18% of firms at end-2025, and "adoption among the smallest firms is stronger than would be expected based on size alone" (CLM-020). NFIB, June 2025: 24% of small employers use AI; customer service is a planned use for only 9% (CLM-016).

Accenture's advanced-AI bookings ($2.2B in Q1 FY2026, the last quarter it will disclose them) (CLM-021) receive **no promotion credit**. They were the legacy episode's headline and they prove nothing about a small brand's willingness to pay a solo operator.

## Proposed synthesis

- Parallel A and supported component: Vendor service-partner directory and the vendor's own professional services → the exact offer (implementation and optimization) and a partner-assisted acquisition path.
- Parallel B and supported component: Fixed-scope readiness implementation against a published checklist → delivery shape and fixed-fee pricing.
- Parallel C and supported component: Outcome-priced software → the before/after measure is the vendor's billing meter, so the deliverable is observable without the operator inventing a metric.
- New combination: A solo implementer serving the brand tier the vendor hands to self-serve, pricing against the buyer's payback (verified resolutions × agent minutes), and treating the never-automate list and handover rules as the product rather than the configuration clicks.
- Primary transfer risk: **The vendor.** It sells the same service, ships a wizard, and could auto-configure from store content. Directory agencies are larger and referred. The solo operator's position exists only in the gap between the wizard and the implementation manager, and that gap is the vendor's to close.

## Proposed Operator Blueprint

- Offer: One "AI desk implementation": 30-day ticket audit by intent and outcome; knowledge base and guidance authored from the brand's policies and macros; skills for the top three intents (order status, returns/exchanges, cancellations/edits); handover and never-automate rules; playground test suite; go-live on one channel first; two weeks of monitored tuning with weekly ticket reviews; before/after report on verified resolutions, first-response time and escalation errors.
- Buyer outcome: A configured agent, a written rule set the brand owns, a measured lift (or a documented reason there was none), and a list of what stays human.
- Delivery workflow:
  1. Export or view 30 days of tickets; classify by intent; mark each intent safe / conditional / never.
  2. Confirm the vendor prerequisites (subscription, connected store, permissions) and the current verified-resolution baseline from the vendor's own report.
  3. Draft knowledge and guidance with AI tools from the brand's policies, macros and help center; the operator edits every rule that touches money, shipping promises or cancellations.
  4. Build skills for the top three intents; set handover triggers deliberately, not at defaults; leave actions (refunds, edits) off unless the brand signs off per action.
  5. Run the playground suite: ordinary, missing-order, duplicate, angry, out-of-policy, and "not in knowledge" cases.
  6. Go live on email first; review ten-plus tickets a week for two weeks; tune guidance; log every wrong handover and wrong answer.
  7. Deliver the before/after report and the never-automate list; hand over admin ownership; recommend expand, hold, or turn off.
- Technology and human stack: Jobs first — ticket judgment, policy interpretation, rule authoring, and the difficult conversation about what must never be automated. The vendor's AI agent is the runtime; a general LLM drafts knowledge from source documents; the vendor's playground and resolution report are the test and measurement instruments. No custom build.
- Required skills or credentials: CX or ops literacy, clear writing, SaaS configuration, and the judgment to say no. No credential is required to configure a helpdesk. Vendor partner status is optional and its availability to a solo operator is unverified (CLM-009).
- Go-to-market path: Start with brands the operator can already reach (Shopify communities, DTC founder groups, ecommerce agencies that do not do CX). Offer a paid ticket audit as the diagnostic entry, credited against the implementation. Apply to the vendor partner program as a second path; treat its prioritized leads as a bonus, not the plan. The vendor's own onboarding threshold is the qualification question: if the brand gets a dedicated implementation manager, it is not this operator's buyer.
- Pricing model: Fixed fee, **bounded hypothesis**. The legacy $2–5K bands are excluded; the test fee below is chosen for falsifiability against buyer payback.
- Defensibility: The never-automate list and the handover rules — judgment the vendor's wizard does not supply and the vendor's per-resolution incentive does not reward. Configuration clicks are not a moat; if the offer collapses to clicks, the vendor's wizard wins and the candidate fails.

Capacity case formula: `$4,000 x 2 implementations x 1 month = $8,000 gross revenue`

Contribution case formula: `$8,000 - $100 software - $800 acquisition and overhead = $7,100 contribution before owner compensation and tax`

Fully loaded check after imputed operator labor: `$7,100 - (60 hours x $60) = $3,500`. Stress case at 45 hours per implementation: `$7,100 - (90 hours x $60) = $1,700`.

**Buyer-payback model (defines the minimum buyer; all inputs modeled or transferred):** a brand at 2,000 tickets a month whose verified-resolution rate rises from 10% to 30% after implementation gains about 400 machine-resolved tickets a month. At roughly $0.90–$1.50 per resolution (CLM-007, CLM-008) that adds about $360–$600 a month in vendor fees. At an assumed 8 agent-minutes per ticket and $25 loaded agent-hour — **both unknown for any specific brand** — that is about 53 agent-hours, roughly $1,300 a month; net about $700–$950 a month, so a $4,000 fee pays back in roughly four to six months. At 500 tickets a month the same arithmetic pays back in well over a year, which is why the buyer is bounded at roughly 1,000 tickets and up. The 10%-to-30% lift is a **hypothesis** to be measured in validation, not a result.

## Narrative and audience hypothesis

- Operator protagonist: An ops or CX generalist deciding whether the work between "AI bought" and "AI working" is a business they can sell.
- Starting state: A two-agent brand switches on the AI agent that came with its helpdesk and adds nothing to it; the agent hands most tickets back and answers a refund question wrong.
- Inciting change: Per-resolution pricing makes the founder look at how few resolutions are actually happening while the agents are still buried in "where is my order."
- Stakes: Agent hours and wrong refunds now; a product that could do it all next release.
- Causal mechanism: The agent is only as good as its knowledge, guidance and handover rules; the vendor's own docs say so; nobody at a small brand owns that work, and the vendor's hands-on onboarding starts above this brand's size.
- Build transformation: Audit → safe/conditional/never → rules → test → go live → tune → measure on the vendor's meter.
- Decisions and tradeoffs: Which intents are safe; where the handover fires; whether to enable money-moving actions; cost versus quality (Klarna); whether to build a business on a vendor who can erase it.
- End state and payoff: A measured before/after and a viewer who knows the minimum buyer, the fee arithmetic and the kill condition.
- Visual evidence classes: the queue by intent; the empty knowledge panel; the handover log; a failing then passing playground test; the resolution meter before and after; the never-automate list.
- Working audience promise: The AI agent your helpdesk sold you only gets paid when it resolves a ticket. Here is the work that decides whether it ever does — and the business of doing it for brands the vendor won't onboard by hand.
- Honest packaging territory: "AI bought, not working" at small-brand scale; the vendor's own threshold as the map of the gap. No earnings claim, no "$5.9 billion."

## Risks and constraints

- Factual uncertainty: No observed solo fee, hours or client outcome. SMB-scale consequence of an unconfigured agent is unquantified. The Gorgias per-resolution figure comes from competitor explainers, not the vendor's page (CLM-007). The revenue thresholds for dedicated onboarding were not opened at source (CLM-013).
- Analogy-transfer uncertainty: Enterprise "no value" findings transferred to five-person brands (one hop, stated). Agency partners transferred to a solo operator (partner-program access unverified).
- Legal or regulatory boundary: Customer data access (order history, addresses, payment status) requires scoped, client-controlled admin access and written consent; consumer-protection rules on refunds and automated messaging vary by jurisdiction; no automated action that moves money without per-action sign-off.
- Permissions, privacy, or guest dependency: Any real implementation shown on screen needs the brand's written permission and redaction. No guest is required.
- Platform or vendor dependency: **Central.** The vendor sells the same service, ships a wizard, changes pricing (Zendesk restructured tiers in 2026), and could auto-configure. A product release can shrink or erase the job. The episode must say so.
- Ethical failure mode: Automating refund, cancellation or medical/safety product questions without guardrails; over-claiming automation rates; building operator lock-in by keeping admin ownership.
- Claims that must not be made: the legacy $40K/month, 85–90% margin, $2–5K project or $6K/month freelancer figures; any typical automation rate or "automate 80%"; Accenture's bookings as proof of small-brand demand; that the vendor's wizard is inadequate (unverified); that Manav has delivered a helpdesk implementation to a client; any current vendor price without a refresh.

## Thirty-day validation plan

1. With written permission, review 30 days of tickets at three brands in the 1,000–5,000-ticket band. Record intent mix, whether the AI agent is on, its verified-resolution baseline, its knowledge sources, and its handover settings. Test the vendor's wizard end to end on the operator's own Starter sandbox and record what it does and does not configure.
2. Run one unpaid implementation on one of those brands following the vendor's go-live checklist. Track hours by step, verified resolutions before and after on the vendor's report, first-response time, and every wrong handover or wrong answer over two weeks.
3. Offer two paid implementations at the predeclared fee to brands below the vendor's dedicated-onboarding threshold; apply to the vendor partner program as an individual and record the response.

Success signal: One brand pays the predeclared fee; the unpaid implementation shows a measured lift of at least ten percentage points in verified resolutions with no money-moving error; delivery hours at or under 45; the wizard leaves the guidance, skills and handover judgment to the merchant.

Kill or redesign condition: The wizard or a new release configures knowledge, skills and handover adequately without human judgment; no brand in the band will pay a fixed fee after fifteen qualified conversations; the partner program refuses solo operators **and** no other channel produces a paying buyer; measured lift under ten points; or the brand's safe-to-automate intents are too few to justify the fee.
