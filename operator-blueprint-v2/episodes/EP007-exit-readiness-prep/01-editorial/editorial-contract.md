## Viewer

Primary viewer/operator: An experienced professional — operations, finance-adjacent, consulting, or systems — who can run a structured engagement and hold a hard conversation with a founder.

Audience situation: employed and exploring / already building

What this person already knows: How businesses actually run day to day. They have seen undocumented process and key-person risk from the inside. They do not know M&A, and they are not a broker.

What this person is trying to decide or accomplish: Whether there is a real, legal, sellable practice here that does not require a licence or a finance background.

Starting belief or confusion: That helping owners sell means becoming a broker, or requires deal experience they do not have.

Practical capability they should have after the episode: Scope a readiness engagement, tell preparation apart from brokerage, price fixed-fee work whose payoff lands at an event they are not paid on, and name the condition under which they should not build it.

## Episode job

One-sentence working promise: The people who help owners sell only get paid when the deal closes — here is the business that gets paid before it, and the line that decides whether you can run it.

Episode-specific show string in plain language: A business you can build for owners who are about to become sellers.

Concrete situation suitable for a cold open: An owner who has run a profitable business for twenty-five years, intends to sell "in a couple of years," and has just had a buyer walk.

Specific operator or business payoff the cold open will tease before the silent sting: That there is paid work in the gap between deciding to sell and being sellable, and almost nobody is doing it.

Mechanism, proof, and final thesis the cold open must withhold: Why buyers walk (value only transfers if the business runs without the seller); the listing-to-sale and involuntary-exit evidence; and the licensing constraint that forces the revenue model.

Normal system the viewer needs explained before the central thesis: How a small business is actually sold today — broker engaged late, paid on success, diligence run fast by a competitive buyer pool.

Central argument: **Every professional near a small-business sale is paid only if it closes, so nobody is paid to do the work that makes closing possible. That gap is a business, and the reason it stays open is that the obvious way to charge for it is illegal in a third of the country.**

Narrative question: Can you get paid for readiness before there is a deal?

Evidence and context required before the central argument can be stated: That most listed businesses do not sell; that owners are measurably unprepared; that buyers now run competitive, fast diligence.

Why this matters now: A large ownership-transition wave meets measured owner unpreparedness and rising buyer competition.

What is at stake for the buyer, operator, or market: For the owner, roughly 80% of net worth inside an asset that usually does not sell. For the operator, a practice that must be paid years before the payoff it serves.

The concrete business being taught: A fixed-fee engagement that makes one owner-operated business survivable in diligence.

Short public category title: **Exit readiness**

Short spoken company name: **a sale-readiness practice**

One-sentence plain definition: It makes a small business able to survive a buyer's inspection.

Precise internal operating description: A fixed-fee engagement that documents operations, reduces owner dependence, resolves records and reporting hygiene, analyses customer concentration and key-person risk, and assembles a diligence package — stopping short of representing the owner in a transaction.

Opportunity thesis: what changed, why the problem matters, and what company belongs in the gap: Ownership transition has become a volume event while buyer diligence has become fast and competitive. Owners are unprepared and the professionals around them are compensated only on closing. The company that belongs in the gap sells preparation as its own product, before and independent of a transaction.

Operator thesis: why this viewer has a credible advantage beyond tool access: The work is operational judgement — seeing where a business depends on one person and knowing what a buyer will discount. That comes from having run or rebuilt operations, not from software.

How AI makes this viable or more viable for one accountable operator: The documentation burden — SOP drafting from interviews, financial narrative assembly, data-room organisation — is the largest cost and the part current tooling reduces most reliably. Without that, this is a team.

Human judgment and accountability AI does not replace: Deciding what a buyer will actually discount; telling a founder they are the problem; classifying what is fixable inside the engagement; owning the boundary that keeps the practice outside brokerage.

What "sustainable" means in this episode without promising income: One operator covering their own compensation from a modeled number of engagements per year at a modeled fee, with the assumptions stated and the arithmetic shown. Not a forecast.

Reachable-share thesis: how a small share of a large, costly problem could support the modeled business: The transition population is very large and the required share is very small — a solo practice needs single-digit engagements per year. The episode must show this without pretending a total market becomes reachable demand.

Underlying established budget, spend, or responsibility: Owners already pay brokers, CPAs and attorneys at transaction time. Readiness is the underserved earlier stage of an existing spend.

Enterprise, platform, specialist, and operator-scale parallels: Enterprise M&A due-diligence preparation; SOC 2 and ISO readiness consulting at platform scale; CEPA-credentialed exit planners at specialist scale; the proposed one-operator practice.

Scale-proof pattern: **same job across scales**

What the scale argument proves: That preparing an organisation to survive external scrutiny is real, paid work at several scales, delivered by generalists working from a framework.

What it does not prove about the exact offer: That a small-business owner will buy it, at this fee, before a transaction exists.

Qualified buyer rule stated without an example industry: An owner-operated business with real profit, an owner intending to exit within one to five years, records that exist but are not diligence-grade, and operations that depend materially on the owner.

Unsuitable buyer rule: A business already professionally managed with a non-owner operator; one with no realistic buyer; one where the owner will not give the time the work requires; and any owner seeking a valuation opinion or a buyer introduction rather than preparation.

Three explanatory cases and the distinct buying decision each clarifies:
1. A trades business where every customer relationship is the owner's — clarifies **owner dependence**.
2. A professional-services firm with concentrated revenue in two clients — clarifies **customer concentration**.
3. A business with real profit and no reporting a buyer can verify — clarifies **records hygiene**.

Why none of those cases defines the company or market: Each shows a different failure the same engagement resolves. The company is defined by the failure class, not by the sector any example happens to sit in.

The aspirational business the operator may ultimately build: A transition-readiness practice serving a referral network of advisers, with repeat engagements and possibly a productised diagnostic.

The entry wedge this episode makes concrete: One fixed-fee readiness engagement for one owner.

Entry-wedge thesis: why this is the responsible first paid way into the larger company: It is bounded, deliverable solo, requires no licence in most of the country, and produces an artifact the buyer can judge — while the operator learns the real hour count before scaling anything.

Why the wedge is an entry point rather than the business ceiling: Repeat and referral work, adviser relationships, and a productised diagnostic all sit beyond it. None should be attempted before one engagement has been delivered and measured.

Proof that would justify the next expansion step: One completed engagement with recorded hours, one owner who paid, and one adviser who referred.

The first responsible action the viewer should be able to take: Run the readiness diagnostic once, unpaid, on a real business, and record the hours.

Intended company-level verdict: **BUILD**

Why an affirmative BUILD verdict is evidence-safe: The problem is quantified from two independent directions, the buyer relationship is direct, the pricing environment is anchored in closed-transaction data, and the entry barrier is genuinely low. The verdict is to **test** the business, with a stated kill condition — not a claim that it works.

Value that must be delivered before any audience ask: The complete blueprint — the failure class, the engagement, the boundary, the arithmetic, and the kill condition.

Natural final like-and-subscribe reason tied to that delivered value: The channel takes a business apart to the point where the viewer can see whether it would work for them, including when the answer is no.

Other asks to omit or combine so the ending does not stack CTAs: One ask. The Canvas download and the subscribe ask combine into a single closing move; no newsletter, community, or consultation ask.

## Operator Economy point of view

What common coverage gets wrong: It is written for owners, by people paid on closing, and treats licensing as a footnote rather than as the thing that determines whether the business can exist.

What a seasoned operator would notice: That the incentive structure explains the gap. Nobody is paid to prepare a business, because everyone in the room is paid on the transaction.

What we believe, stated plainly: The readiness work is real and someone should be paid for it, and the honest version has to be fixed-fee — not because that is nicer, but because the alternative is regulated.

What a reasonable person could disagree with: That owners will pay before a deal exists. That is the assumption the whole business rests on, and it is untested.

Broken incentive, fake solution, or conventional framing we are willing to judge: Success-fee alignment being sold as a virtue. "We only get paid when you do" also means nobody gets paid to tell an owner they are not ready.

What would change our mind: Evidence that brokers already do this work free to win listings, or a real owner declining to pay after seeing findings they had not seen.

## Truth and framing contract

Evidence class: **adjacent synthesis**

Observed facts the episode may rely on: The listing-to-sale rate and involuntary-exit share (CLM-001); owner readiness measures (CLM-002); transaction multiples, deal competition and closed-transaction counts (CLM-003); the existence and shape of brokerage licensing (CLM-004).

Modeled or synthesized elements that require one explicit qualification: Every fee, hour, capacity and contribution figure. The combined offer itself.

Evidence boundaries that should not be repeated unless their meaning changes: EPI's institutional interest; IBBA data being advisor-reported rather than audited; CLM-004 being directional rather than primary law.

Claims the episode may not make:
- Any multiple uplift attributed to preparation. The circulating 4.5–5.5× versus 7.5–9.0× figures are seller-published and **excluded by name**.
- That preparation causes a sale.
- Any figure presented as a typical readiness fee.
- Anything constituting legal advice, including naming a state and telling a viewer what they may do there without primary verification.
- Any implication of deal or transaction experience on the channel's part.

Economics language required: `Modeled scenario, not observed performance or an earnings forecast.`

Legal, permission, access, or ethical boundaries: Compliance is the operator's own responsibility. The episode shows that a perimeter exists, shows that it forces the revenue model, and directs the viewer to verify their own state. The ethical failure mode named aloud is drifting across the line for a share of a deal.

## Scope

Must answer:

- Why do buyers walk away from profitable businesses?
- What exactly is in a readiness engagement, and what is not?
- Where is the line between preparation and brokerage, and why does it decide the revenue model?
- What does it cost to deliver, and what would it have to charge?
- How does an operator reach the first buyer?
- What would prove this does not work?

May answer if it strengthens the story:

- Why the professionals already around the owner do not do this.
- What consolidator demand changes about who the buyer is.

Explicitly out of scope:

- How to value a business.
- How to negotiate or structure a transaction.
- Tax and estate planning.
- Any state-specific legal instruction.
- Buying a business rather than preparing one.

Out of the initial offer but retained in the aspirational business:

- Repeat and monitoring engagements.
- A productised diagnostic.
- An adviser referral network.

## Completion test

A skeptical viewer finishes this episode able to:

1. Explain the buyer problem — value does not transfer while the business is the owner.
2. Explain the offer and delivery mechanism — diagnostic, remediation sprint, package assembly, handoff.
3. Identify the strongest evidence (most listed businesses do not sell; half of exits are unplanned) and the weakest assumption (that owners pay before a deal).
4. Understand the economics as a scenario with stated assumptions.
5. Describe the first validation test — run it once unpaid and record the hours.
6. Explain the mature readiness practice before the single engagement that starts it.
7. Explain what would justify expanding, and what must be proven first.
8. Repeat "a sale-readiness practice" and say what it does in one sentence.
9. State what to begin building without mistaking a BUILD verdict for proof.
10. Explain the opportunity without leaning on one example industry.
11. Name the established transaction spend, the parallel scales, and the operator opening.
12. Tell a qualified owner from an unsuitable one.
13. Explain why the required sales activity cannot be derived from an invented conversion rate — the modeled practice needs single-digit engagements a year, and that number comes from capacity, not from a funnel assumption.
