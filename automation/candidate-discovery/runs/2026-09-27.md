# Crash-report access and solicitation rules, 24 states

Question: in which states can a personal-injury firm lawfully get crash reports for people it does
not yet represent and mail them? Researched 2026-09-27 from statutes, agency pages and bar rules;
secondary sources are marked. State law changes; recheck a state before relying on it.

## Result

| Verdict | Count | States |
|---|---|---|
| Open | 6 | Ohio, New Jersey, Indiana, Missouri, Nevada, Massachusetts (low confidence) |
| Restricted | 9 | Texas, Florida, New York, Georgia, Illinois, North Carolina, Michigan, Virginia, Maryland |
| Closed | 9 | California, Pennsylvania, Washington, Arizona, Tennessee, Kentucky, Colorado, Louisiana, South Carolina |

- **Open:** reports obtainable by a firm without a client, no statute bars using them to solicit;
  bar mail rules still apply.
- **Restricted:** usable, but with a hold, a sworn statement, redaction, or agency refusal.
- **Closed:** reports limited to people in the crash and their own lawyers, or solicitation use is
  banned outright.

**Finding that changes the delivery model.** Maryland makes it a crime for a *nonlawyer* to access
reports to solicit, while lawyers may. Several other states release reports only to attorneys or
under a requester's sworn statement. So the law firm has to be the requester of record; the
operator can only process reports the firm lawfully obtains. An operator buying reports itself and
selling leads is the riskiest version of the business.

**Corrections to the lead as first filed.** The 30-day mail wait is not universal: Pennsylvania,
Illinois, Ohio, North Carolina, New Jersey (outside mass disasters), Virginia, Washington, Arizona,
California and Massachusetts have none in their bar rules, and New York removed its wait on
2026-06-01. Michigan's and Texas's waits are criminal statutes, not bar rules. Florida's is 60 days
in practice.
