### Back-of-book indexing for academic and nonfiction authors (lane 3) — rejected

- Old-way spend: authors pay professional indexers about $3–$7 per indexable page (indexers.ca,
  Society of Indexers, freelancer rate pages; rate cards, context only). Indexia's founders put a
  professional index at $1,000–$3,000 and several weeks (seller claim, armedwithabook.com).
- Substitute: AI indexing tools sold straight to authors — Indexia ($99 per standard index, export to
  Word/LaTeX/CINDEX XML), IndexerLabs, IndexStudio, AI-Indexing; the American Society for Indexing
  reviewed them in 2025–2026 (asindexing.org).
- Why rejected: the residual (editing an AI draft index) is either done by the author with a $99 tool
  or by an existing professional indexer; ASI's March 2026 white paper argues AI indexing quality is
  weak, which favours the incumbent indexer, not a new operator. Fails the substitute check.
- Reopening condition: publishers shown refusing AI-only indexes while authors pay a hybrid
  provider a rate between $99 and the professional rate.

### No Surprises Act arbitration (IDR) desk for small out-of-network practices (lanes 2 and 3) — held as `DISC-2026-10-10-022`

- Operating change (primary-adjacent; law-firm and society summaries, Federal Register text not
  read): the Federal IDR Operations final rule, issued 2026-05-28 by HHS, Labor, Treasury and OPM,
  cuts the administrative fee from $115 to $15 per party per dispute for disputes started on or after
  2026-06-11; raises the batch cap from 25 to 50 items; widens batching (same patient and encounter;
  same or comparable code; same CPT range for anesthesia, radiology, pathology, lab); requires
  certified IDR entities to rule on eligibility within five business days; and requires plans to
  flag IDR eligibility with claim adjustment and remittance codes (Sidley 2026-06; Baker Donelson;
  AASM; ACR summary). Certified IDR entity fees unchanged. Most procedural provisions start on a
  later trigger; sources disagree (60 or 90 days after publication, guidance, or portal readiness).
  A phased "IDR Gateway" portal is due in 2026.
- `problem cost` (read): Georgetown CHIR study (Hoadley and Watts, Health Affairs Forefront
  2026-08-26; Medical Economics 2026-08-27): providers won about 85% of disputes; Radiology Partners
  (30%), HaloMD (27%) and TeamHealth (20%) filed over three-quarters of resolved lines in 2025; "Many
  smaller local practices are not using the IDR process", because it needs portal familiarity and
  resources they lack; whether revenue-cycle firms help them is unclear.
- `problem cost` (SERP, not read): a survey reported nearly 60% of IDR awards won by emergency
  physicians unpaid within the statutory window; under the old $115 fee, small underpayments were
  uneconomic to dispute (telehealth.org).
- `old-way spend`: HaloMD 20% of a $2,000 award in one Nutex contract, provider pays fees up front
  (yespress.io, from litigation records; one contract, not a rate card). An unsourced commentary
  says most out-of-network practices use contingency firms at 20%–25% of collections (context only).
- Audience signal (weak, seller-side): paid trainings for practices on the 2026 rule
  (healthcare.trainingleader.com 2026-04; skillacquire course); Medical Society of DC member webinar
  2026-05-21 "What every DC physician needs to know". These are sellers and an association, not
  buyers asking.
- AI-change test: **partial, to verify.** The fee cut, not AI, is what made small disputes
  worth filing. AI's part: screen every remittance for eligibility, draft the open-negotiation
  notice, and draft an offer brief from the claim, the QPA and public IDR determination data in
  minutes, so one operator can handle small batches that a 20%-contingency firm or an in-house biller
  would skip. HaloMD's scale shows the job automates at volume; unproven that AI is what lets a
  solo operator serve the small tail.
- Delivery boundary: **passes on paper.** No licence is needed to file IDR for a provider; HaloMD
  and revenue-cycle firms do it as non-lawyers. Residual: an unlicensed operator screens, notices,
  files and argues small-practice disputes on a contingency or per-dispute fee. Provider signs off.
  HIPAA business-associate agreement required.
- Substitutes: HaloMD (contingency, about 20%); revenue-cycle management companies; payer-side AI
  (Zelis NSA Claim Advantage, Daffodil Health, 2026) shows tooling exists but sells to plans. No
  provider-side AI filing tool sold to small practices found in two searches; not proof of absence.
- Risks: payers have sued filers for "gaming" IDR (Elevance v. HaloMD dismissed; appeal sought);
  a House member sent letters to IDR entities in 2026-09 asking about AI use; employer groups lobby
  to curb awards. Scope is limited to facility-based and emergency out-of-network care, so most
  primary care is excluded.
- Missing condition (why held): no independent buyer-side question signal. Reddit blocked, no
  Quora titles, no practice-manager forum thread found; `IDR dispute` is near zero in Trends. Also
  unconfirmed that contingency firms turn small practices away.
- Strongest invalidating question: do HaloMD and revenue-cycle firms already take small-volume
  practices at 20%, leaving nothing an AI-equipped solo operator does better?
