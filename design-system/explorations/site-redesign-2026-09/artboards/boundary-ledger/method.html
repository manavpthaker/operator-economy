01 / The pipeline and its gates

Five stages, each behind a gate.

An episode starts as a candidate and ends as a published page, a Canvas, and a PDF. Nothing moves to the next stage on momentum; it moves because a named review let it through.

From candidate to published Canvas

  1. Intake. Is the opportunity worth examining? A candidate is researched, scored, and either promoted or returned with the reason recorded.
  2. Editorial. The Canvas is authored and locked: the operating model, the evidence and its classes, the economics, and the exact words of the script.
  3. Narration. The locked words are performed and the narration is locked. The words don't change at this stage; only the delivery does.
  4. Production. The episode is designed, built, and finished from the locked narration, so the picture follows the argument instead of the other way round.
  5. Publishing. The episode page, the Canvas, and its PDF go out together, each carrying its own hash.
Gates, in plain terms

Between stages, named reviews check the evidence, the language, and the model, and the owner signs off. A Canvas that fails a gate goes back, not through. A locked Canvas can still change: a material edit creates a new revision, and the earlier one stays published and labeled.

  • Evidence. Every observed claim resolves to a receipt, every modeled figure lists its assumptions, and every unknown is registered with how it resolves.
  • Language. Plain sentences, no hype lexicon, and no adjective propping up a modeled figure.
  • Model. The operating model has to be complete before a story is allowed to carry it. A good narrative can't pull an incomplete model through.
  • Owner sign-off. The owner reads the result and signs the lock, or sends it back.

02 / The four evidence classes

Every material claim carries one.

Proof and uncertainty share the same surface. The class tells you what kind of thing a statement is; the receipt tells you where it came from.

Observed

A sourced fact. Someone measured or reported it, and the receipt names them. A vendor-published figure is still observed; the receipt says who published it so you can weigh it.

Observed
Parallel

Transferred from an adjacent model. Nobody has run this exact business, but a neighboring one runs on the same mechanics. The statement says what transfers and what doesn't. A parallel never becomes proof of demand for the exact offer; it earns the right to test, nothing more.

Parallel
Modeled

An assumption with its arithmetic shown. Every modeled figure travels with its assumption list, and scenarios sit side by side, never blended into one confident number. Modeled figures aren't dressed up with reassuring adjectives. If a modeled case fails its own target, the page says so.

Modeled
Unknown

An open question, kept visible. Unknowns are content, not gaps. Each one is registered with how it resolves, and no diagram or polished figure is allowed to paper over one. The most useful line on a Canvas is often the unknown its first test is designed to answer.

Unknown

03 / Model status

The whole model gets its own classification.

Evidence classes label individual statements. Model status classifies the entire Canvas, and it appears once, on the titleblock beside the lock date.

Observed model

The same business, or substantially the same one, has direct operating evidence. Someone runs it today, and the Canvas can point at them.

Runs today
Adjacent synthesis

The proposed business combines neighboring pieces that each run somewhere. The combination itself hasn't been observed, so the Canvas states every transfer and what each one leaves unproven.

Assembled from neighbors
Frontier hypothesis

The opportunity is more novel and carries additional testable assumptions. The Canvas presents a business to test, not one observed working, and it says which assumption you'd have to hear first.

Combination untested
Two labels, never merged A Canvas can be an adjacent synthesis built mostly from observed facts. The status tells you how much of the business has been seen working; the classes tell you what each sentence rests on. Read both before you decide.

04 / Economics rules

Equations, scenarios, and honest failures.

The economics sheet answers one question: where could value move, and under what stated inputs? It doesn't answer what you'll earn.

  • Equations, shown. Economics render as equation lines plus a low, base, and high case. There's no single headline number anywhere on a Canvas. A line reads like monthly revenue = active clients × monthly fee, with each input listed underneath it.
  • Each case carries its own class and assumptions. Low, base, and high aren't three guesses at one number. They're three sets of stated inputs, and each set is labeled Modeled with its assumption list attached.
  • Never blended. The cases sit side by side on a wide screen and one at a time on a phone. No control averages them, and no summary quotes one as if it were the answer.
  • A failing base case is stated plainly. If the base case misses the livelihood requirement, the Canvas says so. Nobody adjusts an input until it passes.
  • The required disclosure. Every Canvas carries one, in the same words, printed beside the figures rather than in a footnote.
Required disclosure Modeled scenarios aren't observed performance or an earnings forecast. Where a Canvas models the operator's own economics, that sentence sits next to the numbers.

05 / Versioning and hashes

A changed Canvas field creates a new revision.

You should be able to check that what you read, what you downloaded, and what was locked are the same system. Three fingerprints make that possible.

The lock

When the owner signs the editorial lock, the Canvas source file gets a SHA-256 fingerprint: a fixed-length code computed from the file's exact contents. Change one character and the code changes. That fingerprint is the authority; the page you read and the PDF you download are derived from it.

Three hashes, all displayed

The source, the published page data, and the PDF each carry their own hash, printed in full in the Canvas's provenance panel. Compare them against what you have. If they match, you're reading the locked system and not a quiet edit of it.

What a revision is

A material edit to a locked Canvas creates a new revision with a new letter and a new set of hashes. The earlier revision stays published, labeled as superseded, and unchanged, so a citation of Rev A keeps pointing at Rev A. The library points at the current revision, and a superseded page says a newer one exists.

  • Canvas source hash. The locked markdown. Everything else derives from it.
  • Projection hash. The published data file the page renders from. Its hash is computed after it's written and stored in the index, so no file ever carries its own fingerprint.
  • PDF hash. The print edition's binary, shown on the download card with the edition date.

06 / What we don't publish

The claims discipline, in reader terms.

These hold on every page, every Canvas, and every episode. They're the reason the library is worth coming back to.

Standing rules

  • No income promises. A livelihood case is a design test the Canvas has to pass or admit failing, not a forecast.
  • No figure without a source or its arithmetic. Estimates are marked and never blended into sourced figures.
  • No modeled number upgraded by an adjective. The inputs make the case or they don't.
  • No diagram that resolves an unknown. A drawing inherits the evidence class of what it draws.
  • No held-back material. Everything load-bearing is on the page; the PDF adds detail and print utility.
  • No schedule promises. An episode publishes when its Canvas has passed its gates, not on a calendar.
